Structure–Function Relationships in Proteins

The four levels of protein structure, what holds each one together, how proteins fold, unfold and get destroyed, and the diseases of misfolding: Alzheimer disease and Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease.

Labobo topic: Biochem Wk 4: Protein Structure & Function

In 60 seconds

  • Primary = sequence joined by peptide bonds; it decides the final shape.
  • Secondary = α-helix and β-sheet, held by backbone hydrogen bonds. The α-helix is right-handed with 3.6 residues per turn; proline breaks helices.
  • Tertiary = whole 3-D shape, from R-group interactions (including disulfide bonds). Quaternary = how subunits fit together (Hb = α₂β₂).
  • Denaturation unfolds secondary and tertiary structure without breaking peptide bonds.
  • Hsp70 works cotranslationally; Hsp60 post-translationally (uses much ATP). Bad proteins get ubiquitin (E1, E2, E3) and go to the proteasome.
  • Alzheimer: extracellular Aβ42 plaques (β-secretase + γ-secretase) and intracellular tau tangles.
  • CJD: infectious prion PrPSc; rapid dementia, myoclonus, death within 1 year.

Learning objectives

Marks’ Basic Medical Biochemistry, 6th ed., Chapter 7, pages 179–190 and 208–216. By the end you can:

  • describe primary, secondary (α-helix, β-sheet, non-repetitive), tertiary and quaternary structure, and structural motifs;
  • explain denaturation and renaturation;
  • explain protein folding, chaperones, the ubiquitin–proteasome system and prions;
  • describe Alzheimer disease and Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease at the molecular level.

The four levels

Proteins are macromolecules made of amino acid monomers. The linear sequence of amino acids contains all the information needed to form one unique 3-D shape, and that shape determines function.

LevelWhat it isHeld together by
PrimaryLinear amino acid sequencePeptide bonds (covalent)
SecondaryLocal folding: α-helix, β-sheet, turns and loopsHydrogen bonds between backbone atoms (C=O and N–H)
TertiaryThe whole 3-D shape of one chainInteractions between R groups: hydrophobic, ionic, hydrogen bonds, disulfide bonds
QuaternaryHow several subunits fit togetherCovalent and noncovalent interactions between subunit surfaces

Primary structure

  • The linear sequence of amino acid residues joined by peptide bonds into a polypeptide chain. This sequence determines the shape of the protein.
  • A peptide bond is an amide linkage between the α-carboxyl group of one amino acid and the α-amino group of the next.
  • It forms by a dehydration (condensation) reaction: one molecule of water is removed.

Secondary structure

Locally folded regions formed by interactions between atoms of the polypeptide backbone (not the side chains). The two regular forms are the α-helix and the β-sheet.

The α-helix

  • A rigid, right-handed spiral with a tightly coiled backbone core.
  • Side chains point outward from the central axis so they don’t get in each other’s way.
  • 3.6 residues per turn, so side chains three or four residues apart come close together in space.
  • Stabilised by extensive hydrogen bonds between carbonyl oxygens and amide hydrogens of the backbone, within the same strand (each C=O bonds to the N–H four residues further on).
  • Proline is a “helix breaker”: its ring doesn’t fit inside the helix (and its nitrogen has no H to donate). It is usually not in the middle of a helix, but can be at the start or in the second position.

The β-sheet (β pleated sheet)

  • Two or more segments of a chain line up side by side, forming a sheet.
  • The carbonyl O of one peptide bond is hydrogen-bonded to the amide H of a peptide bond on an adjacent strand.
  • Strands can run in the same direction (parallel) or opposite directions (antiparallel).

Non-repetitive structures and motifs

Not every region is a helix or sheet. Turns and loops connect them (β-turns often contain glycine and proline). Common combinations of secondary structures are called structural motifs, for example the helix–turn–helix, the zinc finger, and β-hairpins.

Tertiary structure

  • The overall 3-D shape of a protein: the sum of its secondary structural elements.
  • Mainly due to interactions between the R groups.
  • It is flexible and dynamic: side chains and domains keep moving rapidly.
  • A disulfide bond (–S–S–) is a covalent link made from the –SH groups of two cysteine residues, producing a cystine residue.

Denaturation

  • Denaturation = unfolding and disorganisation of a protein’s secondary and tertiary structure without hydrolysis of peptide bonds. The primary structure stays.
  • Denaturing agents: heat, urea, organic solvents, strong acids or bases, detergents, and heavy metal ions such as lead.
  • Under some conditions a denatured protein can refold into its native shape and regain function (renaturation), which shows that the sequence carries the folding information.

Chaperones and misfolding

Chaperones are proteins that help other proteins fold and prevent misfolding. They give a favourable environment for folding and stop unfolded proteins from aggregating. (The word once meant an older woman accompanying a young unmarried lady in public.) Many are heat-shock proteins (Hsp).

FamilyWhenWhat it does
Hsp70Cotranslational (while the chain is being made)Stops new chains folding too early; can also help unfold proteins when needed
Hsp60Post-translational (after synthesis)Folds already-made proteins; uses a lot of ATP

Misfolding

Folding is complex and sometimes goes wrong. Misfolded proteins are usually tagged and degraded, but this quality control is not perfect. Aggregates of misfolded proteins can build up inside or outside cells, and such deposits cause several diseases.

Possible fates of a new protein (slide diagram): correct folding (with or without chaperones) → function; or misfolding → refolding by chaperones, degradation by the proteasome, or aggregation (amyloid).

Ubiquitin and the proteasome

  • The proteasome is an abundant, ATP-dependent protease that destroys incompletely folded proteins.
  • It acts processively: it cuts the whole protein into short peptides.
  • Proteins to be destroyed are marked with polyubiquitin chains, added in a multistep process by three enzyme classes:
  1. E1, ubiquitin-activating enzyme: uses ATP to activate ubiquitin.
  2. E2, ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme: carries the activated ubiquitin towards the substrate.
  3. E3, ubiquitin ligase: recognises the specific substrate protein and attaches ubiquitin to it.
Which E decides the target?

E3 (the ligase) gives the specificity: it chooses which protein is tagged.

Alzheimer disease

  • A progressive neurodegenerative disease and the leading cause of dementia.
  • Risk factors: age > 65 years and genes. People with Down syndrome are at higher risk because the gene for amyloid precursor protein (APP) is on chromosome 21.
  • Main microscopic feature: extraneuronal β-amyloid (Aβ) plaques.
  • Usually begins with short-term memory loss, slowly progressing to dementia with other cognitive deficits; often with depression, anxiety and apathy.

How amyloid plaques form

PathwayFirst cutThen γ-secretase givesResult
Non-amyloidogenic (normal, predominant)α-secretase → sAPPα + C83p3 + AICDNon-toxic fragments
Amyloidogenic (increases in disease)β-secretase → sAPPβ + C99Aβ42 + AICDAβ aggregates into insoluble extracellular plaques

AICD = APP intracellular domain. Aβ42 is hydrophobic and aggregates; the plaques disrupt synaptic signalling, trigger inflammation and promote neuron death.

Tau and neurofibrillary tangles

  • Normally tau stabilises microtubules in neurons, which carry nutrients and signals.
  • In Alzheimer disease tau is hyperphosphorylated, detaches from microtubules and aggregates into neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) inside neurons → cytoskeleton collapses → neuron dies.

Cholinergic hypothesis

There is a marked loss of cholinergic neurons in the basal forebrain (less acetylcholine).

Memory hook

Plaques outside, tangles inside. Aβ plaques are extracellular; tau tangles are intracellular.

Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (clinical correlation)

  • A progressive, fatal, dementing illness caused by an infectious protein, a prion, called PrPSc.
  • PrPSc is highly resistant to proteolysis and forms insoluble aggregates of fibrils.
  • The normal, non-infectious form PrPC (C = cellular) is encoded by the same gene and sits on the surface of neurons and glial cells. The two differ in folding, not sequence: PrPSc has more β-sheet and converts PrPC into more PrPSc.

Early (prodromal) symptomsNonspecific: fatigue, sleep disturbance, weight loss, headache, anxiety, vertigo
Main signsRapidly progressive dementia (weeks to months), ataxia, startle myoclonus (jerks triggered by minimal stimuli)
OutcomeDeath usually in less than 1 year
Definite diagnosisNeuropathologically confirmed spongiform encephalopathy in progressive dementia

Watch out

Watch out: the α-helix hydrogen bonds

Slide 7 says side chains three or four residues apart are brought together “and so” the helix is stabilised by hydrogen bonds. The stabilising hydrogen bonds are between backbone C=O and N–H groups (each C=O to the N–H four residues along), not between side chains. Side chains point outward.

Exam traps

Exam trap
  • Denaturation does not break peptide bonds; primary structure survives.
  • The α-helix is right-handed.
  • Proline is a helix breaker, but can appear at the start of a helix.
  • In β-sheets H bonds are between strands; in α-helices within the strand.
  • E3, not E1, chooses the protein to be destroyed.
  • Down syndrome raises Alzheimer risk because of the APP gene on chromosome 21.
  • PrPC and PrPSc come from the same gene.

    2. 3.6
    3. 4.5
    4. 10

    3. It forms disulfide bonds
    4. It is too large

    3. Interactions between R groups

    2. An oligomer
    3. A motif
    4. A domain

    2. It destroys secondary and tertiary structure without breaking peptide bonds
    3. It can never be reversed
    4. It is caused only by heat
    Show answer

    B. Urea, solvents, acids, bases, detergents and lead also denature; some proteins refold.

    1. Hsp70
    2. Hsp60
    3. Ubiquitin
    4. E3

  8. Which enzyme recognises the specific protein to be ubiquitinated?

    1. E1
    2. E2
    3. E3
    4. The proteasome
    Show answer

    C. E3, the ubiquitin ligase.

  9. Aβ42 is produced when APP is cut by:

    1. α-secretase then γ-secretase
    2. β-secretase then γ-secretase
    3. γ-secretase only
    4. The proteasome

    B. β-secretase gives C99, and γ-secretase cuts C99 into Aβ42 and AICD.

  10. Neurofibrillary tangles are made of:

    1. Extracellular amyloid-β
    2. Hyperphosphorylated tau inside neurons
    3. Prion protein
    4. Ubiquitin
    Show answer

  11. Which feature best fits Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease?

    1. Slow memory loss over 10 years
    2. Rapidly progressive dementia with startle myoclonus
    3. Curable with antibiotics
    4. Caused by a virus
    Show answer

    B. It is caused by a prion (PrPSc) and is fatal, usually within a year.

Key terms

Peptide bond
Amide bond joining amino acids; formed by dehydration.
α-helix
Right-handed coil, 3.6 residues per turn, backbone H bonds within the strand.
β-sheet
Strands side by side, H-bonded between strands.
Structural motif
A common combination of secondary structures.
Disulfide bond
Covalent –S–S– link between two cysteines (a cystine residue).
Protomer
Unit of non-identical subunits in a larger protein.
Oligomer
Protein of identical subunits.
Denaturation
Loss of secondary and tertiary structure without breaking peptide bonds.
Chaperone
Protein that helps others fold and prevents aggregation.
Ubiquitin
Small protein tag; chains of it mark proteins for the proteasome.
Proteasome
ATP-dependent protease that cuts tagged proteins into short peptides.
APP
Amyloid precursor protein; gene on chromosome 21.
Aβ42
Hydrophobic peptide that forms amyloid plaques.
Tau
Microtubule-stabilising protein; forms tangles when hyperphosphorylated.
Prion
Infectious misfolded protein (PrPSc).
Spongiform encephalopathy
Sponge-like brain damage confirming CJD.
