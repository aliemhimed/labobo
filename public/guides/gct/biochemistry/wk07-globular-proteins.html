Globular Proteins: Hemoglobin and Myoglobin

How heme proteins bind oxygen, why hemoglobin’s curve is S-shaped and myoglobin’s is not, what shifts the curve, and the hemoglobin diseases: thalassemias, sickle cell, HbC, methemoglobinemia and CO poisoning.

Labobo topic: Biochem Wk 7: Globular Proteins (Hb and Mb)

In 60 seconds

  • Heme = protoporphyrin IX + Fe²⁺. Fe²⁺ has 6 bonds: 4 to porphyrin N, 1 to the proximal histidine, 1 for O₂.
  • Myoglobin: one chain, hyperbolic curve, P50 ≈ 1 mm Hg. Hemoglobin: α₂β₂, sigmoidal (cooperative) curve, P50 ≈ 26 mm Hg.
  • T form = deoxy, low affinity; R form = oxy, high affinity. The last O₂ binds ~300× more tightly than the first.
  • Right shift (release O₂): ↑ H⁺ (Bohr effect), ↑ CO₂, ↑ 2,3-BPG. HbF binds 2,3-BPG poorly → higher affinity.
  • CO shifts the curve left; methemoglobin (Fe³⁺) can’t carry O₂ — treat with methylene blue.
  • α genes on chromosome 16 (4 copies in total), β gene on chromosome 11. 3 α genes lost = HbH (β₄); 4 lost = Hb Bart (γ₄), hydrops fetalis.
  • HbS: Glu6→Val in β; HbC: Glu6→Lys. Hydroxyurea raises HbF. HbA1c tracks average glucose over the RBC’s 120 days.

Globular heme proteins

A globular protein is water-soluble and folds into a sphere-like shape. Hemeproteins are globular proteins with heme as a tightly bound prosthetic group. The protein’s 3-D structure decides what the heme does.

HemeproteinWhat the heme does
CytochromesIn the mitochondrial electron transport chain, the iron switches rapidly and reversibly between Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺, passing electrons along
CatalaseHeme is part of the active site; breaks down hydrogen peroxide
HemoglobinProtein structure sets how Fe²⁺ sits in the heme plane, controlling O₂ binding between lungs and tissues

Hemoglobin and the heme group

  • Found only in red blood cells. Main job: carry O₂ from the lungs to tissue capillaries. It also carries H⁺ and CO₂ from tissues to the lungs.
  • Human HbA is a tetramer: two α-globin + two β-globin chains.
  • Hydrophobic residues are buried inside: they stabilise folding and form a pocket for the hydrophobic heme.

The heme group

  • Heme = protoporphyrin IX + ferrous iron (Fe²⁺).
  • Colour: purple when deoxygenated (venous blood), red when oxygenated (arterial blood).
  • The top of the heme is hydrophilic (charged propionate side chains); the bottom is hydrophobic.
The six bonds of heme iron
BondPartner
1–4The four nitrogens of the porphyrin ring (holds Fe in the centre)
5Proximal histidine of the globin (its imidazole ring); in myoglobin this is His93 (F8)
6Free to bind O₂ reversibly, on the other side of the ring

In the clinic: HbA1c

HbA is slowly and non-enzymatically glycated. In diabetes, red cells meet higher glucose during their 120-day life, so HbA1c rises. HbA1c is used to monitor average blood glucose over the past months.

Myoglobin

  • Found in heart and skeletal muscle; stores O₂ for the muscle.
  • A classic highly α-helical protein: over 70% of it is in eight α-helices, labelled A to H.
  • The helices fold into a compact sphere with a hydrophobic pocket where heme binds non-covalently.
  • The heme crevice is lined with nonpolar amino acids, except two histidines (basic):
    • Proximal histidine (His93, F8): bonds to Fe (5th bond), anchoring the heme.
    • Distal histidine (His64, E7): near, but not bonded to, the O₂-binding site.

Oxygen-binding curves

Both proteins bind O₂ reversibly through ferrous iron (Fe²⁺). If the iron is oxidised to Fe³⁺ (the met form), the protein can no longer carry O₂.

MyoglobinHemoglobin
Chains / O₂ bound1 chain, binds 1 O₂4 chains, binds up to 4 O₂
Curve shapeHyperbolic (simple equilibrium Mb + O₂ ⇌ MbO₂)Sigmoidal (S-shaped) = cooperative binding
P50≈ 1 mm Hg≈ 26 mm Hg
AffinityVery highLower, and changes with conditions
  • P50 = the pO₂ at which half the binding sites are filled. Higher affinity → lower P50.
  • Cooperativity: O₂ binding to one subunit raises the affinity of the others. The first O₂ binds with difficulty; later ones bind easily (steep part of the curve near 20–30 mm Hg).
  • Hb’s affinity for the last O₂ is about 300 times its affinity for the first.
  • Pulse oximetry estimates arterial O₂ saturation (SpO₂) non-invasively by comparing light absorbed by oxyhemoglobin and deoxyhemoglobin.

Allosteric effectors and the Bohr effect

Hb’s O₂ binding is changed by pO₂, pH, pCO₂ and 2,3-BPG. They are allosteric (“other site”) effectors: binding at one site changes the shape and O₂ binding at other sites.

pO₂

  • Lungs (high pO₂): Hb almost fully saturated, R state, holds O₂.
  • Tissues (low pO₂): Hb shifts to the T state and releases much of its O₂.

The Bohr effect

  • Lower pH (more H⁺) or higher pCO₂ → Hb releases O₂ more easily → lower affinity → curve shifts right. Both stabilise the T form.
  • Why: the T form has a higher affinity for H⁺. Certain histidine side chains have a higher pKa in deoxy-Hb, so when H⁺ rises they become protonated (positive) and form ionic bonds that stabilise deoxy-Hb.
  • Histidine is basic: in acidosis it accepts H⁺; at normal pH it is mostly neutral. This also makes Hb an important blood buffer.
What shifts the Hb curve
Right shift (↓ affinity, more O₂ released)Left shift (↑ affinity, O₂ held)
↑ H⁺ (↓ pH)↓ H⁺ (↑ pH)
↑ pCO₂↓ pCO₂
↑ 2,3-BPG↓ 2,3-BPG (e.g. stored blood)
↑ temperatureHbF, carbon monoxide, methemoglobin
Mnemonic: “CADET, face Right”

CO₂, Acid, DPG (2,3-BPG), Exercise, Temperature: all shift the curve to the right.

2,3-Bisphosphoglycerate (2,3-BPG)

  • Made in red cells from an intermediate of glycolysis.
  • Binds deoxyhemoglobin but not oxyhemoglobin, stabilising the T form → lower O₂ affinity → curve shifts right. This lets Hb release O₂ well at tissue pO₂.
  • 2,3-BPG rises in chronic hypoxia, COPD, emphysema and at high altitude.

Fetal hemoglobin

HbF has γ chains instead of β. The γ chains bind 2,3-BPG poorly because a histidine is replaced by serine. So HbF has a higher O₂ affinity than HbA and pulls O₂ from the mother’s blood across the placenta.

In the clinic: stored blood

2,3-BPG falls during storage, so transfused Hb holds O₂ too tightly and delivers less to tissues. Red cells restore 2,3-BPG in 6–24 hours, which can be dangerous in critically ill patients. A rejuvenation solution regenerates 2,3-BPG and ATP, improving O₂ delivery right after transfusion.

Carbon monoxide (CO)

  • CO binds heme iron very tightly but reversibly, forming carboxyhemoglobin.
  • CO on one or more hemes shifts Hb to the R form, so the remaining hemes hold O₂ more tightly.
  • The curve shifts left: Hb cannot release O₂ to tissues → hypoxia.

β-Thalassemia

  • β-chain synthesis is reduced or absent, usually from point mutations that affect production of functional mRNA.
  • Extra α chains cannot form stable tetramers; they precipitate and kill red cell precursors early (ineffective erythropoiesis).
  • HbA2 (α₂δ₂) and HbF (α₂γ₂) increase.
  • β⁺ = reduced β synthesis; β⁰ = none.
  • β-thalassemia major babies look healthy at birth (still have HbF) but become severely anemic in the first or second year.
  • β⁰/β⁰ (Cooley’s anemia): severe anemia → erythroid hyperplasia and extramedullary hematopoiesis (blood made outside the marrow, e.g. liver, spleen).

Methemoglobinemia

  • Heme iron is oxidised from Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺; Fe³⁺ cannot bind O₂ properly.
  • Causes: drugs such as nitrates, benzocaine and some other local anaesthetics; or congenital NADH-cytochrome b5 reductase deficiency.
  • “Chocolate cyanosis”: blue skin and mucous membranes with brown blood.
  • Symptoms depend on hypoxia: anxiety, headache, dyspnea; rarely coma and death.
  • Treatment: methylene blue, which is oxidised as it reduces Fe³⁺ back to Fe²⁺.

Sickle cell disease, HbC and HbSC

Sickle cell disease (HbS)

  • Autosomal recessive. One nucleotide change in the β-globin gene: glutamate at position 6 → valine.
  • Symptoms start only when enough HbF has been replaced by HbS (infants are protected by HbF, which does not sickle).
  • Red cell lifespan < 20 days (normal 120).
  • HbS gives some protection against Plasmodium falciparum malaria.
  1. Deoxygenation (hypoxemia, dehydration, acidosis) makes HbS polymerise; more 2,3-BPG makes it worse.
  2. Rigid fibres bend the red cell into a sickle.
  3. At first sickling reverses with reoxygenation, but repeated sickling damages the membrane.
  4. Stiff cells are removed by splenic macrophages: extravascular hemolysis.

Clinical features: lifelong painful crises; chronic hemolytic anemia with hyperbilirubinemia; more infections from infancy; acute chest syndrome, stroke, splenic and kidney damage; bone changes from marrow hyperplasia, seen on skull X-ray as a “crew-cut” pattern of radiating striations.

Treatment: hydroxyurea raises HbF, which dilutes HbS and reduces symptoms.

HbC and HbSC

  • HbC: also a change at position 6 of β-globin, but glutamate → lysine.
  • Homozygous HbC: mild chronic hemolytic anemia; no infarctive crises; no specific therapy.
  • HbSC: one HbS gene and one HbC gene (compound heterozygote). Hb levels higher than in sickle cell anemia (may be low-normal); fewer and milder crises, though variable.

Electrophoresis

  • Separates proteins (or DNA) by charge and size. Positive molecules move to the negative pole; negative molecules to the positive pole.
  • Replacing negative glutamate changes charge: valine (neutral) in HbS makes it less negative; lysine (positive) in HbC makes it even less negative.

Watch out

Watch out: how many α genes?

Slide 20 says the α cluster “contains two genes”. That is per chromosome 16. Everyone has two chromosome 16s, so four α genes in total, which is why α-thalassemia is counted 1 to 4 genes lost.

Watch out: HbC charge

Slide 27 says HbC “has a positive charge” and may move the opposite way. In the standard alkaline test all three are negative; HbC is just the least negative, so it moves slowest. The order A > S > C is what is examined.

Exam traps

Exam trap
  • Higher affinity means lower P50.
  • 2,3-BPG binds deoxy-Hb, not oxy-Hb.
  • CO shifts the curve left, not right.
  • HbF has higher O₂ affinity because it binds 2,3-BPG poorly.
  • Sickle hemolysis is mainly extravascular (spleen).
  • HbC homozygotes do not get infarctive crises.
  • Methylene blue treats methemoglobinemia, not CO poisoning.

Check yourself

  1. The fifth coordination bond of heme iron in hemoglobin is to:

    1. Oxygen
    2. The proximal histidine
    3. The distal histidine
    4. A propionate group
    Show answer

    B. The sixth position binds O₂; the distal histidine lies nearby without bonding Fe.

  2. Why is the hemoglobin O₂ curve sigmoidal?

    1. Hb binds only one O₂
    2. Subunits bind O₂ cooperatively
    3. Hb contains Fe³⁺
    4. Hb is found in muscle
    Show answer

    B. Binding at one subunit raises the affinity of the others.

  3. Which has the lowest P50?

    1. HbA
    2. HbA with high 2,3-BPG
    3. Myoglobin
    4. HbA at low pH
    Show answer

    C. Myoglobin’s P50 is about 1 mm Hg (highest affinity).

  4. Which shifts the Hb curve to the right?

    1. Carbon monoxide
    2. Low 2,3-BPG
    3. Increased H⁺
    4. HbF
    Show answer

    C. The Bohr effect: H⁺ and CO₂ stabilise the T form.

  5. The Bohr effect depends mainly on which residues?

    1. Glycine
    2. Histidine
    3. Proline
    4. Cysteine
    Show answer

    B. Histidines with higher pKa in deoxy-Hb become protonated and form ionic bonds.

  6. HbF has higher O₂ affinity than HbA because:

    1. It has more heme groups
    2. Its γ chains bind 2,3-BPG poorly
    3. It contains Fe³⁺
    4. It has no α chains
    Show answer

    B. A histidine is replaced by serine in γ chains.

  7. Carbon monoxide poisoning causes tissue hypoxia because:

    1. It oxidises Fe²⁺
    2. It shifts the curve left so Hb won’t release O₂
    3. It destroys red cells
    4. It blocks 2,3-BPG synthesis
    Show answer

    B. CO pushes Hb into the R form.

  8. A newborn with hydrops fetalis and only γ₄ hemoglobin has:

    1. β-thalassemia major
    2. HbH disease
    3. Hb Bart disease (all four α genes lost)
    4. Sickle cell disease
    Show answer

    C. Without α chains even HbF cannot form.

  9. Which hemoglobins rise in β-thalassemia?

    1. HbA and HbS
    2. HbA2 and HbF
    3. HbH and Hb Bart
    4. HbA1c
    Show answer

    B. α₂δ₂ and α₂γ₂ increase because β is lacking.

  10. Brown blood with cyanosis after benzocaine is best treated with:

    1. Oxygen alone
    2. Methylene blue
    3. Hydroxyurea
    4. Vitamin C only
    Show answer

    B. Methemoglobinemia: methylene blue reduces Fe³⁺ to Fe²⁺.

  11. The sickle cell mutation is:

    1. Glu6→Lys in β-globin
    2. Glu6→Val in β-globin
    3. Val6→Glu in α-globin
    4. Deletion of α genes

    B. Glu6→Lys is HbC.

  12. Hydroxyurea helps in sickle cell disease because it:

    1. Raises HbF
    2. Lowers 2,3-BPG
    3. Removes the spleen
    4. Converts HbS to HbA
    Show answer

    A. HbF does not sickle and dilutes HbS.

  13. On electrophoresis towards the positive pole, the order from fastest is:

    1. C, S, A
    2. S, A, C

Key terms

Globular protein
Water-soluble, roughly spherical protein.
Prosthetic group
Non-protein group tightly bound to a protein, like heme.
Heme
Protoporphyrin IX + Fe²⁺.
P50
pO₂ at which half the O₂ sites are filled.
Cooperativity
Binding at one subunit raises affinity at the others.
T / R form
Low-affinity deoxy / high-affinity oxy conformation of Hb.
Allosteric effector
Molecule binding at one site that changes another site’s activity.
Bohr effect
H⁺ and CO₂ lower Hb’s O₂ affinity.
2,3-BPG
Glycolytic by-product in red cells that binds deoxy-Hb and lowers affinity.
Carboxyhemoglobin
Hb with CO bound.
Methemoglobin
Hb with Fe³⁺, unable to carry O₂.
HbA1c
Glycated HbA; marker of average glucose.
Thalassemia
Reduced or absent synthesis of a globin chain.
Hydrops fetalis
Severe fetal edema; occurs in Hb Bart disease.
Compound heterozygote
Two different abnormal alleles of one gene (e.g. HbSC).
Extramedullary hematopoiesis
Blood formation outside the bone marrow.
