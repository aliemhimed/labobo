Structures of the Major Compounds of the Body

The building blocks of every later lecture: carbon skeletons and functional groups, then carbohydrates, lipids and the nitrogen-containing compounds.

Labobo topic: Biochem Wk 2: Major Biological Compounds

In 60 seconds

  • Body molecules are built mainly from C, H, O, N, S and P; carbon forms 4 covalent bonds.
  • Oxidation = loss of electrons (lose H or gain O); reduction = gain of electrons (gain H or lose O).
  • Aldehyde sugar = aldose; ketone sugar = ketose. Natural glucose is D-glucose.
  • Glucose and galactose are epimers at C4; glucose and mannose are epimers at C2.
  • Lactose = galactose β(1→4) glucose. Glycogen = α1→4 chains with α1→6 branches, built on glycogenin.
  • Oleic acid is 18:1, Δ9 and an ω9 fatty acid. Phosphatidylcholine (lecithin) is the main lung surfactant lipid.
  • Sphingomyelin is the only significant sphingophospholipid; cholesterol is the precursor of all steroid hormones and of bile salts.

Carbon and functional groups

The organic molecules of the body are made mostly of carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur and phosphorus, joined by covalent bonds.

  • Carbon is the key element: it forms four covalent bonds, through single or double bonds, building carbon backbones of any size.
  • Aliphatic: carbon chains (straight or branched, single or double bonds) that contain no ring.
  • Rings are common too. The most common is the six-membered benzene ring, also called a phenyl group when attached to a molecule.

Functional groups

A molecule is defined by its carbon skeleton and its functional groups: groups where carbon is bonded to oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur or phosphate. Their names often appear in the compound’s name.

Groups that carry a charge at body pH (carboxylate –COO⁻, amino –NH₃⁺, phosphate, sulfate) make a molecule more water-soluble.

Oxidised and reduced groups

Carbon–carbon and carbon–oxygen groups are called oxidised or reduced according to the number of electrons around the carbon.

OxidationReduction
ElectronsLoss of electronsGain of electrons
Seen asLoss of H atoms (with one or two electrons), or gain of an O atom or OH groupGain of H atoms, or loss of an O atom

Order from most reduced to most oxidised carbon: alkane (–CH₃) → alcohol (–CH₂OH) → aldehyde (–CHO) → carboxylic acid (–COOH) → CO₂. This is why fats (very reduced) give more energy than sugars when they are oxidised.

Mnemonic: OIL RIG

Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain (of electrons).

Carbohydrates: types

Carbohydrates are made of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. Chemically they are polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones (or their derivatives), and their main job is to supply energy.

Monosaccharides and isomers

Aldoses and ketoses

A simple monosaccharide is a chain of three or more carbons. One carbon forms a carbonyl group (C=O).

  • Carbonyl is an aldehyde (at the end of the chain) → aldose (e.g. glucose).
  • Carbonyl is a ketone (inside the chain) → ketose (e.g. fructose).

NameTrioseTetrosePentoseHexoseHeptose

Chiral carbons, D and L sugars

  • A carbon attached to four different groups is an asymmetric (chiral) centre.
  • Its groups can be arranged in two ways that are mirror images and cannot be superimposed. Such mirror-image pairs are enantiomers, named D and L.
  • D and L are still used in medicine for sugars and amino acids.
  • Natural glucose is D-glucose. L-glucose is made synthetically in small amounts and is less biologically active.

Epimers

Epimers are stereoisomers that differ at only one of their asymmetric carbons.

PairDiffer atNote
D-glucose and D-galactoseCarbon 4Interconverted in human cells by epimerases
D-glucose and D-mannoseCarbon 2
Enantiomer or epimer?

Enantiomers are mirror images: opposite at every chiral centre. Epimers differ at one chiral centre only.

Substituted sugars and glycosidic bonds

Substituted sugars

Sugars often carry extra groups: phosphate (PO₄³⁻), amino (NH₂), sulfate (SO₄²⁻) or N-acetyl (NHCOCH₃). Most free monosaccharides inside cells are phosphorylated at their terminal carbon (for example glucose 6-phosphate). The charged phosphate stops the sugar leaving the cell, trapping it inside.

Glycosidic bonds

A glycosidic bond is a covalent bond that joins a sugar, through its anomeric carbon (the carbonyl carbon, C1 of glucose), to another molecule. It builds disaccharides, oligosaccharides and polysaccharides, and links sugars to alcohols and amines (forming glycosides and nucleosides).

In the clinic: lactase deficiency

Lactase is a brush-border enzyme of the small intestine that splits lactose into absorbable monosaccharides. Without enough lactase, lactose is almost not digested or absorbed, causing lactose intolerance.

  • Primary: lactase declines with age after childhood (no lactase-persistence allele). Common in people of Asian, African or Native American descent.
  • Secondary: the brush border is damaged, e.g. by gastroenteritis (rotavirus) or autoimmune disease.
  • Congenital: rare, from a defective gene.

Oligosaccharides

From 3 to roughly 10 (one slide says 12) monosaccharides. They are often attached to proteins by N- or O-glycosidic bonds, forming glycoproteins.

Polysaccharides

Tens to thousands of monosaccharides in linear or branched chains. Starch (plants) and glycogen (animals) contain thousands of glucose units.

  • Glycogen: chains of glucose linked α1→4, with branch points made by α1→6 bonds.
  • At the centre of every glycogen molecule is glycogenin, the protein primer on which glycogen synthesis starts.

Fatty acids

A fatty acid is usually a straight aliphatic chain with a methyl group at one end (the ω-carbon) and a carboxyl group at the other.

  • Most human fatty acids have an even number of carbons, usually 16–20.
  • Saturated: only single bonds. Most common: palmitic acid (C16) and stearic acid (C18).
  • Unsaturated: one or more double bonds. Monounsaturated = one; polyunsaturated = two or more.

How to read the shorthand

Oleic acid is 18:1, Δ9:

  • 18 = number of carbons;
  • 1 = number of double bonds;
  • Δ9 = the double bond is between carbons 9 and 10, counting from the carboxyl carbon (C1).

The ω (omega) number counts from the other end, the methyl (ω) carbon, to the nearest double bond. Oleic acid is an ω9 fatty acid; linolenic acid is an ω3 fatty acid.

Δ versus ω

Δ counts from the carboxyl end. ω counts from the methyl end. For oleic acid (18 carbons, double bond at 9–10), both happen to give 9.

Acylglycerols and phosphoacylglycerols

Acylglycerols

An acylglycerol is glycerol with one or more fatty acids (acyl groups) attached by ester bonds.

  • Mono-, di- and triacylglycerols carry one, two and three fatty acids.
  • Triacylglycerols (fats) rarely have the same fatty acid at all three positions, so they are called mixed triacylglycerols.
  • Unsaturated fatty acids are most often on carbon 2.

Phosphoacylglycerols (glycerophospholipids)

Fatty acids on carbons 1 and 2 of glycerol, and a phosphate (alone or with a substituent) on carbon 3.

  • Phosphatidylcholine (also called lecithin) is one of the major membrane phosphoacylglycerols.
  • It is the main component of pulmonary surfactant.
In the clinic: surfactant

Surfactant lines the alveoli (air sacs) and reduces surface tension. Without it the alveoli would collapse during exhalation. Premature babies who have not yet made enough surfactant develop breathing difficulty for this reason.

Sphingolipids and Niemann–Pick

Sphingolipids are built on sphingosine, not glycerol.

  1. A long-chain fatty acid is attached to the amino group of sphingosine by an amide bond → ceramide.
  2. The alcohol group at carbon 1 of sphingosine is esterified to phosphorylcholine → sphingomyelin.
  • Sphingomyelin is the only significant sphingophospholipid in humans.
  • It is an important part of the myelin sheath of nerve fibres.
In the clinic: Niemann–Pick disease (syllabus correlation)

An inherited deficiency of the lysosomal enzyme sphingomyelinase, which breaks down sphingomyelin. Sphingomyelin builds up in lysosomes, especially of the liver, spleen and brain, causing enlarged liver and spleen and, in the severe form, nerve damage in infancy.

Steroids

  • Steroids have a four-ring structure called the steroid nucleus.
  • Cholesterol is the precursor of all steroid hormones, made by changing the rings or the C20 side chain.
  • Cholesterol itself is poorly soluble in water, but it is converted into amphipathic, water-soluble bile salts such as cholic acid.
  • Bile salts coat fat droplets in the intestine, forming micelles, which keep the fat emulsified in the watery gut contents so it can be digested.

Amino acids and nitrogen rings

Amino acids

  • Contain an amino group and a carboxylic acid group.
  • In proteins they are always L-α-amino acids.
  • D-amino acids are not normally put into proteins, but bacteria use them, e.g. to build cross-links in their cell walls.

Nitrogen-containing rings

The body’s nitrogen rings are purines, pyrimidines and pyridines (e.g. niacin and pyridoxine, vitamin B6). The ending “-ine” shows nitrogen (amine) in the ring.

Exam traps

Exam trap
  • Oxidation is loss of electrons and of H; gaining O is also oxidation.
  • Glucose/galactose differ at C4, not C2 (that is mannose).
  • Lactose has a β(1→4) bond; glycogen chains are α1→4.
  • Proteins use L-amino acids, while natural sugars are D.
  • Phosphorylating a sugar traps it in the cell; it does not help it leave.
  • The “ω” carbon is the methyl end, not the carboxyl end.

Check yourself

  1. A carbon chain with no ring is described as:

    1. Aromatic
    2. Aliphatic
    3. Phenyl
    4. Chiral
    Show answer

    B. Aliphatic structures are straight or branched chains without a ring.

  2. Which change is an oxidation?

    1. Gain of hydrogen atoms
    2. Gain of electrons
    3. Gain of an oxygen atom
    4. Loss of a hydroxyl group
    Show answer

    C. Oxidation is loss of electrons, seen as loss of H or gain of O or OH.

  3. A six-carbon sugar with a ketone group is a:

    1. Ketohexose
    2. Aldohexose
    3. Ketopentose
    4. Aldopentose
    Show answer

    A. Ketone carbonyl = ketose; six carbons = hexose (e.g. fructose).

  4. D-glucose and D-galactose are:

    1. Enantiomers
    2. Epimers at carbon 2
    3. Epimers at carbon 4
    4. Identical
    Show answer

    C. They differ only at C4 and are interconverted by epimerases.

  5. Why are most free sugars inside cells phosphorylated?

    1. To make them sweeter
    2. To stop them being transported out of the cell
    3. To make them nonpolar
    4. To link them to proteins
    Show answer

    B. Phosphorylation at the terminal carbon traps the sugar in the cell.

  6. The bond between a sugar and a base in a nucleoside is:

    1. O-glycosidic
    2. N-glycosidic
    3. Peptide
    4. Ester
    Show answer

    B. The anomeric carbon bonds to a nitrogen of the base.

  7. Primary lactase deficiency is:

    1. Caused by rotavirus
    2. An age-dependent decline after childhood
    3. Always present at birth
    4. Caused by autoimmune disease
    Show answer

    B. Rotavirus and autoimmune disease cause secondary deficiency; the congenital form is rare.

  8. Branch points in glycogen are formed by:

    1. α1→4 bonds
    2. β1→4 bonds
    3. α1→6 bonds
    4. N-glycosidic bonds
    Show answer

    C. Chains are α1→4; branches are α1→6; glycogenin is the primer.

  9. What does “18:1, Δ9” tell you about oleic acid?

    1. 18 double bonds, 1 carbon, ω9
    2. 18 carbons, 1 double bond, between C9 and C10
    3. 18 carbons, 9 double bonds
    4. 1 carbon chain, 18 branches
    Show answer

    B. Number of carbons : number of double bonds, Δ = position from the carboxyl end.

  10. The main lipid of pulmonary surfactant is:

    1. Sphingomyelin
    2. Cholesterol
    3. Phosphatidylcholine (lecithin)
    4. Triacylglycerol
    Show answer

    C. It lowers surface tension so alveoli don’t collapse on exhalation.

  11. Sphingomyelin is made by adding phosphorylcholine to:

    1. Glycerol
    2. Ceramide
    3. Cholesterol
    4. Diacylglycerol
    Show answer

    B. Ceramide = sphingosine + long-chain fatty acid (amide bond); phosphorylcholine goes on C1.

  12. Bile salts help fat digestion by:

    1. Hydrolysing ester bonds
    2. Forming micelles that keep fat emulsified
    3. Making fat more saturated
    4. Carrying fat in the blood
    Show answer

    B. Amphipathic bile salts (e.g. cholic acid) coat fat droplets in the intestine.

Key terms

Functional group
A group of atoms (e.g. –OH, –COOH, –NH₂) that gives a molecule its chemical behaviour.
Aliphatic
A carbon chain without a ring.
Phenyl group
A benzene ring attached to a molecule.
Aldose / ketose
Sugar with an aldehyde / a ketone group.
Chiral carbon
A carbon with four different groups attached.
Enantiomers
Non-superimposable mirror-image isomers (D and L).
Epimers
Stereoisomers differing at one chiral carbon.
Epimerase
Enzyme that converts one epimer into another.
Anomeric carbon
The carbonyl carbon of a sugar, which forms glycosidic bonds.
Glycosidic bond
Covalent bond from a sugar’s anomeric carbon to an O (O-glycosidic) or N (N-glycosidic).
Glycoprotein
Protein with oligosaccharide chains attached.
Glycogenin
Protein primer at the centre of glycogen.
Saturated fatty acid
Fatty acid with only single C–C bonds.
ω-carbon
The methyl-end carbon of a fatty acid.
Triacylglycerol
Glycerol with three fatty acids in ester bonds (a fat).
Lecithin
Phosphatidylcholine; main surfactant lipid.
Ceramide
Sphingosine with a fatty acid on its amino group.
Sphingomyelin
Ceramide + phosphorylcholine; in the myelin sheath.
Micelle
A droplet of fat coated by amphipathic molecules such as bile salts.
Nucleoside / nucleotide
Base + sugar / base + sugar + phosphate.
