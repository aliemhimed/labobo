Amino Acids in Proteins

The 20 (plus one) building blocks of proteins: their structure, how they are grouped, what special jobs some of them do, how proteins are modified after they are made, and how protein sequences vary.

Labobo topic: Biochem Wk 3: Amino Acids

In 60 seconds

  • Every amino acid has an amino group, a carboxyl group and a side chain (R); proteins use only L-amino acids.
  • Glycine is the only achiral amino acid. Proline is an imino acid.
  • Methionine starts every protein (AUG) and makes SAM; cysteine forms disulfide bridges; two cysteines = cystine.
  • Arg → urea and nitric oxide; His → histamine; Glu → GABA (by GAD with vitamin B6).
  • Cystinuria: poorly soluble cystine forms hexagonal crystals and kidney stones.
  • Post-translational changes: glycosylation (N on Asn, O on Ser/Thr), fatty acylation, phosphorylation (Ser/Thr/Tyr), acetylation (Lys), ADP-ribosylation (cholera toxin).
  • HbF (α₂γ₂) binds O₂ more tightly than HbA (α₂β₂). Creatine kinase has tissue-specific isozymes.

  • explain the clinical correlation cystinuria (and cholera toxin).

General structure and stereochemistry

Amino acids are the building blocks of proteins. Each contains:

  • an amine group (–NH₂),
  • a carboxyl group (–COOH),
  • a side chain (the R group), which is different for each amino acid,

all attached to the same central carbon, the α-carbon, which also carries a hydrogen. The main elements are carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen and oxygen. At body pH the groups are charged: –NH₃⁺ and –COO⁻.

L and D forms

Because the α-carbon has four different groups, each amino acid (except glycine) exists as two non-superimposable mirror images, called enantiomers (L and D). Only L-enantiomers are found in proteins.

Classification by side chain (R group)

Amino acids can be grouped in four ways: by R group, by polarity, by nutritional requirement and by metabolic fate.

The side-chain groups and the facts the slides attach to each
GroupMembersKey facts
Aliphatic (open carbon–hydrogen chains, no ring; all hydrophobic) Glycine (Gly, G), alanine (Ala, A), valine, leucine, isoleucine Glycine: smallest, side chain is just H; the only achiral amino acid; abundant in collagen, gives flexibility.
Alanine: methyl (–CH₃) side chain; carries nitrogen in blood in the glucose–alanine cycle.
Leucine, isoleucine, valine: essential branched-chain amino acids; defects in their breakdown cause maple syrup urine disease.
Aromatic (six-carbon ring with alternating double bonds) Phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan The ring gives special chemical and physical properties (e.g. they absorb ultraviolet light).
Sulfur-containing Methionine (methyl thioether), cysteine (thiol, –SH) Methionine: starts protein synthesis (start codon AUG), so it is the first amino acid of most proteins; precursor of S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), the methyl donor in methylation reactions.
Cysteine: forms disulfide bridges, covalent links within one chain or between chains that stabilise protein structure.
Cystine = two cysteines joined by a disulfide bond (a dimer).
Hydroxy (–OH in side chain) Serine, threonine, tyrosine Form hydrogen bonds and are the sites of regulatory phosphorylation, especially in enzymes. Serine is a precursor of sphingolipids.
Amide (polar, uncharged; C=O attached to N in side chain) Asparagine, glutamine Asparagine: main target of N-glycosylation in the ER, a cotranslational modification.
Glutamine: carries nitrogen in the body.
Acidic (side-chain –COOH, negative at body pH) Aspartic acid (aspartate), glutamic acid (glutamate) Aspartate: its amino group provides one nitrogen of urea in the urea cycle.
Glutamate: main excitatory neurotransmitter of the CNS; converted to the inhibitory transmitter GABA by glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD), which needs pyridoxal phosphate (PLP, vitamin B6).
Basic (side-chain amino group, positive at body pH) Lysine, arginine, histidine Lysine: forms collagen cross-links; acetylated on histones, which affects gene expression.
Arginine: its R group gives urea in the urea cycle; precursor of the vasodilator nitric oxide (NO).
Histidine: found in the active site of many enzymes; precursor of the allergic mediator histamine.
Imino acid Proline Its nitrogen is part of a ring, so it has an –NH– (imino) group instead of –NH₂. Proline is converted to hydroxyproline, a hallmark component of collagen.
The 21st amino acid Selenocysteine (Sec) Cysteine with selenium in place of sulfur. Proteins containing it are selenoproteins; humans have 25 known ones, many of which maintain redox balance, e.g. thioredoxin reductases and glutathione peroxidases.

Essential and non-essential

  • Essential amino acids cannot be made by the body and must come from the diet.
  • Arginine and histidine may become essential when demand rises: during illness and growth phases such as pregnancy or childhood (conditionally essential).

The nine essential amino acids (textbook list): phenylalanine, valine, threonine, tryptophan, isoleucine, methionine, histidine, leucine, lysine.

Mnemonic: PVT TIM HaLL

Phe, Val, Thr, Trp, Ile, Met, His, Leu, Lys.

From the textbook: leucine and lysine are purely ketogenic; isoleucine, phenylalanine, tryptophan, tyrosine and threonine are both glucogenic and ketogenic; the rest are glucogenic only.

Cystinuria (clinical correlation)

In cystinuria, an inherited defect of the amino acid transporter in the kidney tubule (and gut) means cystine (with ornithine, lysine and arginine) is not reabsorbed and spills into the urine.

  • Cystine is less soluble than cysteine, so it crystallises: hexagonal cystine crystals and kidney stones.

Treatment: make cystine more soluble

The amino acids lost in cystinuria: Cystine, Ornithine, Lysine, Arginine. Only cystine forms stones.

Post-translational modifications

After a protein is made, enzymes can add chemical groups to some residues, oxidise them or change them in other ways. This is post-translational modification. More than 100 different modified residues are known in human proteins.

ModificationWhat is added and whereEffect / example
N-linked glycosylationOligosaccharide on the amide nitrogen of asparagineOn cell-surface proteins; protects against proteolysis and immune attack
O-linked glycosylationOligosaccharide on the –OH of serine or threonineCommon in secreted proteins
Fatty acylationPalmitoyl (C16) often on plasma membrane proteins; myristoyl (C14) often on proteins of intracellular vesicle membranesThe lipid anchors the protein in the membrane by hydrophobic interaction
PhosphorylationPhosphate on the –OH of serine, threonine or tyrosine, by a protein kinaseAdds a large negative charge, changing structure and activity
AcetylationAcetyl on lysine of histonesRemoves lysine’s positive charge, loosening its grip on DNA phosphates, which changes gene expression
ADP-ribosylationADP-ribose on a specific residueCholera toxin acting on Gs (below)
Hydroxylation–OH on proline or lysineHydroxyproline in collagen

ADP-ribosylation: cholera toxin

Cholera is a small-intestine infection by Vibrio cholerae.

  1. Cholera toxin binds intestinal cells (enterocytes) and enters them.
  2. It ADP-ribosylates the G protein Gs, locking it in its GTP-bound (active) form.
  3. Gs keeps stimulating adenylate cyclase → cAMP rises continuously.
  4. Cl⁻ and water are secreted into the gut, and Na⁺ and water entry into enterocytes falls.
  5. Rapid fluid loss from the intestine → severe dehydration (watery diarrhea).

Variations in primary structure

The primary structure of a protein is its linear amino acid sequence. Almost every residue contributes to the protein’s shape, but the sequence can still vary a little.

Between species

  • Hypervariable region: many different amino acids are tolerated at a position.
  • Invariant region: binding sites and parts critical for the 3-D shape; exactly the same sequence between individuals, tissues and species.

During development: hemoglobin

HemoglobinChainsWhenO₂ affinity
HbF (fetal)2 α + 2 γLast trimester of pregnancy until after birthHigher: helps the fetus take O₂ at low O₂ tension
HbA (adult)2 α + 2 βReplaces HbF after birthLower

Between tissues: isoforms (isozymes)

Proteins that differ somewhat in sequence and properties from tissue to tissue but keep the same function are tissue-specific isoforms or isozymes. Example: creatine kinase (CK), made of two subunits with 60–72% sequence homology. From the textbook: CK-MM (skeletal muscle), CK-MB (heart), CK-BB (brain); CK-MB in blood points to heart muscle damage.

Commonly confused

TheseThe difference
Cysteine vs cystineCysteine is the single amino acid (–SH); cystine is two cysteines linked by a disulfide bond, and is less soluble.

Glucogenic vs ketogenicPyruvate/TCA intermediates (glucose) vs acetyl CoA/acetoacetate (ketone bodies).

Exam traps

Exam trap
  • Glycine is achiral; every other standard amino acid has L and D forms.
  • N-glycosylation of asparagine is cotranslational (in the ER), not after the protein is finished.
  • Cholera toxin locks Gs in the GTP-bound form; it does not block Gs.
  • HbF has higher, not lower, O₂ affinity than HbA.
  • Methionine makes SAM; serine makes sphingolipids.
  • Phosphorylation targets Ser, Thr, Tyr (hydroxy amino acids), not lysine.

Check yourself

  1. Which amino acid has no chiral centre?

    1. Alanine
    2. Glycine
    3. Proline
    4. Serine
    Show answer

    B. Glycine’s side chain is H, so its α-carbon carries two identical groups.

  2. Defects in the breakdown of leucine, isoleucine and valine cause:

    1. Phenylketonuria
    2. Cystinuria
    3. Maple syrup urine disease
    4. Gaucher disease
    Show answer

    C. These are the essential branched-chain amino acids.

  3. S-adenosylmethionine is made from:

    1. Cysteine
    2. Methionine
    3. Serine
    4. Arginine
    Show answer

    B. SAM is the methyl donor for methylation reactions.

  4. GABA is made from glutamate by an enzyme that requires:

    1. Thiamine
    2. Pyridoxal phosphate (vitamin B6)
    3. Biotin
    4. Vitamin C
    Show answer

    B. Glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD) uses PLP.

  5. Which amino acid is the precursor of nitric oxide?

    1. Histidine
    2. Lysine
    3. Arginine
    4. Glutamine
    Show answer

    C. Arginine also provides urea in the urea cycle.

  6. Which crystals are typical of cystinuria?

    1. Envelope-shaped
    2. Hexagonal
    3. Needle-shaped
    4. Coffin-lid
    Show answer

    B. Poorly soluble cystine forms hexagonal crystals and stones.

  7. Which treatment increases cystine solubility by chelation?

    1. Potassium citrate
    2. Acetazolamide
    3. Penicillamine
    4. Hydration
    Show answer

    C. Potassium citrate and acetazolamide alkalinise the urine; fluids dilute it.

  8. N-linked oligosaccharides are attached to:

    1. The –OH of serine
    2. The amide nitrogen of asparagine
    3. The –SH of cysteine
    4. The ε-amino group of lysine
    Show answer

    B. O-linked sugars go on serine or threonine.

  9. Cholera toxin causes diarrhea by:

    1. Inhibiting adenylate cyclase
    2. ADP-ribosylating Gs and keeping it active, raising cAMP
    3. Phosphorylating the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase
    4. Acetylating histones
    Show answer

    B. Gs is locked in its GTP-bound form, so cAMP stays high and fluid is lost.

  10. HbF differs from HbA because it has:

    1. γ chains instead of β chains and higher O₂ affinity
    2. γ chains instead of α chains
    3. Lower O₂ affinity
    4. Only two chains
    Show answer

    A. HbF is α₂γ₂; HbA is α₂β₂.

  11. Which amino acid is purely ketogenic?

    1. Alanine
    2. Leucine
    3. Serine
    4. Aspartate
    Show answer

    B. Leucine (and lysine) give only acetyl CoA or acetoacetate.

  12. Selenocysteine is:

    1. Cysteine with selenium instead of sulfur
    2. Methionine with selenium
    3. A D-amino acid
    4. Found only in bacteria
    Show answer

    A. It is the 21st amino acid; humans have 25 selenoproteins such as glutathione peroxidases.

Key terms

α-carbon
Central carbon carrying the amino group, carboxyl group, H and R group.
R group
The side chain that makes each amino acid different.
Enantiomers
Mirror-image L and D forms; proteins use L.
Branched-chain amino acids
Leucine, isoleucine, valine.
Disulfide bridge
Covalent S–S bond between two cysteines.
SAM
S-adenosylmethionine, the main methyl donor.
Imino acid
Proline, whose nitrogen is in a ring (–NH–).
Selenoprotein
Protein containing selenocysteine.
Essential amino acid
One the body cannot make; must be eaten.
Glucogenic / ketogenic
Broken down to glucose precursors / ketone body precursors.
Cystinuria
Inherited loss of cystine (and Orn, Lys, Arg) in urine, causing stones.
Post-translational modification
Enzymatic change to a protein after its synthesis.
Protein kinase
Enzyme that adds phosphate to Ser, Thr or Tyr.
ADP-ribosylation
Transfer of ADP-ribose to a protein; how cholera toxin acts.
Isozymes
Tissue-specific forms of an enzyme with the same function.
Hypervariable region
Part of a sequence where many residues are tolerated.
