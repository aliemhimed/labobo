The Extracellular Matrix and Fibrous Proteins

What fills the space between cells: collagen, elastin, laminin, proteoglycans, integrins, fibronectin and the enzymes that remodel them, plus the diseases when any of these go wrong.

Labobo topic: Biochem Wk 6: ECM & Fibrous Proteins

In 60 seconds

  • ECM = collagen (strength) + elastin (stretch) + adhesive proteins (fibronectin, laminin) + proteoglycans.
  • Collagen is the most abundant body protein: a triple helix of Gly-X-Y repeats; glycine every third residue.
  • Synthesis needs vitamin C (hydroxylation) and copper (lysyl oxidase cross-links). Lack of C → scurvy; lack of Cu → Menkes.
  • Collagen diseases: osteogenesis imperfecta (type I), Ehlers–Danlos (I, III, V, processing enzymes), Alport (type IV).
  • Elastin: Gly 31%, Ala 22%, Pro 11%, cross-linked by desmosine; built on fibrillin microfibrils (defective in Marfan).
  • α1-antitrypsin from the liver inhibits neutrophil elastase; deficiency → early panacinar emphysema and cirrhosis.
  • Proteoglycans are about 95% GAG; mucopolysaccharidoses (Hurler, Hunter) come from missing lysosomal enzymes. MMPs are Ca²⁺-dependent zinc endopeptidases, blocked by TIMPs.

The extracellular matrix

The extracellular matrix (ECM) is the network that fills the spaces between cells. It gives structure, determines the shape of tissues, and links cells and tissues together.

ComponentExamplesMain job
Structural proteinsCollagen; elastinStrength; elasticity
Specialised adhesive proteinsFibronectin; lamininLink cells to the matrix
ProteoglycansCore protein + GAG chainsHydration, gel, resistance to compression

Fibrous proteins

Fibrous proteins fold into long filaments or sheets with repeated amino acid sequences. They are not very soluble in water and give tissues strength and support.

  • Collagen: strength of skin, bone, tendons, cartilage.
  • Keratin: hair, nails, skin.
  • Elastin: elasticity of ligaments and blood vessels.

Collagen structure and types

  • The most abundant protein in the human body.
  • A long, rigid molecule: three polypeptide α chains wound around one another in a rope-like triple helix, held together by interchain hydrogen bonds.
  • Rich in glycine and proline:
    • Proline’s ring causes kinks, helping each α chain take its helical shape.
    • Glycine, the smallest amino acid, is in every third position. It fits the tight space where the three chains meet in the middle.
  • Contains hydroxyproline (Hyp) and hydroxylysine (Hyl), which most other proteins lack.

Collagen synthesis

  1. Synthesis (RER): α chains are translated as preprocollagen, mostly Gly-X-Y repeats (X and Y often proline or lysine).
  2. Hydroxylation (RER): specific proline and lysine residues are hydroxylated. Requires vitamin C. Hydroxyproline maximises interchain hydrogen bonds that stabilise the triple helix.
  3. Glycosylation: sugars are added to hydroxylysine residues; three pro-α chains form procollagen, a triple helix held by hydrogen and disulfide bonds.
  4. Exocytosis: procollagen is secreted into the extracellular space.
  5. Proteolytic processing: the disulfide-rich end pieces are cut off by procollagen peptidases → insoluble tropocollagen.
  6. Cross-linking: many staggered tropocollagen molecules are joined by covalent lysine–hydroxylysine cross-links made by copper-containing lysyl oxidase → collagen fibrils.
Where each disease hits

Triple-helix formation → osteogenesis imperfecta. Hydroxylation (vitamin C) → scurvy. Processing enzymes (lysyl hydroxylase, procollagen peptidase) → Ehlers–Danlos. Cross-linking (copper, lysyl oxidase) → Menkes.

Osteogenesis imperfecta

  • A genetic “brittle bone disease”, most often from defects in COL1A1 and COL1A2.
  • Most common form: autosomal dominant, with less production of otherwise normal type I collagen (altered triple-helix formation).

Ehlers–Danlos syndrome (EDS)

Caused by deficiency of collagen-processing enzymes (e.g. lysyl hydroxylase, procollagen peptidase) or mutations in collagen types I, III or V. Typical signs: stretchy skin and loose joints.

Scurvy and copper

Scurvy: vitamin C (ascorbic acid) deficiency

Without vitamin C, prolyl and lysyl hydroxylases cannot work, so the triple helix is unstable. Signs:

  • swollen, bleeding gums; easy bruising; petechiae (small red or purple skin spots);
  • hemarthrosis (bleeding into joints); perifollicular hemorrhages; “corkscrew” hair;
  • anemia, poor wound healing, weak immune response.

Copper

Copper is part of several key enzymes:

  • Lysyl oxidase (collagen and elastin cross-links).
  • Ferroxidases such as ceruloplasmin and hephaestin, which oxidise ferrous iron (Fe²⁺) to ferric (Fe³⁺) for storage or transport in blood.
  • Ceruloplasmin is the major copper-carrying protein in the blood.
In the clinic: Menkes disease

Copper deficiency is mainly genetic, e.g. Menkes disease: a mutation in the Menkes P-type ATPase, encoded by ATP7A. Copper cannot be moved from enterocytes to the liver and other cells → low copper → weak cross-linking of collagen and elastin (plus brittle, kinky hair and nerve damage).

Alport syndrome

  • A genetic defect of type IV collagen (basement membrane).
  • Triad: glomerulonephritis, often sensorineural hearing loss, sometimes eye problems.
  • Infancy: intermittent gross hematuria. Adolescence: signs of chronic kidney disease (e.g. proteinuria), possibly hearing or (rarely) vision loss.
  • Mild forms may stay without symptoms and need only monitoring.
Memory hook

Alport: “can’t see, can’t pee, can’t hear a bee” (eye, kidney, ear).

Elastin and Marfan syndrome

Elastin is the elastic protein of elastic fibres, which are made of elastin plus microfibrils. Elastin is built from cross-linked monomers of tropoelastin.

Elastin composition
ComponentAmount / role
Glycine31%
Alanine22%
Proline11%, mostly not hydroxylated (unlike collagen)
Desmosine and isodesmosineCross-links that give elasticity

How elastin forms

  1. Cells make and secrete tropoelastin into the ECM.
  2. Fibrillin and other glycoproteins form microfibrils.
  3. Tropoelastin attaches to the microfibrils.
  4. Lysyl oxidase converts some lysines to allysine.
  5. Three allysines + one lysine form a desmosine cross-link.
  6. The cross-linked network = mature elastic fibre.
In the clinic: Marfan syndrome (defective fibrillin-1)
  • Tall, with long limbs; long, tapering fingers and toes (arachnodactyly); hypermobile joints.
  • Chest wall deformity: pectus carinatum (pigeon chest) or pectus excavatum (sunken chest).
  • Cystic medial necrosis of the aorta → aortic root aneurysm, rupture or dissection; mitral valve prolapse.
  • Lens dislocated upward and temporally (weak suspensory ligaments).

From the textbook: autosomal dominant, FBN1 gene on chromosome 15.

α1-Antitrypsin (AAT)

  • A plasma protein that inhibits proteases; its key job is to block neutrophil elastase, which digests the elastin of alveolar walls and other structural proteins.
  • Mostly made and secreted by the liver.
  • Alveoli are always exposed to low levels of neutrophil elastase from activated and dying neutrophils; AAT keeps this in check.
In the clinic: α1-antitrypsin deficiency
  • Lungs: unchecked elastase destroys elastin → early-onset panacinar emphysema, uniform destruction of the whole acinus (respiratory bronchioles and alveolar sacs). Breathlessness, wheeze, mucus, chronic cough. Smoking makes it much worse.
  • Liver: misfolded AAT gets stuck in hepatocytes → damage → cirrhosis and higher risk of hepatocellular carcinoma.

Laminin

  • After type IV collagen, the most abundant protein of basal laminae.
  • Binds type IV collagen and other ECM molecules, adding structural support.
  • A heterotrimer of α, β and γ subunits: 5 α (α1–α5), 3 β (β1–β3), 3 γ (γ1–γ3) → up to 45 combinations (5 × 3 × 3).
  • Typical example: laminin 111 = α1β1γ1.

Proteoglycans and glycosaminoglycans

A proteoglycan is a core protein with one or more covalently attached glycosaminoglycan (GAG) chains. They sit on the cell surface (signalling) or in the ECM, filling spaces and binding collagen, hyaluronan and other proteoglycans. They give the matrix its swelling and hydration.

Glycosaminoglycans

  • About 95% of a proteoglycan is GAG, so it is more polysaccharide than protein.
  • GAGs are unbranched chains of repeating disaccharides: a uronic acid (glucuronic or iduronic acid) + an amino sugar (N-acetylglucosamine or N-acetylgalactosamine).
  • Joined to a serine of the core protein through a tetrasaccharide linker: xylose–galactose–galactose–glucuronic acid.

Synthesis

  1. The core protein is made on the ER and enters its lumen, where glycosylation starts.
  2. UDP-sugars add sugars, first to the protein, then to the non-reducing end of the growing chain. Glycosylation continues in the Golgi.
  3. Sulfation in the Golgi makes the chains strongly negative.
  4. Negative charge attracts Na⁺, Na⁺ attracts water → a highly hydrated gel.

Degradation

Proteoglycans, glycoproteins and glycolipids are taken in by endocytosis and broken down by lysosomal glycosidases: endoglycosidases cut chains into shorter oligosaccharides; exoglycosidases remove sugars one by one from the non-reducing end.

Mucopolysaccharidoses

When a lysosomal glycosidase is missing, partly broken-down carbohydrates collect in vesicles inside cells (residual bodies). Organs enlarge and stop working properly. These are the mucopolysaccharidoses. The syllabus names two:

Integrins and fibronectin

Integrins

  • Transmembrane receptors for cell–cell and cell–ECM adhesion.
  • Two main jobs: attach cells to the ECM and carry signals from the ECM into the cell.
  • Also involved in extravasation, cell migration, and as virus receptors (adenovirus, echovirus, hantavirus). They allow rapid, flexible responses at the cell surface.
  • Two subunits: α decides which ligand binds; β carries signals inside and binds the cytoskeleton.
  • Over 24 integrins are made from 18 α and 8 β subunits.

α5β1Binds fibronectin; development and wound healing

Adhesion proteins: fibronectin

Adhesion proteins in the ECM link integrins to other ECM parts. Fibronectin is the prime example: a large ECM glycoprotein that binds integrins, and also collagen, fibrin and heparan sulfate proteoglycans. It takes part in cell adhesion, growth, migration and differentiation. Losing adhesion can lead to normal or abnormal cell movement.

Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs)

  • Calcium-dependent, zinc-containing endopeptidases that can degrade all kinds of ECM proteins.
  • Also cut cell-surface receptors, release apoptotic ligands and inactivate chemokines; involved in proliferation, migration, differentiation, angiogenesis, apoptosis and host defence.
  • Controlled by: gene transcription, proteolytic activation, the plasma protein α2-macroglobulin, and tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs).
  • MMPs and TIMPs must be regulated together; an imbalance contributes to some cancers and atherosclerosis.
  • Metastasis: tumour cells must degrade the ECM (collagen, elastin) to move out of their tissue and into blood or lymph; MMPs do this.

Watch out

Watch out: VLA-1

Slide 24 labels VLA-1 as “αLβ1”. VLA-1 is α1β1 (αL pairs with β2 to form LFA-1). Remember VLA-1 as an immune-cell adhesion integrin.

Watch out: Hurler and Hunter

The syllabus lists Hurler and Hunter, but the slides only define mucopolysaccharidoses in general. The table above follows Lippincott; learn the enzymes and the corneal difference.

Exam traps

Exam trap
  • Vitamin C is for hydroxylation; copper is for cross-linking (lysyl oxidase).
  • OI hearing loss is conductive; Alport hearing loss is sensorineural.
  • Vascular EDS is type III; classical EDS is type V.
  • AAT deficiency emphysema is panacinar and early; smoker’s emphysema is centriacinar.
  • MMPs need zinc and are calcium-dependent.
  • The α integrin subunit chooses the ligand, not the β.

    2. COL3A1
    3. COL4A5
    4. COL5A1

Key terms
