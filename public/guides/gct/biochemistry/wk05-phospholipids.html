Phospholipids, Sphingolipids and Eicosanoids

The membrane lipids built on glycerol and on sphingosine, what they do in signalling, the lung, the mitochondrion and the nerve, the storage diseases when their breakdown fails, and the eicosanoids that drive inflammation and clotting.

Labobo topic: Biochem Wk 5: Phospholipids & Sphingolipids

In 60 seconds

  • Membrane lipids are amphipathic; the backbone is glycerol or sphingosine. All glycerophospholipids come from phosphatidic acid.
  • DPPC (made by type II pneumocytes) is the main surfactant lipid; too little → neonatal RDS.
  • PLC splits PIP₂ into IP₃ (releases Ca²⁺ from ER) and DAG (activates PKC). GPI anchors fail in PNH (no CD55/CD59).
  • Cardiolipin: inner mitochondrial membrane; antigen in the Wassermann syphilis test.
  • Ceramide → sphingomyelin, cerebrosides, gangliosides, sulfatides. Missing lysosomal enzymes cause sphingolipidoses (Niemann–Pick, Tay–Sachs, Gaucher, Fabry, Krabbe, MLD).
  • Eicosanoids come from 20-carbon ω-6/ω-3 fatty acids (arachidonic acid). Aspirin blocks COX irreversibly; low dose (< 300 mg/day) is antiplatelet.
  • Leukotrienes: LTB4 attracts neutrophils; LTC4/D4/E4 drive asthma. Zileuton blocks 5-lipoxygenase; montelukast and zafirlukast block the receptor.

Learning objectives

  • explain the sphingolipidoses and neonatal respiratory distress syndrome;
  • describe eicosanoids, how anti-inflammatory drugs block them, and their role in asthma.

Membrane lipids

The major membrane lipids are amphipathic: they have a hydrophilic (water-loving) part and a hydrophobic (water-fearing) part.

  • Backbone: glycerol or sphingosine.
  • Hydrophilic head: a phosphate group plus an alcohol (e.g. choline, ethanolamine, serine, inositol).
  • Hydrophobic tail: fatty acids or fatty-acid-derived hydrocarbons.

Glycerophospholipids

Phospholipids with a glycerol backbone. They are the major class of phospholipids. All are derivatives of phosphatidic acid (PA): a diacylglycerol (DAG) with a phosphate on carbon 3.

Phosphatidylcholine and neonatal RDS

Dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine (DPPC) has palmitate (a saturated long-chain fatty acid) at both positions 1 and 2 of glycerol.

  • Made and secreted by type II pneumocytes.
  • A major lipid of lung surfactant.
  • Surfactant lowers surface tension of the fluid lining the alveoli, so less pressure is needed to reinflate them; this prevents alveolar collapse (atelectasis).
In the clinic: neonatal respiratory distress syndrome
  • In preterm infants, from too little surfactant made or secreted.
  • Incidence is inversely proportional to gestational age: the earlier the birth, the more likely.
  • Maternal diabetes raises the risk: the baby’s hyperinsulinemia inhibits surfactant synthesis.
  • Prevention: glucocorticoids to the mother shortly before delivery speed lung maturation.
  • Treatment: give the baby natural or synthetic surfactant after birth.

Phosphatidylinositol

Signal transduction: the PIP₂ pathway

  1. A signal activates phospholipase C (PLC).
  2. PLC splits phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP₂) into two second messengers.
  3. IP₃ (inositol trisphosphate) → releases Ca²⁺ from the endoplasmic reticulum. DAG (diacylglycerol) → activates protein kinase C (PKC).
  4. Together they control secretion, contraction and gene expression.

Anchoring proteins: GPI anchors

Some proteins are fixed to the outer surface of the membrane by a glycosyl-phosphatidylinositol (GPI) anchor, which links the protein to PI in the membrane.

  • Lipoprotein lipase, which breaks down triglycerides, is held on capillary walls by a GPI anchor.
  • DAF (CD55) stops complement activation on the cell surface; CD59 blocks formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC). Without them, red cells are treated as “non-self” and destroyed.
In the clinic: paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH)

A rare acquired disorder: a mutation in the PIGA gene of blood stem cells means GPI anchors are not made, so red cells lack CD55 and CD59. Complement destroys them inside vessels (intravascular hemolysis), giving:

  • hemoglobinuria: dark urine, especially in the morning;
  • anemia and fatigue;
  • thrombosis, the major cause of death;
  • pancytopenia in some patients.

Cardiolipin

  • Two phosphatidic acids esterified through their phosphates to an extra glycerol = cardiolipin (diphosphatidylglycerol).
  • Found in membranes of prokaryotes and eukaryotes. In eukaryotes it is almost only in the inner mitochondrial membrane, where it keeps certain electron transport chain complexes working.
  • It is antigenic: people infected with Treponema pallidum (syphilis) make anti-cardiolipin antibodies. The Wassermann test detects them using cardiolipin as the antigen.

Plasmalogens and platelet-activating factor

These are ether glycerophospholipids: carbon 1 of glycerol carries an alkyl chain joined by an ether bond, not an ester bond.

Phospholipases

  • Glycerophospholipids are broken down by phospholipases, present in all tissues and in pancreatic juice.
  • Many toxins and venoms have phospholipase activity, and several pathogenic bacteria make phospholipases that dissolve cell membranes and help infection spread.

Sphingophospholipids

  • Backbone: sphingosine, made from serine + palmitate.
  • Ceramide = sphingosine + long-chain fatty acid by an amide bond. Ceramide is the precursor of cerebrosides and gangliosides.
  • Sphingomyelin = ceramide whose C1–OH binds phosphorylcholine. It is the major sphingophospholipid, essential for the myelin sheath and fast nerve conduction.
  • Ceramides maintain the skin’s water-permeability barrier; low ceramide levels are linked to several skin disorders.

Synthesis of sphingomyelin

  1. Palmitoyl CoA + serine condense → (via 3-ketosphinganine) sphinganine. Needs PLP (vitamin B6).
  2. Sphinganine is acylated on its amino group with a long-chain fatty acid, then desaturated → ceramide.
  3. Phosphorylcholine is transferred from phosphatidylcholine (PC) to ceramide → sphingomyelin + DAG.

Breakdown of sphingomyelin

  1. Sphingomyelinase (a type of phospholipase C) removes phosphorylcholine → ceramide.
  2. Ceramidase splits ceramide → sphingosine + free fatty acid.

Niemann–Pick disease

Cause: sphingomyelinase deficiency → sphingomyelin builds up. Type C is different: NPC1/NPC2 mutations → cholesterol and sphingomyelin build up.

TypeDefectFeatures
A: severe infantileSphingomyelinaseProgressive neurodegeneration, developmental delay, cherry-red spot on the macula, hepatosplenomegaly, foam cells (lipid-laden macrophages); fatal in early childhood
B: chronic visceralSphingomyelinaseLittle or no nerve involvement; affects liver and spleen; later onset, longer survival
C: lipid transport defectNPC1/NPC2Cholesterol + sphingomyelin accumulate; variable nerve involvement
Why a cherry-red spot?

Lipid builds up in the retinal ganglion cells, making the retina pale. The macula has no ganglion cells, so it stays red from the choroidal vessels underneath. Pale retina + red macula = cherry-red spot.

Glycosphingolipids

Glycosphingolipids differ from sphingomyelin: they contain no phosphate. Their head is a mono- or oligosaccharide attached directly to ceramide by an O-glycosidic bond.

  • Act as cell-surface antigens (ABO blood groups, fetal and tumour antigens); the sugar part decides the antigen.
  • Act as receptors for toxins (cholera, tetanus) and some viruses.

Synthesis

  • Mainly in the Golgi, by adding sugars one at a time from UDP-sugar donors.
  • The enzymes are glycosyltransferases, each specific for the type and position of the bond.
  • Sulfate for sulfatides comes from PAPS (3′-phosphoadenosine-5′-phosphosulfate).

Eicosanoids

  • Made from 20-carbon polyunsaturated fatty acids, either ω-6 or ω-3 (“eicosa” = 20).
  • ω-6 and ω-3 fatty acids are essential: humans cannot make them and must eat them.
  • The sn-2 position of PI and PC often stores arachidonic acid, which phospholipase A₂ can release quickly for inflammation and signalling.
  • From arachidonic acid, cyclooxygenase (COX) makes prostaglandins and thromboxanes; 5-lipoxygenase makes leukotrienes.
  • In the stomach and duodenum, acid triggers release of PGE2, which increases bicarbonate and mucus secretion and protects the epithelium from acid.

Aspirin and acetaminophen

DrugHow it blocks COXWhere
Acetaminophen (paracetamol)ReversiblyMostly in the CNS; inactivated in the periphery, so little anti-inflammatory effect

Leukotrienes and asthma

  • Made from arachidonic acid by macrophages, mast cells and leukocytes (neutrophils, eosinophils, monocytes), via 5-lipoxygenase.
  • LTA4 is inactive; it gives rise to active LTB4 and LTC4.
  • LTC4 is made by adding glutathione. Removing Glu then Gly gives LTD4 and then LTE4. LTC4, D4 and E4 are the cysteinyl leukotrienes (CysLTs).
  • LTB4 attracts neutrophils, dendritic cells and T cells (chemotaxis).
  • LTC4 increases the permeability of postcapillary venules (and CysLTs constrict bronchi).
In the clinic: asthma

Inflammation of the bronchi and bronchioles → smooth muscle contraction and recruitment of leukocytes. Long term: hypertrophy of smooth muscle and of mucus glands.

Drugs for prevention and chronic treatment: zileuton (inhibits 5-lipoxygenase), and montelukast and zafirlukast (block the CysLT receptor 1).

Watch out

Watch out: the leukotriene label

Slide 24 writes “Leukotriene C4 (LTB5) increases the permeability of postcapillary venules”. “LTB5” is a typo: the molecule meant is LTC4. LTB4 is the one that attracts neutrophils.

Watch out: different textbook

This week follows Lippincott (Chapter 17), not Marks. Use Lippincott’s wording for sphingolipid diseases.

Exam traps

Exam trap
  • DPPC is made by type II, not type I, pneumocytes.
  • Maternal diabetes raises RDS risk (fetal insulin inhibits surfactant).
  • PNH is acquired (PIGA mutation in stem cells), not inherited.
  • Cardiolipin is in the inner, not outer, mitochondrial membrane.
  • Sphingomyelinase is a type of phospholipase C.
  • Glycosphingolipids have no phosphate.
  • Aspirin inhibits COX irreversibly; acetaminophen reversibly.
  • LTA4 is inactive.

Key terms

Amphipathic
Having both a water-loving and a water-fearing part.
Phosphatidic acid
DAG with phosphate on C3; parent of glycerophospholipids.
DPPC
Dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine, main surfactant lipid.
Atelectasis
Collapse of alveoli.
PIP₂
Phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate, split by PLC.
GPI anchor
Glycosyl-phosphatidylinositol tail holding a protein to the membrane.
Cardiolipin
Diphosphatidylglycerol of the inner mitochondrial membrane.
Plasmalogen
Ether glycerophospholipid with an unsaturated alkyl chain at C1.
Ceramide
Sphingosine + fatty acid (amide bond).
Cerebroside
Ceramide + one sugar (galactose or glucose).
Ganglioside
Ceramide oligosaccharide with NANA.
Sulfatide
Sulfated galactocerebroside.
PAPS
The sulfate donor.
Sphingolipidosis
Lysosomal storage disease from a missing sphingolipid-degrading enzyme.
Eicosanoids
Signalling molecules from 20-carbon fatty acids: prostaglandins, thromboxanes, leukotrienes.
COX
Cyclooxygenase; makes prostaglandins and thromboxanes.
5-Lipoxygenase
Makes leukotrienes; blocked by zileuton.
