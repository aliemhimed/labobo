The Cytoskeleton

The three protein filament systems of the cell: microtubules (tubulin, MTOC, kinesin and dynein), microfilaments (actin, treadmilling, cortex) and intermediate filaments (keratins, vimentin family, neurofilaments, lamins).

Labobo topic: Histo Lec 7: Cytoskeleton

In 60 seconds

  • Three filaments: microtubules (thickest, ~25 nm), intermediate filaments (~10 nm), microfilaments (thinnest, 5–7 nm). Together: cell shape, organelle and vesicle movement, whole-cell movement.
  • Microtubules: α/β-tubulin heterodimers → 13 protofilaments form the wall. Organised by the MTOC (centrosome with centrioles).
  • Motors use ATP: kinesin → toward the + end (away from the nucleus, anterograde); dynein → toward the − end (toward the nucleus, retrograde).
  • Microfilaments: G-actin → F-actin double helix (needs K⁺, Mg²⁺). Nucleated by formin and Arp2/3 (branches). Treadmilling: add at +, lose at −. Form the cell cortex.
  • Intermediate filaments: stable, give mechanical strength; dimers → antiparallel tetramers → bundles. Types: keratins (epithelia; tonofibrils), vimentin, desmin (muscle), GFAP (glia), neurofilaments (neurons), lamins (nuclear lamina).

Learning objectives

  • compare the three cytoskeletal filaments by subunit, size, stability and function;
  • describe microtubule structure, MTOCs and motor proteins;
  • describe actin assembly, treadmilling and the cortex;
  • name the intermediate filament proteins and the cells that contain them.

Overview

  • The cytoskeleton is a complex array of microtubules, microfilaments (actin filaments) and intermediate filaments.
  • These protein polymers:
    • determine cell shape;
    • move organelles and vesicles;
    • allow whole cells to move.

Microtubules

  • Fine, dynamic, hollow tubes in the cytoplasm of eukaryotic cells (~25 nm across).
  • Functions: structural support, cell shape, cell division (the mitotic spindle) and intracellular transport. Also the core of cilia and flagella.
  • Subunit: a heterodimer of α- and β-tubulin.
  • Dimers line up end to end as protofilaments; 13 parallel protofilaments form the wall.
  • They have polarity: a fast-growing plus (+) end and a minus (−) end anchored at the MTOC.

MTOCs and motor proteins

  • Microtubules grow from microtubule-organising centres (MTOCs). The commonest MTOC is the centrosome, near the nucleus, which contains a pair of centrioles and organises microtubules during cell division.
  • Microtubules act as tracks. Motor proteins use ATP to carry vesicles and organelles along them, in opposite directions:
MotorDirectionName of transport
KinesinAway from the MTOC/nucleus toward the + end (cell periphery)Anterograde
Cytoplasmic dyneinToward the − end, generally toward the nucleusRetrograde

Needed, for example, to extend the ER and move material between the Golgi and other parts of the cell.

Mnemonic

Kinesin Kicks things out (+ end); Dynein Drags them back home (− end).

Microfilaments (actin filaments)

  • Thin (5–7 nm) filaments of actin: cell movement and contraction.
  • G-actin (globular monomers) assemble into F-actin (filamentous), a double-stranded helix, in the presence of K⁺ and Mg²⁺.
  • New filaments are started (nucleated) by formin (straight filaments) and the Arp2/3 complex (which also makes branching networks).
  • Dynamic: monomers are added quickly at the + end and removed from the − end = treadmilling. This allows fast changes in structure.
  • Abundant just under the plasma membrane, forming the cell cortex, which supports endocytosis and cell movement. Also in microvilli and, with myosin, in muscle.

Intermediate filaments

  • Unlike the other two, they are stable (not constantly assembled and disassembled) and give mechanical strength.
  • Size ~10 nm: “intermediate” between microfilaments and microtubules.
  • Made of different proteins in different cell types.
  1. Subunits are coiled rod-like dimers.
  2. Two dimers join antiparallel → a tetramer (so the filament has no polarity).
  3. Tetramers assemble into large, stable bundles (protofibrils) → filament.

There are more than a dozen kinds; defects cause specific diseases. Because each cell type has its own kind, pathologists stain for them to identify the origin of a tumour.

Watch out

Watch out: vimentin, desmin and GFAP

The slide reads as if vimentin works “via” desmin and GFAP. They are separate intermediate filament proteins of the same family: vimentin in mesenchymal cells, desmin in muscle, GFAP in astrocytes.

Exam traps

Exam trap
  • 13 protofilaments, not 9 or 12.
  • Kinesin goes to the plus end; dynein to the minus end.
  • Actin filaments are the thinnest; microtubules the thickest.
  • Intermediate filaments are the stable ones with no polarity.
  • Lamins are intermediate filaments inside the nucleus.
  • Keratin = epithelium; desmin = muscle; GFAP = glia.

Key terms

Protofilament
Line of tubulin dimers; 13 make a microtubule.
MTOC
Microtubule-organising centre.
Centrosome
Main MTOC, containing two centrioles.
Kinesin / dynein
Motors toward + end / − end.
G-actin / F-actin
Monomer / filament form of actin.
Treadmilling
Simultaneous growth at + and loss at − end.
Cell cortex
Actin network under the plasma membrane.
Tonofibrils
Keratin bundles in skin cells.
Nuclear lamina
Lamin network lining the nuclear envelope.
