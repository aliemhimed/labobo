Histology and Its Methods of Study

What histology studies, how a piece of tissue becomes a stained slide (fixation to sectioning), how H&E staining works, and the special methods that find specific molecules: autoradiography, cell culture, enzyme histochemistry, immunohistochemistry and in situ hybridisation.

Labobo topic: Histo Lec 1: Methods of Study

In 60 seconds

  • Histology = the study of tissues and how they are arranged into organs. A tissue = cells + extracellular matrix (ECM).
  • Tissue is too thick for light, so we cut thin sections. Steps, in order: Fixation → Dehydration → Clearing → Infiltration → Embedding → Trimming (then cut on a microtome).
  • Fixative: formaldehyde (cross-links proteins, stops enzymes). Dehydration: rising alcohol. Embedding medium: paraffin.
  • Basophilic parts (DNA, RNA) take basic dyes such as hematoxylin (blue-purple). Acidophilic parts (most proteins, collagen) take acidic dyes such as eosin (pink). H&E is the commonest stain.
  • Autoradiography: radioactive precursors → black silver grains show where new molecules are (³H-thymidine = DNA-replicating cells).
  • Enzyme histochemistry: unfixed tissue, cryostat, substrate → insoluble coloured product (phosphatases, dehydrogenases, peroxidase).
  • Immunohistochemistry: labelled antibodies find proteins; indirect (secondary antibody) is more sensitive. In situ hybridisation: labelled probes find DNA/RNA sequences.

Tissues and the ECM

  • Histology is the study of the body’s tissues and how they are arranged to form organs.
  • Every tissue has two interacting parts: cells and the extracellular matrix (ECM).
  • The ECM is made of many macromolecules, most forming complex structures such as collagen fibrils. It:
    • supports the cells;
    • holds the fluid that brings nutrients to cells and carries away wastes and secretions.
  • During development, cells and their matrix become specialised, giving the basic tissue types. Organs are orderly combinations of these tissues; the precise arrangement lets each organ (and the whole body) work.
  • Cells and matrix are tiny, so histology depends on microscopes and molecular methods.

Preparing tissue for study

The commonest procedure is to make sections (thin slices) that can be examined with transmitted light (light that passes through the tissue). Most tissues are too thick for light to pass through, so thin, translucent sections are cut and put on glass slides. The ideal slide is preserved so it looks exactly as the tissue did in the body.

  1. Fixation. Small pieces go into chemicals that cross-link proteins and inactivate degradative enzymes, so the cells do not digest themselves. A common fixative is formaldehyde (the slide says 37%).
  2. Dehydration. The tissue passes through increasingly concentrated alcohol (e.g. 70% → 95% → 100%) to remove all water. Paraffin and water do not mix, so water must go.
  3. Clearing. Alcohol is replaced by an organic solvent (e.g. xylene) that mixes with both alcohol and paraffin. It is called clearing because the tissue turns translucent.
  4. Infiltration. The tissue sits in melted paraffin (in an oven, ~56–60 °C) until paraffin fills it completely.
  5. Embedding. The infiltrated tissue is put in a small mould of paraffin and allowed to harden into a block.
  6. Trimming. The block is trimmed to expose the tissue, then sliced on a microtome (sections of a few micrometres, ~3–10 μm).
Mnemonic: order of the steps

Fix, Dehydrate, Clear, Infiltrate, Embed, Trim: “Find Dad’s Car In Every Town.”

Staining

  • Most cells and ECM are colourless, so sections must be stained. Stains make components both visible and distinguishable.
  • Many stains work by charge:
    • Basophilic (“base-loving”) components are acidic, negatively charged (e.g. nucleic acids, with their phosphate groups). They attract basic dyes.
    • Acidophilic (“acid-loving”) components are basic (many proteins). They attract acidic dyes.

  • H&E (hematoxylin and eosin) is the most widely used stain.
  • Special stains give extra contrast: trichrome stains (e.g. separate collagen from muscle) and the periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) reaction (magenta for carbohydrate-rich molecules such as glycogen and glycoproteins).

Autoradiography

Finds where newly made macromolecules are in cells.

  1. Give living cells radioactively labelled building blocks (metabolites).
  2. They are built into DNA, RNA or protein.
  3. Sections are coated with photographic emulsion; the weak radiation exposes it.
  4. After developing, small black grains of metallic silver sit over the labelled molecules.
  • ³H-thymidine (tritiated thymidine) is only used to make DNA, so labelled nuclei = cells replicating DNA.
  • Inject radioactive amino acids and take samples over time to track protein production and secretion (e.g. ER → Golgi → secretory granules).

Cell and tissue culture

  • Live cells are kept in vitro (outside the body) so we can watch them directly and do experiments impossible in vivo.
  • Cells grow in complex nutrient solutions, often with serum or growth factors.
  • They are separated from tissue mechanically and enzymatically and grown in sterile dishes: these are primary cultures.
  • Most normal cells have a finite lifespan; some become immortalised by transformation, changes like those in early cancer cells.
  • Histology on cultured cells taught us much about the cytoskeleton (microtubules, microfilaments).

Enzyme histochemistry

  • Localises structures by a specific enzyme activity.
  • To keep enzymes working, tissue is unfixed or only mildly fixed and cut on a cryostat (a microtome in a freezer). Paraffin processing would destroy the enzymes.
  1. Put the section in the enzyme’s substrate.
  2. The enzyme acts on it.
  3. Add a marker compound that reacts with the product.
  4. An insoluble, coloured or dense precipitate forms exactly where the enzyme is, visible by light or electron microscopy.

Visualising specific molecules: immunohistochemistry

  • A tagged compound that binds one target molecule is added. The tag must be visible: fluorescent label, radioactive atom, enzyme (e.g. peroxidase) or metal particle (e.g. gold for electron microscopy). This can detect sugars, proteins and nucleic acids.
  • Immunohistochemistry (IHC) uses labelled antibodies to find a specific protein.
  • Antibodies are made by injecting a purified protein into another species (e.g. human protein into a rabbit); its immune system makes antibodies against it.

Hybridisation and in situ hybridisation (ISH)

  • Hybridisation = binding of two complementary nucleic acid strands (DNA or RNA) by base pairing. Used to detect, identify or quantify specific sequences.
  • In situ hybridisation finds specific DNA or RNA sequences inside fixed cells or tissue sections (“in situ” = in place).
  1. Sample preparation: fix (usually formaldehyde) and section.
  2. Hybridisation: incubate with labelled probes under conditions favouring specific binding.
  3. Washing: remove unbound probe.
  4. Detection: see the probe by microscopy, using fluorescence (FISH) or autoradiography.

Commonly confused

PairDifference
Basophilic vs basic dyeA basophilic structure is acidic and binds a basic dye (hematoxylin).
Dehydration vs clearingDehydration removes water (alcohol); clearing removes alcohol (solvent).
Infiltration vs embeddingInfiltration fills the tissue with paraffin; embedding sets it in a hardened block.
Microtome vs cryostatCryostat = microtome in a cold chamber for frozen, unfixed tissue (enzymes survive).
IHC vs ISHIHC finds proteins (antibodies); ISH finds nucleic acids (probes).

Watch out

Watch out: “37% formaldehyde”

37% formaldehyde is the stock solution (formalin). For fixing tissue it is diluted: the usual fixative is 10% buffered formalin, about 4% formaldehyde. If an exam asks for the fixative, answer “formaldehyde/formalin”.

Watch out: hematoxylin “basic”

Strictly, hematoxylin needs a metal mordant to act as a basic dye. For exams treat it as the basic dye that stains nucleic acids.

Exam traps

Exam trap
  • Dehydration comes before clearing; clearing before infiltration.
  • Nucleic acids are basophilic; collagen is acidophilic (eosin).
  • ³H-thymidine labels DNA (not RNA: RNA uses uridine).
  • Enzyme histochemistry uses unfixed / lightly fixed, frozen tissue.
  • Indirect IHC is the more sensitive one.
  • Monoclonal = one clone.

Key terms

Histology
Study of tissues and how they form organs.
ECM
Extracellular matrix: material between cells.
Fixation
Chemical preservation by cross-linking proteins.
Microtome
Instrument that cuts thin sections.
Cryostat
Microtome in a freezer for frozen sections.
Basophilia
Staining with basic dyes (hematoxylin).
Acidophilia
Staining with acidic dyes (eosin).
PAS
Periodic acid–Schiff: stains carbohydrates magenta.
Autoradiography
Locating radiolabelled molecules with photographic emulsion.
Immunohistochemistry
Locating proteins with labelled antibodies.
In situ hybridisation
Locating nucleic acid sequences with labelled probes.
