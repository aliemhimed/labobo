The Plasma Membrane, Transport and Signalling

Cell differentiation, the structure of the plasma membrane (lipids, proteins, glycocalyx, lipid rafts), how molecules cross it (diffusion, channels, carriers, pumps), endocytosis and exocytosis, and how cells receive signals.

Labobo topic: Histo Lec 3: Plasma Membrane & Transport

In 60 seconds

  • Cells are the smallest living units. All come from the zygote → blastomeres → inner cell mass (embryonic stem cells) → differentiated cells.
  • Plasma membrane: phospholipids, cholesterol, proteins + oligosaccharides; 7.5–10 nm thick, seen only by EM as three layers (dark–light–dark: trilaminar).
  • Proteins ≈ 50% of membrane weight: integral (in the bilayer; multipass if they cross many times) and peripheral. Sugars outside = glycocalyx. Fluid mosaic model; lipid rafts are cholesterol-rich, less fluid.
  • Passive: diffusion (small nonpolar), channels (pores), carriers (change shape). Active: pumps (ATPases) use ATP against gradients.
  • Endocytosis: phagocytosis (large particles, phagosome), pinocytosis (fluid, ~80 nm vesicles, transcytosis), receptor-mediated (e.g. LDL).
  • Exocytosis: vesicle fuses with the membrane, often triggered by Ca²⁺. Constitutive (continuous) vs regulated (on a signal).
  • Signalling: endocrine, paracrine, synaptic, autocrine, juxtacrine. ~25 receptor families. Membrane receptors: channel-linked, enzymatic, G protein-coupled.

Learning objectives

  • describe how cells differentiate from the zygote;
  • describe the structure of the plasma membrane and the fluid mosaic model;
  • distinguish passive and active transport;
  • compare the three kinds of endocytosis and the two kinds of exocytosis;
  • classify signalling by distance and receptors by mechanism.

Cells and differentiation

  • Cells + extracellular material make up all tissues. Cells are the basic structural and functional units: the smallest living parts of the body.
  • Animal cells are eukaryotic: a membrane-bound nucleus surrounded by cytoplasm.
  1. Zygote: sperm + egg.
  2. Early divisions → blastomeres.
  3. Blastomeres → inner cell mass, which gives rise to all fetal tissues (grown in culture, these are embryonic stem cells).
  4. Differentiation: cells switch on specific genes for special functions, often changing shape.

Example: muscle precursors become long fibres full of actin and myosin, turning chemical energy into contraction.

The plasma membrane

  • Made of phospholipids, cholesterol and proteins, with oligosaccharide chains attached to many of them.
  • Jobs:
    • selective barrier: controls what enters and leaves; keeps the cytoplasm’s ion balance different from extracellular fluid;
    • recognition and signalling with the environment;
    • integrins link the inner cytoskeleton to the ECM.
  • Very thin: 7.5–10 nm, visible only with the electron microscope.
  • Phospholipids have a hydrophilic head and hydrophobic tails, forming a bilayer.
  • The two layers have different phospholipids (asymmetry). Some outer lipids carry sugar chains that form a protective coat, the glycocalyx.
  • In TEM the membrane looks trilaminar: two dark lines (the heads) with a light layer between (the tails).

Membrane proteins and the fluid mosaic

  • Proteins are about 50% of the membrane’s weight.
  • Integral proteins are embedded in the bilayer. Those crossing it many times are multipass proteins.
  • Peripheral proteins sit on the surface, usually the cytoplasmic side.
  • Glycoproteins carry carbohydrate chains that add to the glycocalyx; they help in cell interactions, recognition and hormone responses.
  • Fluid mosaic model: proteins are not fixed; they drift sideways in the fluid bilayer.
  • Proteins in large enzyme complexes (especially for signal transduction) move less. They gather in lipid rafts: areas rich in cholesterol and saturated fatty acids, which are less fluid.

Membrane transport

MechanismHowEnergy
DiffusionSmall, nonpolar molecules (O2, CO2) pass straight through the lipid bilayerPassive
ChannelsMultipass proteins forming a pore; ions or small molecules pass selectivelyPassive
CarriersBind a molecule and move it across by a change of shape (conformation)Passive
PumpsEnzymes (ATPases) that hydrolyse ATP to move ions/solutes against steep gradients (e.g. Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase)Active

Passive transport moves substances down their concentration gradient, driven by their own kinetic energy.

Endocytosis

Large molecules enter mainly by endocytosis: the membrane folds inward (often after binding receptors) and pinches off a vesicle.

TypeWhat is taken inDetails
Phagocytosis (“cell eating”)Large particles: bacteria, dead cellsBy macrophages and neutrophils. Membrane extends around the particle → phagosome, which fuses with a lysosome
Pinocytosis (“cell drinking”)Extracellular fluid and dissolved substancesSmall invaginations → pinocytotic vesicles (~80 nm). Fuse with lysosomes, or cross to the opposite surface and release contents: transcytosis
Receptor-mediatedSpecific ligands (e.g. LDL)Ligand binds receptor; receptors cluster in a region that invaginates. Selective

Exocytosis

  • A vesicle carrying large molecules fuses with the plasma membrane and releases them outside, without breaking the membrane.
  • Fusion is often triggered by a brief rise in cytosolic Ca²⁺ and controlled by specific membrane proteins.

Signal reception and transduction

  • Cells communicate to guide development, control growth and division, and coordinate function.
  • They may share ions and small molecules directly through gap junctions.
  • They use about 25 families of receptors for extracellular signals. Signal molecules = ligands; cells with the matching receptor = target cells.
SignallingRouteExample
EndocrineHormones carried in the blood to target cells anywhereInsulin
ParacrineDiffuses in extracellular fluid but is quickly broken down, so acts only locallyLocal growth factors
SynapticSpecial paracrine: neurotransmitters act on the next cell across a synapseAcetylcholine
AutocrineBinds receptors on the same cell that made itSome growth factors
JuxtacrineContact: a ligand on one cell surface binds a receptor on the neighbourImmune cell contacts

Receptor types

Hydrophilic signals (polypeptide hormones, neurotransmitters) cannot cross the membrane, so their receptors are transmembrane proteins in the plasma membrane. Three classes:

ReceptorWhat ligand binding does
Channel-linkedOpens a channel for ions or molecules
EnzymaticSwitches on catalytic activity in associated peripheral proteins
G protein-coupledActivates a G protein, which binds GTP and is released to activate other cytoplasmic proteins

(Hydrophobic signals such as steroid hormones cross the membrane and bind receptors inside the cell.)

Exam traps

Exam trap
  • Channels and carriers are passive; only pumps use ATP.
  • Lipid rafts are less fluid (more cholesterol and saturated fats).
  • Transcytosis = vesicle crosses the cell to the opposite surface.
  • LDL enters by receptor-mediated endocytosis.
  • Synaptic signalling is a special kind of paracrine.
  • Juxtacrine needs cell contact; autocrine acts on the same cell.

Key terms

Inner cell mass
Embryo cells that form all fetal tissues.
Integrin
Membrane protein linking cytoskeleton to ECM.
Glycocalyx
Sugar coat on the cell surface.
Fluid mosaic
Model of proteins drifting in a fluid lipid bilayer.
Lipid raft
Cholesterol-rich, less fluid membrane patch.
ATPase pump
ATP-driven active transporter.
Phagosome
Vacuole holding a swallowed particle.
Transcytosis
Vesicle transport across a cell.
Ligand
Signal molecule binding a receptor.
Target cell
Cell with the receptor for a signal.
