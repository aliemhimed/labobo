Mitochondria and Cytoplasmic Inclusions

Mitochondrial structure and ATP synthesis, their role in apoptosis, how they divide and why they look like bacteria; then the inclusions you see in electron micrographs: glycogen granules, lipid droplets, pigments and secretory granules. Built from the mitochondria slides of the Lecture 5 deck and the “Organelles” image set.

Labobo topic: Histo Lec 6: Mitochondria & Inclusions

In 60 seconds

  • Mitochondria make most ATP. Glucose → pyruvate in the cytoplasm; pyruvate is oxidised in the mitochondrion.
  • Two membranes: outer with pores (lets pyruvate in); inner folded into cristae (more surface) with the electron transport chain and ATP synthase.
  • Electron transport pumps protons into the intermembrane space; protons flow back through ATP synthase, which rotates and makes ATP (> 100 ATP/s): oxidative phosphorylation.
  • Release of cytochrome c triggers apoptosis.
  • They grow and divide by fission; have their own DNA and ribosomes, but most proteins come from nuclear genes → endosymbiont (aerobic bacterium) origin.
  • Inclusions = stored, non-living material: glycogen (dense granules/rosettes, liver and muscle), lipid droplets (round, no membrane), pigments (lipofuscin, melanin), secretory granules.

Learning objectives

  • describe the membranes and compartments of a mitochondrion;
  • explain how the proton gradient drives ATP synthase;
  • state the role of mitochondria in apoptosis and the evidence for endosymbiosis;
  • recognise the main cytoplasmic inclusions in electron micrographs.

Mitochondrial structure

  • Membrane-bound organelles that make ATP, the cell’s main energy currency, by aerobic respiration.
  • Glucose is split to pyruvate in the cytoplasm (glycolysis); pyruvate is oxidised further in the mitochondria.
  • In TEM there are two membranes:
    • outer membrane: has pores (porins) that let small molecules such as pyruvate through;
    • inner membrane: folded into cristae, which greatly increase its surface area.
  • Compartments: intermembrane space (between the membranes) and matrix (inside the inner membrane; holds DNA, ribosomes and citric acid cycle enzymes).
  • Cells with high energy needs (heart muscle, kidney tubules) have many mitochondria with many cristae.

Oxidative phosphorylation

  • The inner membrane is specialised for energy production. It holds the electron transport chain; the citric acid cycle supplies its electrons (NADH, FADH2).
  • Electrons pass along a chain of enzyme complexes, which pump protons (H⁺) from the matrix into the intermembrane space.
  • This builds an electrochemical gradient across the inner membrane, which powers ATP synthesis.
  • This process makes most of the cell’s ATP.

ATP synthase

  1. ATP synthase complexes sit in the inner membrane with a proton channel.
  2. Protons flow back down their gradient into the matrix through the channel.
  3. The flow makes part of the enzyme rotate: electrical energy → mechanical motion.
  4. The rotation drives ADP + Pi → ATP.

Very efficient: each synthase makes more than 100 ATP per second.

Apoptosis, division and origin

  • Under stress, mitochondria release cytochrome c (an electron carrier) into the cytosol, which triggers apoptosis (programmed cell death).
  • Mitochondria grow and divide (fission). At cell division each daughter cell gets about half of them.
  • They are partly autonomous: own DNA (circular), ribosomes and protein-making machinery.
  • But most mitochondrial proteins are coded by nuclear DNA, made in the cytoplasm and imported.
  • These bacteria-like features support the endosymbiotic theory: mitochondria came from an aerobic prokaryote living inside an ancestral eukaryotic cell.

Cytoplasmic inclusions

Inclusions are stored or accumulated materials, not organelles with a working machinery. Many have no membrane.

Watch out

Watch out: where the citric acid cycle happens

The slide says the inner membrane houses enzymes of the citric acid cycle. Most citric acid cycle enzymes are in the matrix; only succinate dehydrogenase sits in the inner membrane (it is also Complex II). The electron transport chain and ATP synthase are in the inner membrane.

Exam traps

Exam trap
  • Protons are pumped into the intermembrane space, then flow back into the matrix through ATP synthase.
  • Cristae belong to the inner membrane.
  • Cytochrome c release → apoptosis.
  • Most mitochondrial proteins are nuclear-encoded.
  • Lipid droplets and glycogen have no membrane.

Key terms

Cristae
Folds of the inner mitochondrial membrane.
Matrix
Space inside the inner membrane.
Oxidative phosphorylation
ATP synthesis driven by electron transport.
ATP synthase
Rotary enzyme making ATP from the proton flow.
Cytochrome c
Electron carrier; its release starts apoptosis.
Fission
Division of a mitochondrion.
Endosymbiosis
Origin of mitochondria from engulfed bacteria.
Inclusion
Stored or accumulated cytoplasmic material.
