Basic Properties of Cells: Prokaryotes and Eukaryotes

How cells were discovered, the cell theory, how big cells and their parts are, the basic properties every cell shares, and the fundamental split between prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells.

Labobo topic: MB Lec 1: Cells, Prokaryote vs Eukaryote

In 60 seconds

  • Hooke named “cells” (cork); Leeuwenhoek saw protists (“animalcules”) and bacteria from his teeth.
  • Cell theory (1838, Schleiden and Schwann; Virchow added that cells come only from pre-existing cells).
  • 1 μm = 10⁻⁶ m; 1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m; 1 Å = 0.1 nm = 10⁻¹⁰ m.
  • DNA ≈ 2 nm wide; ribosomes, microtubules, microfilaments 5–25 nm; nucleus 5–10 μm; mitochondrion ≈ 2 μm; prokaryote 1–5 μm; eukaryote 10–30 μm.
  • Cells are organised, have a genetic program, reproduce, use energy, run enzyme reactions, move, respond, self-regulate and evolve.
  • DNA replication: fewer than 1 error per 10 million nucleotides.
  • Eukaryotes have a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles; prokaryotes don’t.

Learning objectives

Karp’s Cell and Molecular Biology (8th ed., 2016) and Alberts’ Essential Cell Biology (5th ed., 2019). By the end you can:

  • describe the discovery of cells and the cell theory;
  • use the units of cell measurement and the sizes of cell parts;
  • list and explain the basic properties of cells;
  • compare prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells.

The cell theory

  1. All organisms are made of one or more cells.
  2. The cell is the structural unit of life.
  3. Cells arise only by division of a pre-existing cell.

What makes something alive

Life is easy to recognise but hard to define. Living things:

  1. are highly organised compared with non-living objects;
  2. show homeostasis (a stable internal environment);
  3. reproduce;
  4. grow and develop from simple beginnings;
  5. take energy and matter from the environment and transform them;
  6. respond to stimuli;
  7. show adaptation to their environment.

Basic properties of cells

PropertyWhat it means
Complex and organisedRemarkably precise: DNA replication makes fewer than 1 error in 10 million nucleotides, and most are repaired. Levels: atoms → molecules → polymers → organelles → cells. Organelles have consistent shapes and positions; proteins fold into exact 3-D shapes
Genetic programInformation is packaged in chromosomes; genes hold the recipes for structures and activities. Changes (mutations) create variation and drive evolution
Reproduce themselvesBy division: a mother cell gives two daughter cells with complete, equal genetic information
Acquire and use energyPhotosynthesis turns light into chemical energy (sucrose, starch). In humans, glucose in the blood is the main fuel; its energy is captured as ATP, the ready-to-use energy currency
Carry out chemical reactionsAll need enzymes; metabolism = the sum of all these reactions
Mechanical activitiesTransport, contraction, assembly of structures, cell movement, driven by motor proteins: myosin, dyneins (cilia, flagella), kinesins (mitosis, meiosis)
Respond to stimuliObvious in protists; in multicellular organisms through surface receptors for hormones, growth factors and extracellular materials
Self-regulateRobust: feedback circuits return the cell to its proper state after disturbance
EvolveThe first cell’s origin is unknown, but living cells share a genetic code, plasma membrane and ribosomes. Evolution continues: drug resistance in bacteria, new COVID-19 variants

Two basic types of cells

  • All organisms fall into two groups by cell structure; the key is the nucleus (and membrane-bound organelles).
  • Eukaryotes: cells with a nucleus.
  • Prokaryotes: cells without a nucleus.

The slide groups living things into six kingdoms: bacteria, protists, fungi, plants, animals, and (with question marks) viruses, which are non-cellular.

Prokaryotes vs eukaryotes

A bacterial (prokaryotic) cell often has a tough cell wall around its plasma membrane, enclosing a single compartment with the cytoplasm and a circular DNA. The fuller comparison (Karp):

FeatureProkaryoticEukaryotic
NucleusNone; DNA in a nucleoidMembrane-bound nucleus
DNAUsually one circular chromosome, few proteinsSeveral linear chromosomes with histones
Membrane organellesNoneER, Golgi, mitochondria, lysosomes, (chloroplasts)
Size1–5 μm10–30 μm
RibosomesSmaller (70S)Larger (80S)

Watch out

Watch out: viruses are not a kingdom

Slide 20 lists viruses as a sixth kingdom with question marks. Viruses are not cells and are not placed in any kingdom. The usual six kingdoms are archaebacteria, eubacteria, protists, fungi, plants and animals.

Watch out: ATP is not a store

Slide 15 says glucose energy is “stored in the form of ATP”. ATP is the immediate energy currency, used within seconds. Energy is stored as glycogen and fat.

Watch out: “first microscope”

Leeuwenhoek made the best simple (single-lens) microscopes of his time; compound microscopes existed earlier (Hooke used one). The exam point is what he saw: protists and bacteria.

Exam traps

Exam trap
  • Hooke named cells; Leeuwenhoek saw bacteria.
  • “Cells only from cells” is Virchow, not Schleiden or Schwann.
  • 1 Å = 0.1 nm, not 10 nm.
  • Kinesins and dyneins move on microtubules; myosin on actin.
  • The defining difference is the nucleus.

Key terms

Cell theory
All life is cellular; cells come from cells.
Prokaryote
Cell without a nucleus.
Eukaryote
Cell with a nucleus and membrane organelles.
Nucleoid
DNA region of a prokaryote.
Homeostasis
Maintaining a stable internal environment.
Metabolism
All enzyme reactions of a cell.
Motor protein
Protein that moves along filaments (myosin, kinesin, dynein).
Receptor
Protein that binds a signal and triggers a response.
Ångström
0.1 nm.
