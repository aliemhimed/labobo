In 60 seconds

  • Genetics = the science of heredity and variation. DNA → gene → RNA/protein → cell function.
  • Human genome: about 3.2 billion bp, 46 chromosomes in 23 pairs (22 autosome pairs + XX or XY), 20,000–25,000 protein-coding genes = only 1–2% of DNA.
  • Allele = a version of a gene; locus = its position; genotype = alleles carried; phenotype = observable trait (genotype + environment).
  • Mendel’s postulates: (1) unit factors in pairs, (2) dominance/recessiveness, (3) segregation → monohybrid ratios 1 : 2 : 1 and 3 : 1.

  • Sickle cell: HBB gene, A→T, GAG (Glu) → GTG (Val) → HbS.
  • Germ-line mutations are inherited; somatic mutations are not (but can cause cancer).

What genetics is

Genetics is the biological science of heredity and variation. It asks how traits pass from parents to offspring and why individuals of the same species differ.

  • It explains how information in DNA determines the structure, function and behaviour of living things: how it is stored, copied, expressed and changed.
  • It links molecules → cells → organisms → populations → health and disease.
  • Hereditary information is organised into genes: DNA sequences that code for proteins or functional RNAs. All of it together is the genome.
  • The cascade DNA → gene → RNA/protein → cell function is the framework for understanding traits, normal physiology and disease.

A brief history

  1. Ancient societies recognised heredity through plant and animal breeding.
  2. Greek philosophers proposed speculative theories (humors, the homunculus).
  3. Gregor Mendel (19th century; 1822–1884, an Augustinian monk, the “father of genetics”): pea-plant experiments showed discrete hereditary units.
  4. Early 20th century: chromosomes identified as the carriers of genes.
  5. 1953: the structure of DNA discovered → genetics becomes a molecular science.

Why genetics matters in medicine

Disease typeCauseExamples
Single-gene disordersA specific DNA change in one geneCystic fibrosis, Duchenne muscular dystrophy, sickle-cell anemia
Complex diseasesMany genes interacting with the environmentDiabetes, cardiovascular disease

Genetics supports diagnosis, risk prediction, screening and counselling, and personalised medicine (choosing a drug and dose by genetic profile). Genome sequencing, genetic testing and gene editing bring new treatments, along with ethical questions.

The human genome and chromosomes

  • About 3.2 billion DNA base pairs (one slide rounds to 3 billion).
  • Organised into 46 chromosomes = 23 pairs: 22 pairs of autosomes (same in both sexes) and 1 pair of sex chromosomes (XX female, XY male).
  • One chromosome of each pair comes from each parent, so we inherit half our genetic material from the mother and half from the father.
  • About 20,000–25,000 protein-coding genes, only 1–2% of the DNA. The rest: regulatory elements, non-coding RNAs, and DNA that organises and stabilises chromosomes.

Chromosomes carry heredity

  • A chromosome is one continuous DNA double helix wrapped around histones.
  • During cell division they condense into rod-like bodies visible under the microscope.
  • Each holds hundreds to thousands of genes, each at a fixed locus.
  • Because we are diploid, every somatic cell carries two copies of each autosomal gene, one from each parent.

Mendel’s first three postulates

PostulateWhat it saysPea example
1. Unit factors in pairsEach trait is controlled by unit factors (genes) that occur in pairs, one from each parent; the two may be the same (homozygous) or different (heterozygous)Flower colour: PP, pp or Pp
2. Dominance/recessivenessWhen two different alleles are present, one (dominant) masks the other (recessive)Tall is dominant over dwarf: tall and heterozygous plants are both tall; only homozygous recessive plants are dwarf
3. SegregationThe two alleles separate during gamete formation, so each gamete gets one; fertilisation restores the pairPp makes P and p gametes in equal numbers

Postulate 2 explains why a recessive trait can disappear in one generation and reappear in the next.

Genetic variation

Genetic variation = differences in DNA sequence between individuals of a species. Humans share more than 99% of their DNA, yet the small differences explain traits (height, eye colour, metabolism), disease risk, and why people respond differently to the same drug.

Sources

SourceHow
MutationPermanent change in DNA; may be neutral, harmful or helpful
RecombinationHomologous chromosomes swap segments in meiosis (crossing-over) → new allele combinations
Independent assortmentEach gamete gets a random mix of maternal and paternal chromosomes

Together these make every person genetically unique, except identical twins.

Types

TypeWhat it isNote
SNP (single nucleotide polymorphism)Change in a single baseThe most common variation
Insertions and deletions (indels)Small additions or losses of basesMay affect gene function
STRs (short tandem repeats)Short sequences repeated in a rowHighly variable; used in forensics
CNVs (copy number variations)Large segments duplicated or deletedInfluence traits and disease risk

Genotype to phenotype: sickle-cell anemia

  1. Mutation in the HBB gene (β-globin): one base change A → T.
  2. Codon GAG (glutamic acid) becomes GTG (valine).
  3. Abnormal hemoglobin: HbS.
  4. At low oxygen HbS molecules stick together, distorting red cells into a sickle shape.
GenotypePhenotype
HbS/HbS (homozygote)Sickle-cell anemia: fragile cells → chronic anemia; blocked small vessels → severe pain, organ damage, stroke risk
HbA/HbS (heterozygote)Usually healthy (sickle-cell trait); mild symptoms under stress

One DNA change can therefore produce severe clinical disease.

Model organisms

Many discoveries come from non-human species, chosen because they:

  • grow and reproduce quickly;
  • are easy to manipulate in the laboratory;
  • share fundamental genetic processes with humans.

Example: genes involved in human retinitis pigmentosa (a complex retinal disease) are identical to Drosophila genes involved in retinal degeneration, so studying the fly helps explain the human disease. Other common models (from the textbook): E. coli, yeast (Saccharomyces), the worm C. elegans, the mouse and the plant Arabidopsis.

Watch out

Watch out: “tall-flower allele”

Slide 10 calls tallness a “tall-flower allele (P)”. Mendel’s tall/dwarf trait is plant height (stem length), a different gene from flower colour (purple/white). The idea being tested, dominance, is the same; don’t mix the two traits in one cross.

Exam traps

Exam trap
  • Only 1–2% of the genome codes for protein.
  • Phenotype depends on genotype and environment.
  • Somatic mutations are not passed to children.
  • A heterozygote Pp makes P and p gametes in equal numbers (segregation).
  • The most common variation is the SNP; STRs are used in forensics.
  • Sickle cell is Glu → Val (GAG → GTG).

Key terms

Genetics
Science of heredity and variation.
Gene
DNA segment coding for a protein or functional RNA.
Allele
Alternative form of a gene.
Locus
Fixed position of a gene on a chromosome.
Genome
All the genetic material of an organism.
Autosome
Any chromosome that is not a sex chromosome.
Diploid / haploid
Two sets / one set of chromosomes.
Homozygous / heterozygous
Same / different alleles at a locus.
Dominant / recessive
Expressed in one copy / only in two copies.
Segregation
Separation of the two alleles into different gametes.
Monohybrid cross
Cross following one gene with two alleles.
Polymorphic
Having several common alleles in a population.
SNP
Single nucleotide polymorphism.
CNV
Copy number variation of a large DNA segment.
Model organism
Species studied because it shares genetic processes with humans and is easy to work with.
