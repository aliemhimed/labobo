Chromosome Mapping and Linkage

How geneticists find where genes sit on a chromosome without looking at it: crossing over, map units, the three-point test cross, DNA markers and genome databases, plus sister chromatid exchange and Bloom syndrome.

In 60 seconds

  • Genes on the same chromosome are linked: they tend to be inherited together.
  • Crossing over in prophase I makes recombinant gametes; the farther apart two genes, the more often it happens.
  • 1 map unit (m.u.) = 1 centiMorgan (cM) = 1% recombination.

Linked genes and mapping

  • A chromosome is like a long necklace with genes as beads in a line. Some are close together, some far apart.
  • Genes on the same chromosome are linked: they tend to be inherited together, so they do not assort independently.
  • Chromosome mapping = finding where genes are on a chromosome and how far apart they are.
  • It is done not with a microscope, but by measuring how often genes are separated during meiosis.

Crossing over and map units

  1. In prophase I of meiosis, homologous chromosomes pair up.
  2. They can swap segments of DNA: crossing over.
  3. Gametes with new combinations of alleles are recombinant; those with the original combinations are parental (non-recombinant).
Genes areCrossing over between themInherited
Very closeRareUsually together
Far apartFrequentOften separated

So the frequency of recombination measures distance, in map units (m.u.) or centiMorgans (cM): 1 m.u. = 1 cM = 1% recombinant gametes.

Worked example

  • 10% of gametes are recombinant for genes A and B → A and B are 10 cM apart.
  • B and C show 20% recombination → 20 cM apart, farther than A and B.

From the textbook: recombination between two genes can never exceed 50%; genes that far apart (or on different chromosomes) behave as if unlinked.

Recombination frequency formula

RF (%) = recombinant offspring ÷ total offspring × 100. The answer in % is the distance in cM.

Gene order: the three-point test cross

To find the order of three genes (A, B, C), geneticists use a three-point test cross: a triple heterozygote crossed with a triple homozygous recessive, so each offspring’s phenotype shows exactly which alleles came from the heterozygous parent.

  1. Sort the 8 offspring classes into 4 pairs of reciprocal types.
  2. The most frequent pair = parental (non-crossover).
  3. The least frequent pair = double crossovers.
  4. Compare parental and double-crossover types: the gene whose allele has switched is the middle gene.
  5. Distance between each pair = (single crossovers in that region + double crossovers) ÷ total × 100.

Example

Parental types: A B C and a b c. Double crossovers: A b C and a B c. Only B has swapped relative to its neighbours → B is in the middle: order A–B–C.

DNA markers and genome databases

In the past, mapping relied on visible traits (eye colour, body colour). Today DNA itself is used.

  • A DNA marker is a known, short DNA sequence that varies between people: a “signpost” in the genome. It usually doesn’t cause a trait; it shows where you are.
MarkerWhat it is
RFLPRestriction fragment length polymorphism: different fragment sizes when DNA is cut by a restriction enzyme
STR / microsatelliteShort repeated sequences; repeat number varies
SNPOne base differs (e.g. A → G)

Compare markers in people with and without a disease: a marker that is always inherited with the disease lies near the disease gene. This is how the genes for cystic fibrosis and Huntington disease were found.

Annotated computer databases

  • Examples: NCBI Genome Data Viewer, Ensembl, UCSC Genome Browser (and OMIM for diseases).
  • They show a gene’s exact position (in base pairs), its function, known variants, and links to diseases and studies.
  • Used to locate genes, compare species and study how variants cause disease.

Sister chromatid exchange and Bloom syndrome

  • Before mitosis each chromosome is copied into two identical sister chromatids, joined at the centromere.
  • Sister chromatids can still swap pieces: sister chromatid exchange (SCE).
  • SCEs happen in S and G2 phase (during copying or repair) and are seen in mitosis, especially metaphase.
  • Detected by staining the two chromatids differently with BrdU (bromodeoxyuridine) (“harlequin” chromosomes).

Why SCEs matter

  • Sister chromatids are identical, so SCEs don’t change genetic information.
  • But they show how busy DNA repair is. Many SCEs suggest unstable DNA or faulty repair.
In the clinic: Bloom syndrome

Mutation in the BLM gene on chromosome 15, which encodes a DNA helicase (best known for its role in DNA replication). Cells show very high SCE rates → chromosomal instability → higher cancer risk and developmental problems.

Exam traps

Exam trap
  • Crossing over happens in prophase I of meiosis.
  • The middle gene is found by comparing parental with double-crossover classes.
  • SCEs do not create new allele combinations.
  • BLM encodes a helicase.
  • A marker linked to a disease need not cause the disease.

Key terms

Linkage
Tendency of genes on one chromosome to be inherited together.
Crossing over
Exchange of segments between homologous chromosomes in meiosis.
Recombinant
Gamete or offspring with a new allele combination.
Map unit / centiMorgan
Distance giving 1% recombination.
Test cross
Cross to a homozygous recessive to reveal gametes of the other parent.
Three-point test cross
Test cross with three genes to find their order and distances.
DNA marker
Variable DNA sequence used as a genomic signpost.
RFLP
Restriction fragment length polymorphism.
Sister chromatids
Two identical copies of a chromosome joined at the centromere.
SCE
Sister chromatid exchange.
Bloom syndrome
BLM helicase defect with high SCE and cancer risk.
