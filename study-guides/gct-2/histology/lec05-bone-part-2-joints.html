Bone II: Remodelling, Ossification, Repair and Joints

How bone is remodelled and formed (intramembranous and endochondral ossification), the five zones of the growth plate, fracture repair, bone’s role in calcium balance, and the structure of joints, with rickets, osteomalacia, osteogenesis imperfecta, herniated disc and rheumatoid arthritis.

Labobo topic: Histo Lec 5: Bone (Part 2) & Joints

In 60 seconds

  • Remodelling: osteoclasts cut tunnels, osteoblasts fill them as new osteons; 5–10% of adult bone turns over each year.
  • Intramembranous ossification: osteoblasts directly from mesenchyme (skull, jaws, clavicle, scapula). Endochondral: a hyaline cartilage model is replaced (most bones).
  • Growth plate zones, epiphysis → diaphysis: reserve, proliferation, hypertrophy (type X collagen), calcified cartilage, ossification. Plates close by about age 20.
  • Fracture repair: hematoma → procallus (fibrocartilage) → hard callus (woven bone) → remodelling.
  • Bone holds 99% of body calcium. PTH raises blood Ca²⁺ (via osteoblast RANKL → osteoclasts); calcitonin lowers it (acts directly on osteoclasts).
  • Joints: synarthroses (synostosis, syndesmosis, symphysis) and diarthroses (synovial joints with type A and type B synovial cells).

Bone remodelling

Remodelling continues throughout life. In compact bone, osteoclasts remove old bone and cut tunnel-like cavities; osteoprogenitor cells from endosteum or periosteum and capillary loops invade; osteoblasts line the tunnel and secrete osteoid in cycles, forming a new osteon with trapped osteocytes. In healthy adults 5–10% of bone turns over each year; in young children turnover can be up to 200 times faster.

In the clinic: tetracycline labelling

Tetracycline is fluorescent and binds newly mineralising osteoid. Given twice, 11–14 days apart, then a bone biopsy (undecalcified), it shows two fluorescent lines whose distance gives the rate of appositional growth. Useful in osteomalacia (impaired mineralisation) and osteitis fibrosa cystica (excess osteoclast activity with fibrous degeneration).

Osteogenesis

IntramembranousEndochondral
Starts fromCondensed mesenchyme: osteoblasts differentiate directlyA hyaline cartilage model that is eroded and invaded
BonesMost flat bones: skull, jaws, scapula, clavicleMost bones, especially long bones

In both, woven bone comes first and is soon replaced by lamellar bone; woven bone, resorbing bone and lamellar bone lie side by side in any growing bone.

In the clinic: osteogenesis imperfecta

“Brittle bone disease”: congenital disorders in which osteoblasts make too little or defective type I collagen. Without normal collagen to reinforce the mineral, bones are very fragile.

Intramembranous ossification

  1. In condensed sheets (“membranes”) of mesenchyme, ossification centres form: osteoprogenitor cells proliferate and make layers of osteoblasts around developing capillaries.
  2. Osteoid calcifies into small irregular areas of woven bone with osteocytes.
  3. Centres enlarge and fuse; woven bone is replaced by compact plates around cancellous bone with marrow.
  4. Mesenchyme that does not ossify becomes periosteum and endosteum.

In flat skull bones, inner and outer compact plates form while the middle stays cancellous (diploë). The fontanelles of newborns are unossified membrane.

Endochondral ossification

  1. A hyaline cartilage model of the bone forms.
  2. Late in the first trimester, osteoblasts in the perichondrium (becoming periosteum) make a bone collar around the middle of the diaphysis.
  3. The collar blocks diffusion, so chondrocytes inside hypertrophy, compress the matrix and calcify it (osteocalcin, alkaline phosphatase), then die.
  4. Vessels from the periosteum penetrate the collar, bringing osteoprogenitor cells: the primary ossification centre in the diaphysis. Osteoblasts make woven bone on calcified cartilage remnants (remnants basophilic, new bone acidophilic).
  5. Around birth, secondary ossification centres form in the epiphyses.
  6. Two cartilage regions remain: articular cartilage (lifelong) and the epiphyseal plate (growth in length). The plates close at different times, all by about age 20; their closure gives a person’s “bone age”.

The epiphyseal growth plate

Five overlapping zones, from the epiphysis toward the diaphysis:

#ZoneWhat happens
1Reserve (resting) cartilageTypical hyaline cartilage
2ProliferationChondrocytes divide, enlarge, secrete type II collagen and proteoglycans, and line up in columns along the bone’s axis
3HypertrophySwollen, terminally differentiated chondrocytes compress matrix into spicules and secrete type X collagen, which limits diffusion and with growth factors attracts vessels
4Calcified cartilageChondrocytes about to die release matrix vesicles and osteocalcin: hydroxyapatite forms
5OssificationCapillaries and osteoprogenitor cells invade empty lacunae (forming marrow cavity); osteoblasts lay osteoid on calcified cartilage spicules → woven bone → lamellar bone
In the clinic: rickets and osteomalacia

In children, calcium deficiency (low dietary calcium or lack of vitamin D, needed for intestinal Ca²⁺ absorption) causes rickets: matrix fails to calcify, the growth plate is distorted by body weight, and bones grow slowly and deform (bowed legs, knock knees). In adults the same deficiency causes osteomalacia: poor calcification of new bone and partial decalcification of old bone.

Growth in width

Long bones grow in circumference not by endochondral ossification but by appositional (radial) growth: osteoblasts from periosteal osteoprogenitor cells add bone beneath the periosteum, starting with the bone collar. At the same time endosteal osteoclasts remove bone around the marrow cavity, which enlarges, so the bone gets wider without becoming much heavier.

Fracture repair

With osteoprogenitor cells in periosteum, endosteum and marrow, and a rich blood supply, bone repairs well, especially if the periosteum is preserved.

  1. Torn vessels bleed and clot: a fracture hematoma. Nearby bone cells die; macrophages clear the clot and debris, osteoclasts resorb dead matrix.
  2. A soft, fibrocartilage-like procallus replaces the hematoma; the periosteum re-forms over it.
  3. Vessels and osteoblasts invade; over weeks the fibrocartilage is replaced by woven bone, forming a hard callus.
  4. The woven bone is remodelled into compact and cancellous bone continuous with the uninjured bone, and normal vessels return.

Metabolic role of bone

Ca²⁺ is needed for many enzymes, adhesion, cytoskeletal movement, exocytosis and membrane permeability. The skeleton holds 99% of body calcium as hydroxyapatite; blood calcium stays at about 9–10 mg/dL through constant exchange, mainly from cancellous bone.

HormoneSourceEffect
Parathyroid hormone (PTH)Parathyroid glandsRaises blood Ca²⁺: stimulates osteoclasts and osteocytes to resorb. Acts indirectly on osteoclasts: PTH receptors are on osteoblasts, which release RANKL
CalcitoninThyroid glandLowers blood Ca²⁺: acts directly on osteoclasts to slow resorption
Growth hormoneAnterior pituitaryMakes the liver produce IGF-1 (somatomedin), which drives the epiphyseal cartilage
In the clinic: growth hormone disorders

Too little GH while growing: pituitary dwarfism. Too much while growing: gigantism. Too much in adults (no growth plates): bones thicken by periosteal growth, acromegaly.

Synarthroses and the intervertebral disc

Synarthroses allow little or no movement; they are fibrous or cartilaginous.

TypeJoined byExamples
SynostosisBone to bone; no movementSkull bones in older adults (sutures of dense CT in younger people)
SyndesmosisDense connective tissue onlyInterosseous ligament of the inferior tibiofibular joint; posterior sacroiliac joint
SymphysisThick pad of fibrocartilage between thin articular cartilages; all in the midlineIntervertebral discs, pubic symphysis

Intervertebral disc

  • Annulus fibrosus: concentric fibrocartilage laminae with collagen bundles at right angles in adjacent layers; very tough against pressure and torsion.
  • Nucleus pulposus: central gel rich in hyaluronan and type II collagen, with vacuolated cells from the notochord, the only notochord cells that persist after birth. Large in children, it shrinks with age and is partly replaced by fibrocartilage. Acts as a shock absorber.
In the clinic: herniated disc

Degeneration of the annulus lets the nucleus pulposus shift: a slipped or herniated disc, usually posterior (fewer collagen bundles there). Pressure on nerves causes severe pain in their territory, usually the lower lumbar region.

Diarthroses (synovial joints)

Diarthroses (elbow, knee, knuckles) permit free movement. Ligaments and a capsule of dense CT keep the bones aligned; the capsule encloses a sealed joint cavity of viscous synovial fluid; bone ends are covered with articular cartilage.

The cavity is lined not by epithelium but by the synovial membrane, a specialised CT with folds and villi, well vascularised with fenestrated capillaries. Its surface cells have no basement membrane or junctional complexes.

CellOriginRole
Type A (macrophage-like)Blood monocytes; about 25% of lining cellsRemove wear debris; regulate joint inflammation
Type B (fibroblastic)Local fibroblastsMake abundant hyaluronan and some proteoglycans, which with water from capillaries form synovial fluid: lubrication and nutrition of cartilage

Proteoglycan–hyaluronan complexes in articular cartilage act as a biomechanical spring: pressure squeezes water into the synovial fluid, release draws it back, exchanging O₂, CO₂ and metabolites with the chondrocytes.

In the clinic: rheumatoid arthritis

Chronic inflammation thickens the synovial membrane and makes macrophages release collagenases and other hydrolases that destroy the articular cartilage, until bones touch directly.

Ageing: biopsies of the iliac crest show a thinner periosteum (osteogenic layer replaced by fibrous tissue), fewer canals, empty osteocyte lacunae and thin trabeculae with fatty marrow; trabecular meshwork falls by about 45% after 65, so autologous bone grafts lose osteogenic power.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • The epiphyseal growth plate is hyaline cartilage; length growth stops when it closes.
  • Growth in width is appositional (periosteum), not endochondral.
  • Type X collagen is unique to hypertrophic chondrocytes.
  • The synovial membrane is not an epithelium (no basement membrane, no junctions).
  • All symphyses are in the midline.
  • The nucleus pulposus contains the only notochord cells left after birth.
  • In both ossification types, woven bone forms first.
