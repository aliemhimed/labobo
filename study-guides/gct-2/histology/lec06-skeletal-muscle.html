Skeletal Muscle

The three muscle types, then skeletal muscle in detail: how fibres develop, the connective tissue wrappings, the sarcomere and its filaments, the T-tubule and sarcoplasmic reticulum, how contraction happens, motor units, proprioceptors and fibre types, with Duchenne muscular dystrophy.

Labobo topic: Histo Lec 6: Skeletal Muscle

In 60 seconds

  • Skeletal muscle fibres are long, cylindrical, multinucleated (fused myoblasts), with peripheral nuclei and cross-striations; satellite cells repair them.
  • Wrappings: epimysium (whole muscle), perimysium (fascicle), endomysium (each fibre, reticular fibres + capillaries).
  • Sarcomere = Z disc to Z disc (~2.5 μm): A band (myosin, constant), I band (actin only), H zone, M line. Titin anchors myosin; α-actinin anchors actin.
  • Contraction: ACh at the motor end plate → impulse along T-tubules → Ca²⁺ from terminal cisternae (triad) → Ca²⁺ binds troponin C → tropomyosin moves → crossbridge cycling with ATP. I band and H zone shorten; A band does not.
  • Fibre types: I slow oxidative, IIa fast oxidative-glycolytic, IIb fast glycolytic.

Three types of muscle

Muscle cells are specialised for contractility, driven by actin and myosin. Almost all come from mesoderm and differentiate by lengthening and making myofibrillar proteins.

SkeletalCardiacSmooth
CellsVery long, multinucleatedElongated, branched, joined by intercalated discsFusiform
StriationsYesYesNo
ContractionQuick, forceful, voluntaryInvoluntary, vigorous, rhythmicSlow, involuntary

Special names: cytoplasm = sarcoplasm; smooth ER = sarcoplasmic reticulum; cell membrane + external lamina = sarcolemma.

In the clinic: hypertrophy and hyperplasia

Exercise enlarges skeletal muscle by forming new myofibrils and thickening fibres: hypertrophy (bigger cells). Hyperplasia (more cells) happens readily in smooth muscle, whose cells can still divide.

Skeletal muscle fibres and their development

  • Fibres are long cylindrical multinucleated cells, 10–100 μm across.
  • Mesenchymal myoblasts align and fuse into multinucleated myotubes, which make myofilament proteins, show striations, and push their nuclei to the edge.
  • Elongated nuclei just under the sarcolemma: a feature unique to skeletal muscle.
  • Some myoblasts do not fuse: satellite cells sit on the fibre surface inside the external lamina, and proliferate to make new fibres after injury.

Organisation of a muscle

LayerSurroundsMade of
EpimysiumThe whole muscleDense irregular CT; septa carry larger nerves, vessels, lymphatics inward
PerimysiumEach fascicle (a functional bundle of fibres)Thin CT; nerves, vessels and lymphatics enter here
EndomysiumEach muscle fibre (outside its external lamina)Delicate reticular fibres, few fibroblasts, a rich capillary network and nerve fibres

All three contain types I and III collagen, which transmit force (single fibres seldom run the whole muscle length). They and the deep fascia are continuous with the tendon at myotendinous junctions, where tendon collagen inserts among infoldings of the sarcolemma.

Myofibrils and the sarcomere

The sarcoplasm is filled with myofibrils (1–2 μm wide) running along the fibre, with mitochondria and SR between them. Sarcomeres of neighbouring myofibrils line up, so the whole fibre shows cross-striations.

BandMade ofNotes
A band (dark)Thick (myosin) filaments, overlapping thin ones at its endsAnisotropic (birefringent in polarised light)
I band (light)Thin (actin) filaments onlyIsotropic; bisected by the Z disc
H zoneThick filaments only (no overlap)Centre of the A band
M lineProteins linking thick filamentsMiddle of the H zone

The sarcomere, from Z disc to Z disc, is about 2.5 μm long at rest and is the repeating contractile unit. Thin filaments are anchored by α-actinin in the Z disc; thick filaments are tied to the M line and to the Z disc by the giant spring-like protein titin.

Thick and thin filaments

Thick filamentThin filament
ProteinMyosin (200–500 molecules)F-actin + tropomyosin + troponin
Size1.6 μm long, 15 nm wide1.0 μm long, 8 nm wide
WhereA bandI band and the ends of the A band
  • Myosin (~500 kDa): two heavy chains twisted into a tail, each with a globular head carrying the four light chains. Heads bind actin (crossbridges) and ATP (actomyosin ATPase). In a thick filament, heads point toward both ends.
  • Each G-actin has a myosin-binding site.
  • Tropomyosin: 40 nm coil of two chains in the groove between the actin strands.
  • Troponin: TnT attaches to tropomyosin, TnC binds Ca²⁺, TnI regulates actin–myosin interaction.

Sarcoplasmic reticulum and T-tubules

  • The sarcoplasmic reticulum (smooth ER) wraps each myofibril and stores Ca²⁺ with its pumps; voltage-gated channels release it when the fibre is depolarised.
  • Transverse (T) tubules are finger-like invaginations of the sarcolemma that penetrate deep and encircle each myofibril at the A–I band junctions, so the whole fibre contracts at once.
  • Each T-tubule with two expanded terminal cisternae of SR on either side forms a triad, where depolarisation triggers Ca²⁺ release.

Mechanism of contraction

  1. A nerve impulse releases acetylcholine into the synaptic cleft; it binds ACh receptors of the motor end plate, starting a muscle impulse in the sarcolemma.
  2. The impulse runs along the T-tubules; Ca²⁺ is released from the terminal cisternae.
  3. Ca²⁺ binds troponin, which changes shape and moves tropomyosin, exposing active sites on actin. Myosin heads attach: crossbridges.
  4. Heads pivot, pulling thin filaments toward the sarcomere centre. ATP binds and is split; heads detach and re-cock. The cycle repeats while Ca²⁺ stays bound.
  5. When impulses stop, Ca²⁺ is pumped back into the SR, tropomyosin re-covers the sites and filaments slide back.

In contraction the Z discs move closer; the I band and H zone shorten, the A band stays the same; titin is compressed and helps restore length. Fibres contract all or none.

In the clinic: rigor mortis

After death mitochondria stop making ATP; without ATP, myosin cannot detach, crossbridges stay locked and muscles become rigid.

Innervation and motor units

  • Myelinated motor nerves branch in the perimysium; unmyelinated terminal twigs pass through the endomysium. Schwann cells cover the contacts.
  • Each twig ends in a trough on the fibre: the neuromuscular junction (motor end plate). The terminal has mitochondria and vesicles of acetylcholine; across the synaptic cleft, the sarcolemma forms deep junctional folds with many ACh receptors.
  • A motor unit = one motor neuron + all the fibres it supplies. One axon to one fibre gives fine control (extraocular muscles); large muscles have axons supplying 100 or more fibres.
  • Force is graded by how many motor units fire, and their sizes, not by partial contraction of fibres.

In the clinic: Duchenne muscular dystrophy

Dystrophin is a large actin-binding protein just inside the sarcolemma that links the cytoskeleton to the ECM. Mutations (X-linked) weaken this link; contractions tear it, and fibres atrophy, with progressive weakness from early childhood.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • Only skeletal muscle has nuclei under the sarcolemma; cardiac and smooth have central nuclei.
  • In contraction the A band does not change.
  • T-tubules are at the A–I junction in skeletal muscle.
  • Ca²⁺ binds troponin C, not tropomyosin or myosin.
  • ATP is needed for myosin to detach; its absence causes rigor mortis.
  • Skeletal muscle grows by hypertrophy; satellite cells make new fibres only after injury.
  • The endomysium is mainly reticular fibres.

Key terms
