Connective Tissue I: Cells, Fibres and Ground Substance

What connective tissue is made of: its resident and wandering cells, the three kinds of fibres, the ground substance that binds water, and how interstitial fluid moves, with keloids, Marfan syndrome and edema.

Labobo topic: Histo Lec 1: Connective Tissue I

In 60 seconds

  • Connective tissue is mostly extracellular matrix (fibres + ground substance), not cells. All of it comes from mesenchyme (mesoderm).
  • Fibroblasts are the resident cells that make the matrix; macrophages, mast cells, plasma cells and other leukocytes come from the bone marrow.
  • Fibres: collagen type I (strong, eosinophilic bundles), reticular (type III, silver-black, PAS+), elastic (elastin on fibrillin microfibrils).
  • Ground substance: GAGs (hyaluronan the largest), proteoglycans, multiadhesive glycoproteins (laminin, fibronectin) binding integrins.
  • Hydrostatic pressure pushes fluid out of capillaries; colloid osmotic pressure pulls it back; the excess drains into lymphatics. Too much = edema.

  • compare collagen, reticular and elastic fibres;

What connective tissue is

Connective tissue forms a matrix that supports and connects other tissues; its interstitial fluid is the medium through which nutrients and wastes diffuse. Unlike epithelium, muscle and nerve, which are mostly cells, its main component is the extracellular matrix (ECM):

  • protein fibres: collagen and elastic;
  • ground substance: anionic, hydrophilic proteoglycans, glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) and multiadhesive glycoproteins (laminin, fibronectin), which bind integrins on cells.

The variety of connective tissues reflects different amounts and kinds of cells, fibres and ground substance.

Embryonic mesenchyme

All connective tissues come from mesenchyme, developing mainly from mesoderm. Mesenchymal cells are undifferentiated and spindle-shaped, with large euchromatic nuclei and prominent nucleoli (high synthetic activity), in a matrix rich in hyaluronan with very little collagen. Mesenchyme gives connective tissue proper, cartilage and bone, and stem cells for blood, vascular endothelium and muscle.

Fibroblasts

Fibroblasts are the key, most common cells of connective tissue proper. They arise locally from mesenchyme and are permanent residents. They make and maintain most of the ECM: collagen (the body’s most abundant protein), elastin, GAGs, proteoglycans and multiadhesive glycoproteins.

Adipocytes

Adipocytes (fat cells) are large mesenchyme-derived cells specialised for storing lipid as neutral fat, or less often for producing heat. Adipose connective tissue cushions and insulates the skin and organs and has major metabolic importance. (Adipose tissue types are covered in lecture 2.)

Macrophages and the mononuclear phagocyte system

Macrophages are highly phagocytic: they turn over protein fibres and remove apoptotic cells, debris and particles, and are abundant at sites of inflammation. Pathologists call them histiocytes.

  • Size 10–30 μm; eccentric, oval or kidney-shaped nucleus.
  • TEM: irregular surface with pleats, protrusions and indentations (pinocytosis, phagocytosis), well-developed Golgi and many lysosomes.
  • They derive from blood monocytes, which cross venule walls and mature into macrophages (larger, more protein synthesis, Golgi and lysosomes). Yolk-sac monocytes seed organs early in development.
  • Long-lived (months to years). In inflammation they increase by proliferation and by recruiting monocytes; they remove debris, secrete growth factors for repair, and present antigen to lymphocytes.

Mast cells

Mast cells are oval or irregular cells, 7–20 μm, filled with basophilic secretory granules that often hide the central nucleus. They lie in loose connective tissue, often near small blood vessels.

  • Granules show metachromasia: their sulfated GAGs turn basic dyes such as toluidine blue from blue to purple or red.
  • Granules are poorly preserved by common fixatives, so mast cells can be hard to see in routine slides.

They release, locally, molecules for inflammation, innate immunity and repair:

Plasma cells and leukocytes

Plasma cells are lymphocyte-derived, antibody-producing cells: large and ovoid, with basophilic cytoplasm rich in RER and a large pale Golgi beside the nucleus. The nucleus is round and eccentric, with peripheral heterochromatin clumps giving a “clock-face” look. Life span only 10–20 days; more numerous in infected tissue (e.g. inflamed lamina propria).

Other leukocytes are wandering cells: they leave venules between endothelial cells, a process that rises greatly in inflammation (the vascular and cellular response to injury or foreign substances). Chemical mediators from cells, ECM and plasma raise blood flow and vascular permeability, bring leukocytes in and activate macrophages. Most leukocytes act for hours or days and die by apoptosis; some lymphocytes and antigen-presenting cells return via blood or lymph to lymphoid organs.

Collagen

The three fibre types are collagen, reticular (both collagen family) and elastic (mainly elastin). Collagen is the body’s most abundant protein, about 30% of dry weight; 28 collagens are known, numbered in order of discovery. Collagens are very strong and resist shearing and tearing.

Network (sheet-forming)IVMade by epithelial cells; lattice in basal and external laminae

  • Procollagen α chains are made in the RER; three are aligned, joined by disulfide bonds at their C-termini, and folded into a triple helix, the defining feature of collagen.
  • Type I fibrils are 20–90 nm wide; subunits are staggered by 67 nm, giving cross-striations by EM. Fibrils → fibres (visible by light microscopy) → bundles. Large bundles look white (tendon, sclera).
  • Turnover: very slow in tendons and ligaments, fast in the periodontal ligament. Degradation starts with collagenases, members of the matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), many secreted by macrophages during repair.
In the clinic: keloid

A keloid is a swelling of abnormally large amounts of collagen in a skin scar (after piercing or surgery), most common in people of African descent. It can be disfiguring, and excision is almost always followed by recurrence.

Reticular fibres

Reticular fibres are mainly collagen type III, forming a delicate network (reticulum) that supports cells.

  • Seldom visible with H&E; stain black with silver salts (argyrophilic) and are PAS-positive, both because type III chains carry much sugar (up to 10% carbohydrate versus about 1% in other collagen fibres).
  • Found in the reticular lamina of basement membranes and around adipocytes, smooth muscle, nerve fibres and small vessels.
  • Stroma of the liver, endocrine glands, bone marrow, spleen and lymph nodes, where they support changing populations of proliferating and phagocytic cells.

Elastic fibres

Elastic fibres are thinner than type I collagen fibres and form sparse networks in organs that stretch or bend (lung stroma). In large arteries elastin forms fenestrated sheets, elastic lamellae. They stain poorly with H&E but darker than collagen with other stains.

  • Made of fibrillin (350 kDa), forming 10 nm microfibrils as a scaffold, embedded in a larger mass of cross-linked elastin (60 kDa), which gives the rubber-like property.
  • Both are secreted by fibroblasts (and smooth muscle in vessel walls); microfibrils form first, then elastin is deposited on them.
In the clinic: Marfan syndrome

Mutations in the fibrillin genes weaken tissues rich in elastic fibres. The aorta, with high pressure and many elastic components, develops aneurysms, which are life-threatening; patients also have elongated limb and finger bones.

Ground substance

Ground substance is highly hydrated and transparent. It lets small molecules diffuse and, being viscous, lubricates and blocks invaders. It has three classes of macromolecule.

Glycosaminoglycans (GAGs)

  • Long polymers of repeating disaccharides (usually a hexosamine and a uronic acid); also called mucopolysaccharides.
  • Hyaluronan (hyaluronic acid) is the largest and most common: glucosamine–glucuronate, not sulfated, made directly into the ECM by membrane hyaluronan synthase; binds much water, aiding diffusion and lubricating joints and organs.
  • All other GAGs are smaller, sulfated, bound to proteins and made in the Golgi. Their negative charge extends them and holds cations and water: space-filling, cushioning, lubrication.

Proteoglycans

A core protein with sulfated GAGs attached, made in the RER and Golgi; the GAGs often outweigh the protein. After secretion they bind hyaluronan through link proteins. Aggrecan (25,000 kDa) has about 50 keratan sulfate and 100 chondroitin sulfate chains. Proteoglycans (especially heparan sulfate) store growth factors, released when they are degraded during repair.

Multiadhesive glycoproteins and integrins

  • Laminin (200–400 kDa, trimer): binds integrins, type IV collagen and proteoglycans; essential for basal laminae.
  • Fibronectin (235–270 kDa dimer, mainly from fibroblasts): binds collagens and GAGs; forms fibrillar networks for cell adhesion and migration.
  • Integrins: transmembrane α/β heterodimers that bind specific sequences in laminin, fibronectin and collagens with low affinity, so cells can explore without becoming stuck.

Interstitial fluid and edema

Water in ground substance is interstitial fluid, ionically like plasma and holding small plasma proteins; up to one-third of the body’s plasma proteins are in it. Capillaries bring nutrients and take wastes to the liver and kidneys.

  • Hydrostatic pressure (from the heart) pushes water out at the arterial end.
  • Colloid osmotic pressure of plasma proteins such as albumin, which cannot leave, pulls water back in. Small ions and molecules are equal on both sides and cancel out.
  • Less water returns than leaves; the excess drains into lymphatic capillaries and back to the blood.
In the clinic: edema

Edema is excess interstitial fluid. In inflammation capillary walls become more permeable, so more fluid leaves and the tissue swells (pitting when pressed).

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • Fibroblasts are permanent residents from local mesenchyme; macrophages, mast cells and plasma cells come from the bone marrow.
  • Reticular fibres are type III collagen, not elastic or type I.
  • Type II collagen forms fibrils but no fibres or bundles; type IV forms sheets.
  • Hyaluronan is the only GAG that is not sulfated and not made in the Golgi.
  • Collagen breakdown starts with matrix metalloproteinases (collagenases).
  • Marfan syndrome is a fibrillin defect, not an elastin or collagen defect.
