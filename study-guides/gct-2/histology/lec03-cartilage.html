Cartilage

The avascular supporting tissue made by chondrocytes: its matrix, the perichondrium, the three types (hyaline, elastic, fibrocartilage), and how cartilage forms, grows and repairs, with osteoarthritis and cartilage tumours.

Labobo topic: Histo Lec 3: Cartilage

In 60 seconds

  • Cartilage = chondrocytes in lacunae + matrix of type II collagen, aggrecan and hyaluronan holding much water (shock absorber). No other cell types.
  • Avascular and without nerves: nutrients diffuse from the perichondrium (or synovial fluid for articular cartilage, which has none). Low metabolism, poor repair.
  • Hyaline: commonest; joints, respiratory passages, costal cartilage, epiphyseal plates, fetal skeleton. Elastic: + elastic fibres; ear, epiglottis. Fibrocartilage: + type I collagen bundles; intervertebral disc, pubic symphysis; no perichondrium.
  • Isogenous groups (up to 8 cells) come from one chondroblast; territorial matrix is GAG-rich and more basophilic.
  • Growth: interstitial (chondrocytes divide) and appositional (new chondroblasts from perichondrium).

What cartilage is

Cartilage is a tough supporting connective tissue whose ECM is rich in GAGs and proteoglycans interacting with collagen and elastic fibres. Its firm matrix bears stress without permanent distortion. It frames the respiratory tract, ears and nose, gives smooth lubricated surfaces in joints, and guides growth of long bones.

  • Chondrocytes make and maintain all of the matrix and sit in cavities called lacunae. Unlike connective tissue proper, there are no other cell types.
  • Properties come from electrostatic bonds between type II collagen, hyaluronan and the sulfated GAGs of proteoglycans. Water bound to the negative GAGs makes it semi-rigid and a shock absorber.
  • All cartilage is avascular and lacks nerves; chondrocytes get nutrients by diffusion from perichondrial capillaries and have low metabolic activity.
In the clinic: genetic cartilage defects

Many conditions with defective cartilage, joint deformities or short limbs come from recessive mutations in genes for type II collagen, the aggrecan core protein, the sulfate transporter and other proteins chondrocytes need.

Hyaline cartilage

Hyaline (“glassy”) cartilage is the most common type: homogeneous and semitransparent when fresh.

  • Articular surfaces of movable joints.
  • Walls of larger respiratory passages: nose, larynx, trachea, bronchi.
  • Ventral ends of ribs (costal cartilage).
  • Epiphyseal plates of long bones (longitudinal growth).
  • The embryo’s temporary skeleton, later replaced by bone.
In the clinic: osteoarthritis

A chronic condition of ageing with gradual loss or change of the articular hyaline cartilage, mostly in weight-bearing (knee, hip) or heavily used (wrist, fingers) joints. Wear fragments make macrophages secrete matrix metalloproteinases and other factors that worsen damage and cause pain and inflammation.

Hyaline matrix

  • Dry weight nearly 40% collagen, mostly type II (thin fibrils, barely visible), in a hydrated gel of proteoglycans and glycoproteins. Proteoglycans make the matrix basophilic.
  • Aggrecan (250 kDa core, about 150 chondroitin sulfate and keratan sulfate chains) is the main proteoglycan; hundreds bind hyaluronan through link proteins, and the complexes bind type II fibrils.
  • Water is 60–80% of fresh weight (about 75%).
  • Chondronectin, a multiadhesive glycoprotein like fibronectin, binds GAGs, collagen and integrins, attaching chondrocytes to the matrix.
  • Territorial matrix right around each chondrocyte is richer in GAGs (more basophilic); interterritorial matrix between groups is richer in collagen.

Chondrocytes

  • Cells occupy little of the mass. At the periphery, young chondroblasts are elliptical, long axis parallel to the surface; deeper, cells are round.
  • Deep cells form isogenous aggregates of up to 8 cells from one chondroblast; as they secrete matrix they are pushed into separate lacunae.
  • In slides they shrink and pull away from the lacuna; in life they fill it.
  • Growth and matrix synthesis are stimulated by growth hormone (somatotropin), acting indirectly through insulin-like growth factors (somatomedins) from the liver.
In the clinic: calcification and tumours

Hyaline cartilage, unlike other types, often calcifies with age, especially costal cartilage, resembling endochondral ossification. Cartilage cells give benign chondromas or slow-growing malignant chondrosarcomas, which seldom metastasise and are removed surgically.

Perichondrium

A sheath of dense connective tissue covering all hyaline cartilage except articular cartilage, essential for growth and maintenance. It carries the blood supply and a small nerve supply.

  • Outer layer: type I collagen and fibroblasts.
  • Inner layer: mesenchymal stem cells → chondroblasts → chondrocytes.

Articular cartilage has no perichondrium; it is nourished by diffusion from synovial fluid and is what erodes in arthritis.

Elastic cartilage

Like hyaline cartilage, but with an abundant network of elastic fibres as well as type II fibrils, giving a yellowish colour fresh and dark fibres with special stains. It is more flexible and has a perichondrium.

Found in the auricle, walls of the external auditory canal, auditory (Eustachian) tubes, epiglottis and upper respiratory tract.

Fibrocartilage

A mixture of hyaline cartilage and dense connective tissue: very tough yet cushioning. Found in intervertebral discs, attachments of some ligaments, the pubic symphysis (and knee menisci).

  • Chondrocytes lie singly or in aligned isogenous rows, with sparse matrix around them; between them are fibroblasts and dense type I collagen bundles for tensile strength. Both type I and type II collagen are present.
  • Few proteoglycans → matrix is acidophilic (pink).
  • No distinct perichondrium.
  • Intervertebral discs act as lubricated cushions and shock absorbers between vertebrae.

Formation, growth and repair

  1. Chondrogenesis starts in mesenchyme: cells retract their processes, round up, multiply and pack together.
  2. Chondroblasts (basophilic, RER-rich) secrete matrix that swells with water and separates them; differentiation runs from the centre outward; the superficial mesenchyme becomes the perichondrium.
  3. Chondroblasts divide within the matrix, forming isogenous groups surrounded by territorial matrix; in mature cartilage this division stops.
GrowthHowImportance
InterstitialMitosis of existing chondrocytes inside the cartilageLengthening of cartilage regions in long bones (epiphyseal plates)
AppositionalNew chondroblasts from perichondrial progenitors added at the surfaceMore important after birth

Repair is slow and often incomplete except in young children, because cartilage is avascular with a low metabolic rate. Perichondrial cells invade the damage, but often make a scar of dense connective tissue instead of cartilage. Articular cartilage, with no perichondrium, must replace worn cells and matrix from within.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • Articular cartilage and fibrocartilage have no perichondrium; elastic cartilage does.
  • The epiphyseal growth plate is hyaline cartilage.
  • The epiglottis is elastic; the larynx and trachea are mainly hyaline.
  • Cartilage has no blood vessels and no nerves; chondrocyte metabolism is slow.
  • Hyaline matrix is basophilic (proteoglycans); fibrocartilage matrix is acidophilic.
  • Growth hormone acts on cartilage indirectly, through liver somatomedins (IGFs).
  • Only hyaline cartilage commonly calcifies with age.

Key terms

Chondrocyte: cartilage cell in a lacuna · Chondroblast: young, dividing cartilage cell · Lacuna: matrix cavity holding a chondrocyte · Perichondrium: dense connective tissue sheath of cartilage · Type II collagen: thin fibrillar collagen of cartilage · Aggrecan: main cartilage proteoglycan · Link protein: binds proteoglycans to hyaluronan · Chondronectin: glycoprotein attaching chondrocytes to matrix · Territorial matrix: GAG-rich matrix around chondrocytes · Interterritorial matrix: collagen-richer matrix between groups · Isogenous aggregate: group of cells from one chondroblast · Somatomedins: liver IGFs stimulating chondrocytes · Chondrogenesis: formation of cartilage from mesenchyme · Interstitial growth: growth by internal cell division · Appositional growth: growth at the surface from the perichondrium · Osteoarthritis: degeneration of articular cartilage · Chondrosarcoma: slow-growing malignant cartilage tumour.
