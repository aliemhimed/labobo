Bone I: Cells, Matrix and Bone Types

The three bone cells (osteoblasts, osteocytes, osteoclasts), how matrix is laid down and mineralised, the periosteum and endosteum, compact and cancellous bone, and lamellar versus woven bone, with osteoporosis, osteopetrosis and bone tumours.

Labobo topic: Histo Lec 4: Bone (Part 1)

In 60 seconds

  • Osteoblasts (from mesenchymal stem cells) secrete osteoid (type I collagen, osteonectin, osteocalcin) and start mineralisation with matrix vesicles (alkaline phosphatase).
  • Osteocytes sit in lacunae, linked by processes in canaliculi with gap junctions; they maintain matrix and sense load (“mechanostat”).
  • Osteoclasts: giant multinucleated cells from fused monocytes (need M-CSF and RANKL from osteoblasts); resorb bone in Howship lacunae. Defective in osteopetrosis.
  • Matrix: ~50% inorganic (hydroxyapatite); organic part 90% type I collagen.
  • Compact bone (80%) and cancellous bone (20%); lamellar (mature, osteons, lamellae at 90°) and woven (first-formed, random collagen).
  • Osteon: 5–20 concentric lamellae around a central (Haversian) canal; Volkmann canals link osteons; interstitial lamellae are remnants of old osteons.

Functions and components of bone

Bone supports the body, protects organs (cranial and thoracic cavities), houses bone marrow, stores calcium, phosphate and other ions, and forms levers that turn muscle contraction into movement.

Bone is a specialised connective tissue of calcified matrix and three main cells:

  • Osteocytes in lacunae between matrix layers (lamellae), with processes in canaliculi;
  • Osteoblasts, which make the organic matrix;
  • Osteoclasts, giant multinucleated cells that resorb and remodel bone.

Metabolites cannot diffuse through calcified matrix, so osteocytes exchange with capillaries through canaliculi. Bone is softened in a decalcifying solution, or embedded in plastic, before sectioning.

Osteoblasts and mineralisation

  • From mesenchymal stem cells; make the organic matrix: type I collagen, proteoglycans and glycoproteins such as osteonectin; also control mineral deposition.
  • Active osteoblasts lie only on bone surfaces, bound by integrins, as a single layer of cuboidal cells joined by adherent and gap junctions. They are polarised, secreting toward the bone.
  • They lay down osteoid, uncalcified collagen-rich matrix between the cells and the old bone; calcium salts are then deposited (appositional growth).
  • When done, most undergo apoptosis; some become osteocytes, some flatten into bone lining cells.

How matrix is mineralised

  1. Osteocalcin (vitamin K-dependent) and glycoproteins bind and concentrate Ca²⁺.
  2. Osteoblasts release matrix vesicles rich in alkaline phosphatase, raising local PO₄³⁻.
  3. The vesicles are foci for hydroxyapatite crystals, the first visible step of calcification.
  4. Crystals grow and merge into a confluent mineral mass embedding collagen and proteoglycans.
In the clinic: bone tumours

Primary bone cancer is uncommon (about 0.5% of cancer deaths); osteosarcoma arises from osteoprogenitor cells. Bone is often the site of metastases, most often from breast, lung, prostate, kidney and thyroid.

Osteocytes

  • Osteoblasts trapped in their own matrix become osteocytes, one per lacuna. Their long dendritic processes become enclosed in canaliculi (250–300 nm).
  • Nutrients move through the interstitial fluid of the canaliculi; processes of neighbouring cells meet at gap junctions.
  • Usually the most abundant bone cells; less RER, smaller Golgi and more condensed chromatin than osteoblasts.
  • They maintain the matrix: when they die, the matrix is quickly resorbed. They release paracrine and endocrine factors regulating remodelling.
  • Their network is a “mechanostat” sensing load: resistance exercise increases bone density; inactivity or weightlessness (astronauts) reduces it.

Osteoclasts

  • Very large, motile, multinucleated cells formed by fusion of bone marrow-derived monocytes; essential for resorption in growth and remodelling.
  • Their development needs two osteoblast products: macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) and RANKL (receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand).
  • They lie in enzymatically etched pits called resorption (Howship) lacunae.
  • Controlled by local factors; osteoblasts activated by parathyroid hormone release factors that regulate osteoclast formation and activity.
In the clinic: osteopetrosis

A genetic disease of dense, heavy “marble bones”: osteoclasts lack ruffled borders and cannot resorb. Bones overgrow and thicken, often obliterating the marrow, causing anemia and loss of white cells. Most patients have mutations in osteoclast proton-ATPase pumps or chloride channels.

Bone matrix

PartContent
Inorganic (~50% of dry weight)Calcium hydroxyapatite mainly; also bicarbonate, citrate, Mg²⁺, K⁺, Na⁺ and non-crystalline calcium phosphate. Hydrated crystal surfaces exchange ions with body fluids
Organic90% type I collagen; small proteoglycans; glycoproteins such as osteonectin; calcium-binding osteocalcin; phosphatases in matrix vesicles
  • Other type I collagen-rich tissues lack osteocalcin and matrix vesicles, so they do not calcify.
  • Mineral plus collagen gives hardness and resistance. Decalcified bone keeps its shape but becomes soft and pliable, and stains acidophilic (high collagen).

Periosteum and endosteum

Periosteum (outer surface)Endosteum (inner surface)
StructureLike perichondrium. Outer fibrous layer: dense CT, type I collagen, fibroblasts, vessels. Inner cellular layer: osteoblasts, bone lining cells, osteoprogenitor cellsVery thin; covers trabeculae projecting into marrow cavities; osteoprogenitor cells, osteoblasts and lining cells in sparse, delicate collagen
Special featuresPerforating (Sharpey) fibres: collagen bundles anchoring periosteum to bone; vessels enter the bone from it

Osteoprogenitor cells can proliferate extensively and make many osteoblasts, so they are key to bone growth and repair.

In the clinic: osteoporosis

Common in immobilised patients and postmenopausal women: resorption exceeds formation, so calcium is lost and bone mineral density falls. At-risk people are tested with DEXA scans.

Compact and cancellous bone

Compact (cortical)Cancellous (trabecular, spongy)
Share of bone mass80%20%
StructureDense; mostly osteonsLattice of thin trabeculae (struts) with many interconnecting cavities; strong but light
CoveringPeriosteum outsideEndosteum on all trabeculae
  • Long bones: epiphyses = cancellous with a thin compact shell; diaphysis = almost all compact, a little cancellous around the marrow cavity.
  • Short bones (wrist, ankle): cancellous core surrounded by compact bone.
  • Flat bones of the calvaria: two compact plates separated by cancellous diploë.

Lamellar bone and osteons

Both compact and cancellous bone can be lamellar (mature) or woven (new). Most adult bone is lamellar.

  • Lamellae are 3–7 μm thick, in parallel sheets or concentric rings. Type I collagen in each lamella is aligned, and the direction shifts by about 90° in the next (like plywood), adding strength and causing birefringence (bright and dark bands) in polarised light.
  • An osteon (Haversian system) is 100–250 μm across, with 5–20 concentric lamellae around a central canal containing small vessels, nerves and endosteum. Osteocytes between lamellae connect through canaliculi and receive nutrients from the central canal. Its boundary is the cement line, rich in non-collagen proteins.
  • Osteons are long cylinders parallel to the diaphysis. Transverse perforating (Volkmann) canals, with few or no concentric lamellae, link central canals to each other, the marrow and the periosteum.
  • Interstitial lamellae: irregular remnants of osteons partly removed by osteoclasts during remodelling.
  • In the diaphysis, external circumferential lamellae lie under the periosteum and fewer inner circumferential lamellae line the marrow cavity.

Woven bone

  • Non-lamellar, with randomly arranged type I collagen.
  • The first bone in embryonic development and in fracture repair; usually temporary, replaced by lamellar bone, except near calvarial sutures and some tendon insertions.
  • Less mineral (more radiolucent) and more osteocytes than lamellar bone: forms faster but is weaker.

Commonly confused

PairHow to tell them apart
Osteoblast vs osteocyteOsteoblast: cuboidal, on the surface, active RER. Osteocyte: in a lacuna, processes in canaliculi, less RER
Osteoblast vs osteoclast originOsteoblast: mesenchymal stem cell. Osteoclast: fused monocytes (blood-derived)
Osteoid vs bone matrixOsteoid: new, uncalcified organic matrix. Bone matrix: mineralised
Central vs perforating canalCentral (Haversian): runs lengthwise inside an osteon with concentric lamellae. Perforating (Volkmann): transverse, links osteons, few lamellae
Lamellar vs woven boneLamellar: mature, ordered, strong. Woven: first-formed, random, less mineral, more osteocytes
Osteoporosis vs osteopetrosisOsteoporosis: resorption > formation, thin bones. Osteopetrosis: osteoclasts fail, dense bones, marrow obliterated

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • Osteoclasts come from monocytes, not from osteoblasts; but they need osteoblast M-CSF and RANKL.
  • Parathyroid hormone acts on osteoblasts, which then signal osteoclasts.
  • Osteocalcin is vitamin K-dependent.
  • Osteocytes are the most abundant bone cells; when they die, matrix is resorbed.
  • Decalcified bone is acidophilic (collagen); cartilage matrix is basophilic.
  • Woven bone persists in adults only near skull sutures and some tendon insertions.
  • The cement line marks an osteon’s boundary.

Key terms
