Cardiac & Smooth Muscle

Cardiac muscle: branched mononucleated cells joined by intercalated discs, dyads instead of triads, atrial natriuretic factor and Purkinje fibres. Smooth muscle: fusiform unstriated cells, dense bodies, calmodulin and MLCK, autonomic varicosities. Then how each muscle type regenerates.

Labobo topic: Histo Lec 7: Cardiac & Smooth Muscle

In 60 seconds

  • Cardiac cells do not fuse: they are branched, usually with one central nucleus, striated, and joined end to end by intercalated discs.
  • Intercalated disc: transverse parts = desmosomes + fascia adherens (adhesion); longitudinal parts = gap junctions (electrical coupling).
  • Cardiac SR is sparse; one T-tubule + one terminal cistern = a dyad; T-tubules lie at the Z disc. Mitochondria up to 40% of the cell.
  • Atrial cells secrete ANF (sodium and water loss). Purkinje fibres: big pale glycogen-rich conducting cells, no T-tubules.
  • Smooth muscle: fusiform, central nucleus, no striations, no T-tubules; thin filaments anchor to dense bodies (α-actinin); contraction via calmodulin + MLCK, not troponin.
  • Regeneration: skeletal limited (satellite cells), cardiac very poor (scar), smooth good (mitosis, pericytes).

Cardiac muscle cells

  • In the embryo, mesenchymal cells around the heart tube line up in chains but do not fuse; they form junctions between interdigitating processes.
  • Cells branch and join cells of neighbouring fibres, giving spiralling layers whose contraction “wrings out” the ventricles.
  • Mature cells: 15–30 μm wide and 85–120 μm long, striated like skeletal muscle, but usually with one central nucleus.
  • Endomysium with a rich capillary network surrounds each cell; thicker perimysium separates bundles and in places forms the fibrous cardiac skeleton.

Intercalated discs

Unique to cardiac muscle: dark, irregular, step-like lines crossing the fibres where two cells meet.

Part of the discJunctionsJob
Transverse (across the fibre)Desmosomes and fascia adherensStrong adhesion during constant contraction
Longitudinal (parallel to myofibrils)Gap junctionsIonic continuity: “electrical synapses”, so neighbouring cells contract as a unit

Do not confuse intercalated discs (widely spaced, irregular) with the striations (closely spaced, repetitive).

Mitochondria, SR and dyads

  • Contractile apparatus is essentially the same as in skeletal muscle.
  • Mitochondria occupy up to 40% of the cell volume, more than in slow oxidative skeletal fibres.
  • Main fuel: fatty acids, stored as triglyceride in lipid droplets; glycogen and perinuclear lipofuscin may be present.
  • SR is sparse and less organised. A T-tubule meets one terminal cistern: a dyad (not a triad). Function is the same.
  • Ventricular T-tubules are large and lie at the Z discs; in atrial muscle they are small or absent. Ventricular muscle is much thicker than atrial.

Contraction, innervation and ANF

  • Contraction is intrinsic and spontaneous (cells keep beating in culture). Specialised nodal fibres generate and conduct the impulses.
  • Like skeletal fibres, each cell contracts all or none.
  • Autonomic nerves at the nodes change the rate: sympathetic speeds it up, parasympathetic slows it.
  • Atrial cells have secretory granules (0.2–0.3 μm) at the nuclear poles, near small Golgi complexes, most in the right atrium (~600 per cell). They hold the precursor of atrial natriuretic factor (ANF).
  • ANF acts on the kidney: sodium and water loss (natriuresis, diuresis), opposing aldosterone and ADH. So the atria are also endocrine.

Purkinje fibres

  • Found in the subendocardium (under the endocardium lining the chambers).
  • Conduct the impulse so the ventricular myocardium contracts in a synchronised way; run from the interventricular septum to the papillary muscles and up the lateral ventricular walls.
  • Larger than cardiac muscle cells, with fewer myofibrils, much glycogen (pale cytoplasm), many mitochondria and no T-tubules.
  • Joined by desmosomes and gap junctions, but not by intercalated discs.

Myocardial ischaemia

In the clinic: ischaemia and infarction

The commonest injury to heart muscle is ischaemia: lack of oxygen when a coronary artery is narrowed or blocked by atherosclerotic plaque. Too many cholesterol particles build up in the artery wall as plaque; if a plaque ruptures, a clot forms and can cause a myocardial infarction. With no satellite cells, adult heart muscle hardly regenerates; research looks at mesenchymal stem cells, based on fish, amphibians and newborn mice, which can regrow heart muscle.

Smooth muscle cells

  • Specialised for slow, steady contraction under autonomic nerves and hormones; found in blood vessels and the digestive, respiratory, urinary and reproductive tracts (visceral muscle).
  • Cells are elongated, tapering (fusiform), unstriated, each with an external lamina and an endomysium of type I and III collagen (reticulin).
  • Length 20 μm (small vessels) to 500 μm (pregnant uterus); 5–10 μm wide at the centre, where the single elongated nucleus sits.
  • Narrow ends pack against broad parts of neighbours, so a cross section shows profiles of many sizes and only the largest contain a nucleus.
  • In the gut, an inner circular and an outer longitudinal layer contract together to produce peristalsis.
  • Near the nucleus: mitochondria, polyribosomes, RER, Golgi. All cells are linked by many gap junctions. Surface caveolae hold signalling proteins and the ion channels that control Ca²⁺ release.

Dense bodies and contraction

  • Thin and thick filaments are not grouped into myofibrils; bundles crisscross the cell obliquely, with fewer crossbridges.
  • Thin filaments insert into dense bodies in the cytoplasm and under the membrane. Dense bodies contain α-actinin, so they work like Z discs.
  • Desmin intermediate filaments (10 nm) also attach to dense bodies; membrane dense bodies include cadherins of desmosomes linking neighbouring cells. Force is passed within and between cells.
  • No troponin or tropomyosin control: Ca²⁺ binds calmodulin, which activates myosin light-chain kinase (MLCK).
  • Rudimentary SR and no T-tubules (unneeded in small tapering cells with many gap junctions).
  • On contraction the cell shortens, its border becomes scalloped and the nucleus turns corkscrew-shaped.

Innervation and ECM synthesis

  • Not voluntary; no well-defined neuromuscular junctions. Stimulated by autonomic nerves, plus paracrine signals in the gut and oxytocin in the uterus.
  • Autonomic axons have swellings called varicosities next to the muscle; they release acetylcholine or norepinephrine, which diffuses to many cells. Gap junctions spread the stimulus so cells contract together.
  • Smooth muscle cells also act like fibroblasts, making collagen, elastin and proteoglycans (like myofibroblasts and pericytes).
In the clinic: leiomyoma

Benign tumours of smooth muscle, usually harmless, can arise anywhere it is found (skin, eye, bladder, gut, airways). Most occur in the uterine wall, called fibroids, and can grow large enough to cause painful pressure and unexpected bleeding.

Regeneration of muscle

SkeletalCardiacSmooth
CapacityLimitedVery poorGood
HowQuiescent satellite cells (inside the external lamina) activate, proliferate and fuse into new fibres; fibres themselves cannot divideNo satellite cells; damage (infarct) is replaced by fibroblasts and connective tissue: a scarSurviving cells undergo mitosis; pericytes help repair vascular smooth muscle

Satellite cells also fuse with existing fibres after heavy exercise, adding mass beyond hypertrophy. After major trauma, scarring interferes with skeletal muscle regeneration.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • Cardiac cells do not fuse; they have one or two central nuclei.
  • Gap junctions are in the longitudinal parts of the intercalated disc.
  • Atrial T-tubules are small or absent; ventricular ones are large.
  • ANF causes sodium and water loss, the opposite of aldosterone and ADH.
  • Purkinje fibres are in the subendocardium and lack T-tubules.
  • Smooth muscle has no T-tubules and no troponin.
  • Heart muscle damage heals by scar; smooth muscle regenerates best.

Key terms

Intercalated disc: junction between cardiac cells · Fascia adherens: actin-anchoring junction in the disc · Gap junction: channel for ionic coupling · Cardiac skeleton: dense fibrous CT of the heart · Dyad: T-tubule plus one SR cistern · Lipofuscin: perinuclear wear-and-tear pigment · Atrial natriuretic factor: atrial hormone causing Na⁺ and water loss · Purkinje fibre: subendocardial conducting cell · Subendocardium: layer beneath the endocardium · Ischaemia: tissue damage from lack of oxygen · Fusiform: spindle-shaped · Peristalsis: wave moving gut contents · Caveolae: surface invaginations with signalling and Ca²⁺ channels · Dense body: α-actinin anchor in smooth muscle · Desmin: intermediate filament of muscle · Calmodulin: Ca²⁺-binding protein · MLCK: myosin light-chain kinase · Varicosity: transmitter-releasing swelling of an autonomic axon · Leiomyoma: benign smooth muscle tumour (fibroid) · Satellite cell: reserve myoblast · Pericyte: contractile cell around small vessels.
