Types of Connective Tissue and Adipose Tissue

Loose and dense connective tissue, reticular and mucoid tissue, and the two kinds of fat: white adipose tissue for storage and brown adipose tissue for heat, with obesity, lipoma and liposarcoma.

Labobo topic: Histo Lec 2: Types of Connective Tissue

In 60 seconds

  • Loose (areolar): cells, fibres and ground substance in about equal parts; under epithelia. Dense irregular: random type I bundles (dermis, capsules). Dense regular: parallel bundles (tendons, ligaments, aponeuroses).
  • Reticular tissue: type III collagen made by reticular cells; stroma of marrow, lymph nodes and spleen. Mucoid tissue: hyaluronan-rich Wharton’s jelly of the umbilical cord.
  • White fat: unilocular, 50–150 μm, signet-ring nucleus, stores lipid, secretes leptin; ~20% of adult weight.
  • Brown fat: multilocular, central nucleus, many mitochondria; heat by UCP1 (thermogenin); 2–5% of newborn weight.
  • Adult obesity = adipocyte hypertrophy; childhood obesity = hypertrophy + hyperplasia. Weight loss shrinks cells, not their number.

Learning objectives

  • distinguish loose, dense irregular and dense regular connective tissue and give examples;
  • describe reticular and mucoid tissue;
  • compare white and brown adipose tissue in structure, location, function and development.

Loose and dense connective tissue

“Loose” and “dense” refer to the amount of collagen.

Loose (areolar)Dense irregularDense regular
Make-upCells, fibres, ground substance in roughly equal parts; all CT cell types, nerves, small vesselsFew cells (mostly fibroblasts); type I bundles randomly interwovenType I bundles and fibrocytes in parallel; very little ground substance; few vessels
PropertyDelicate, flexible, not very resistant to stressResists stress from all directionsResists prolonged stress from one direction; inextensible, white fresh
WhereBeneath epithelia; between muscle and nerve fibresDeep dermis; capsules of most organsTendons, aponeuroses, ligaments
  • Loose and dense irregular tissue often grade into each other.
  • In tendons, fibrocytes with elongated nuclei lie between collagen fibres and wrap them with thin cytoplasmic folds. Tendons join muscle to bone; aponeuroses are sheet-like tendons with layers of bundles at 90°; ligaments join bones. Some (elastic ligaments of the vertebral column) also have many elastic fibres.
  • Tendons and ligaments are covered by dense irregular tissue continuous with muscle and bone; being poorly vascularised, they repair slowly.

Reticular tissue

Reticular tissue has abundant type III collagen (reticulin) forming a delicate network, made by modified fibroblasts called reticular cells that stay on and partly cover the fibres.

  • Gives a framework and special microenvironment for cells of hemopoietic tissue and lymphoid organs: bone marrow, lymph nodes, spleen.
  • The cell-lined meshwork lets leukocytes and lymph pass; macrophages and dendritic cells in it monitor cells and remove debris.

Mucoid tissue

Mucoid (mucous) tissue is the main tissue of the umbilical cord, called Wharton’s jelly. Abundant ground substance of hyaluronan makes it gelatinous, with sparse collagen and scattered fibroblasts, including many mesenchymal stem cells (studied for regenerative medicine). It resembles embryonic mesenchyme and the tissue of the eye’s vitreous and young tooth pulp; it is rare in adults.

White adipose tissue

There are two types of fat. Both have a rich blood supply, and, unlike other CT cells, each adipocyte is surrounded by an external lamina with type IV collagen.

  • White adipocytes store energy long-term. Spherical alone, polyhedral when packed; 50–150 μm across; one huge triglyceride droplet: unilocular.
  • Lipid is dissolved by xylene in routine slides, so cells look empty; the flattened peripheral nucleus gives a signet-ring look. Osmium tetroxide preserves lipid and stains it black.
  • Organelles sit near the nucleus; the thin rim of cytoplasm has SER and pinocytotic vesicles; many caveolae help lipid trafficking and droplet formation.
  • Tissue is divided into incomplete lobules by septa with vessels and nerves; reticular fibres support each cell; fibroblasts, macrophages and other cells make up about half the cells.
  • Distribution changes with age and sex hormones (breasts, thighs); colour runs white to yellow with dietary carotenoids.
In the clinic: adipose tumours

Unilocular adipocytes form common benign lipomas; malignant liposarcomas are rare. Fetal lipomas of brown fat are called hibernomas.

Storing and releasing lipid

White adipocytes store triglyceride from three sources:

  1. dietary fat carried as chylomicrons;
  2. liver-made lipid carried as VLDL;
  3. fatty acids and glycerol made by the adipocyte itself.
  • Lipoprotein lipase, made by adipocytes and moved to the capillary surface, hydrolyses chylomicrons and VLDL.
  • Insulin increases glucose uptake and its conversion to triglyceride, increases lipoprotein lipase and lipid-synthesis enzymes, and inhibits hormone-sensitive lipase.
  • Lipid is mobilised by hormone-sensitive lipase, activated by norepinephrine and peptide hormones; fatty acids travel on albumin.
  • White adipocytes secrete leptin, a satiety factor acting on the hypothalamus that regulates appetite and new fat formation.
  • Lipid is taken fairly evenly from all depots, but fat of the palms, soles and behind the eyes resists even long starvation.
  • Visceral fat (drains to the portal circulation) raises the risk of diabetes and cardiovascular disease; subcutaneous fat does not.

Development of fat and obesity

Adipocytes come from mesenchymal stem cells via preadipocytes (like large fibroblasts with lipid droplets). In white cells the droplets fuse into one. A smaller population of beige adipocytes stays in white fat; in cold they reversibly form many droplets, express brown-fat genes and make heat.

  • White fat accumulates from about the 14th week of gestation; afterwards fat grows mainly by filling existing cells until about age 10, then new cells form through adolescence, around small vessels.
  • Adult-onset obesity: mainly larger cells (hypertrophy), often with lower hormone-sensitive lipase activity.
  • Childhood obesity: larger and more cells (hyperplasia), which predisposes to obesity later.
  • Weight loss reduces cell volume, not number. No diet type beats another: any intake below expenditure loses fat.
  • White fat also releases many proinflammatory cytokines; obesity is a state of chronic mild inflammation, linked to diabetes and heart disease.

Brown adipose tissue

  • 2–5% of newborn weight (back, neck, shoulders); greatly reduced in childhood; in adults only scattered (around kidneys, adrenals, aorta, mediastinum).
  • Colour from abundant mitochondria (cytochromes) and many capillaries.
  • Cells are multilocular (many small droplets), polygonal, smaller than white cells, with a central nucleus; packed around capillaries in well-defined lobules; direct sympathetic innervation.

How it makes heat (nonshivering thermogenesis)

  1. In newborns (and animals ending hibernation) nerves release norepinephrine, activating hormone-sensitive lipase.
  2. Unlike white fat, the fatty acids are not released but oxidised at once in the mitochondria.
  3. UCP1 (thermogenin) in the inner mitochondrial membrane lets protons flow back into the matrix without passing ATP synthase, so the energy becomes heat, warming the blood.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • White adipocytes look empty in H&E because solvents remove the lipid; osmium keeps it (black).
  • Adipocytes, unlike other CT cells, have an external lamina (type IV collagen).
  • Brown fat fatty acids are burned in place, not released into the blood.
  • UCP1 makes heat by letting protons bypass ATP synthase.
  • Insulin inhibits hormone-sensitive lipase; norepinephrine activates it.
  • Reticular tissue supports lymphoid organs except the thymus.
  • Weight loss does not reduce adipocyte number.
