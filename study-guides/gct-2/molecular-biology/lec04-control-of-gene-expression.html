Control of Gene Expression

Why cells with the same genome differ, the translation cycle, the steps where expression can be controlled, promoters and regulatory DNA, the trp and lac operons, eukaryotic enhancers, Mediator and chromatin modifiers, combinatorial control, master regulators, and how transcription-factor binding sites are found.

Labobo topic: MolBio Lec 4: Control of Gene Expression

In 60 seconds

  • All cells of an organism share the same DNA; differentiation comes from which genes are expressed (a typical cell uses about half its genes).
  • Expression can be controlled at every step, but mainly at transcription.
  • Bacteria: σ factor finds the promoter. Eukaryotes: Pol II needs general transcription factors; TBP of TFIID binds the TATA box.
  • trp operon: 5 genes, one mRNA, switched off by the trp repressor when tryptophan is high. lac operon (lacZ, lacY, lacA): ON only when lactose present (allolactose removes the repressor) and glucose absent (cAMP–CAP activates).
  • Eukaryotic activators bind enhancers far away; DNA loops and Mediator links them to the promoter; they also recruit histone acetyltransferases and chromatin remodelling complexes.
  • Binding sites are found by deletion mapping, DNA footprinting (DNase I) and ChIP (ChIP-chip, ChIP-seq).

Overview of gene expression

  • Even bacteria switch genes on and off, e.g. to make enzymes for available food.
  • A neuron and a liver cell are hugely different but share the same genome: cell differentiation is achieved by changes in gene expression.
  • Examples: pancreatic β cells make insulin, α cells glucagon; B lymphocytes make antibodies.
  • A typical differentiated cell expresses only about half its genes; the selection differs by cell type.
  • The final amount of a protein depends on the rate of every step, including degradation of mRNA and protein.

The translation cycle

Before translation, aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase charges each tRNA (amino acid activation or aminoacylation). Then a four-step cycle repeats:

  1. A charged tRNA binds the empty A site by pairing with the codon. A and P sites are so close that their tRNAs pair with contiguous codons, keeping the reading frame.
  2. The carboxyl end of the chain is released from the tRNA in the P site and joined by a peptide bond to the amino acid on the A-site tRNA (catalytic site in the large subunit).
  3. The large subunit shifts, moving the two tRNAs into the E and P sites.
  4. The small subunit moves exactly three nucleotides, ejecting the spent tRNA and emptying the A site.

mRNA is read 5′→3′; the N-terminus is made first and each cycle adds to the C-terminus. In prokaryotes the small subunit recognises the Shine–Dalgarno sequence on the mRNA.

Where expression is controlled

A cell can control its proteins by regulating:

  1. when and how often a gene is transcribed (the main site for most genes);
  2. how the transcript is spliced or processed;
  3. which mRNAs are exported to the cytosol;
  4. how fast mRNAs are degraded;
  5. which mRNAs are translated;
  6. how fast proteins are destroyed, and protein activity.

Promoters and regulatory DNA

  • Transcription regulators (transcription factors) bind short regulatory DNA sequences. In bacteria they bind close to the polymerase site; in eukaryotes they may be thousands of base pairs away.
  • A promoter binds RNA polymerase and orients it; it has the start site plus recognition sites for σ factor (bacteria, released once transcription begins) or general transcription factors (eukaryotes).
  • RNA polymerase II needs GTFs named TFIIB, TFIID and so on. Most promoters have a TATA box, bound by the TATA-binding protein (TBP), a subunit of TFIID; it sets the correct start site.
  • Regulatory sequences range from ~10 bp simple switches (typical of bacteria) to over 100,000 bp integrating many signals (eukaryotes). They act only when a regulator binds them.
  • Regulators often bind as dimers, doubling the protein–DNA contacts, and cooperate (e.g. NFAT-1 and AP-1 on a cytokine gene, bending the DNA).

Operons: the trp operon, repressors and activators

  • Bacteria regulate genes according to available food. In E. coli, five genes for making tryptophan sit together and are transcribed from one promoter as one mRNA: an operon.
  • Low tryptophan → operon transcribed. High tryptophan → tryptophan activates the trp repressor, which binds the operator and switches the genes off.
  • Activators work on weak promoters: they bind a nearby sequence and contact RNA polymerase to help it start. Their DNA binding is often controlled by a small molecule.

Eukaryotic regulation at a distance

  • Enhancers raise and repressor (silencer) elements lower expression. Like promoters they are short (50–1,000 bp) with binding sites, but can lie up to 1 million bp away, upstream or downstream.
  • An activator on a distant enhancer attracts RNA polymerase and GTFs: the DNA loops, and the large Mediator complex acts as go-between, forming the transcription initiation complex.
  • Eukaryotic regulators act in two ways: they affect assembly of the polymerase and GTFs at the promoter, and they modify local chromatin.
  • They recruit histone acetyltransferases (acetyl groups become binding sites for activating proteins) and chromatin-remodelling complexes (make nucleosomal DNA, e.g. the TATA box, accessible).

Combinatorial control and master regulators

  • Combinatorial control: a “committee” of regulators decides each gene’s expression; a typical gene has dozens of regulators over tens of thousands of base pairs. GTFs are the same for all Pol II genes; regulators differ. Spacer DNA between sites binds nothing.
  • One regulator can still be decisive by completing a combination. Cortisol: the cortisol–receptor complex binds a sequence in many liver genes, turning on glucose production in starvation or stress; when cortisol falls they return to normal.
  • Two classes of transcription factor: general (core promoter, with the polymerase) and sequence-specific (activators or repressors). Sites differ in affinity; usually several factors must bind.
  • Master regulator: expressing the fly gene eyeless (Ey) in leg precursor cells builds a structurally normal eye on the leg (not connected to the brain).

DNA sites and how to find them

  • Core promoter: from about the TATA box to the start site (+1); assembly of the preinitiation complex of Pol II and GTFs. TATA box is about 25–30 bases upstream.
  • Proximal promoter: CAAT box and GC box, 100–150 bp upstream, bound by widespread factors (NF1, SP1). TATA sets where transcription starts; CAAT and GC boxes set how often.
  • A consensus sequence is the most common nucleotide at each position of aligned related sequences.
MethodHow it works
Deletion mappingPromoters with parts deleted are put into cells. Less transcription → an activator site was lost; more → a repressor site; no change → unimportant
DNA footprintingBound protein protects DNA from DNase I; the protected stretch (the “footprint”) is the binding site
Genome-wide location (ChIP)Formaldehyde cross-links factors to DNA; chromatin is sheared and precipitated with an antibody; DNA is read on microarrays (ChIP-chip) or by sequencing (ChIP-seq)

Many binding sites lie far from promoters. Sites bound by all of Oct4, Sox2 and Nanog (pluripotency factors in embryonic stem cells) are much more likely to matter.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • The lac operon needs both lactose present and glucose absent.
  • cAMP is high when glucose is low.
  • The lac repressor binds allolactose, not glucose.
  • TBP is part of TFIID.
  • The small subunit moves three nucleotides; the large subunit shifts first.
  • Most genes are controlled mainly at transcription.
