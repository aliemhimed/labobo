Intermediate Filaments and Actin Filaments

Rope-like intermediate filaments and their diseases (ALS, epidermolysis bullosa simplex, plectin defects, Duchenne dystrophy, progeria), linker proteins across the nuclear envelope, then actin: structure, treadmilling, actin-binding proteins, the cortex, cell crawling, myosins, muscle contraction and Rho-family GTPases.

Labobo topic: MolBio Lec 6: Intermediate Filaments and Actin Filaments

In 60 seconds

  • Intermediate filaments (~10 nm) are the toughest filaments: they resist stretching. Assembly: monomer → coiled dimer → staggered antiparallel tetramer → 8 tetramers → rope.
  • Four classes, e.g. keratins (epithelia, >50 genes), neurofilaments (axons), and nuclear lamins (nuclear lamina).
  • Diseases: EB simplex (keratin KRT5/KRT14, skin blisters), ALS (neurofilament build-up in motor neurons), plectin mutations, progeria (LMNA → progerin).
  • Actin: two-stranded helix, twist every 37 nm, ATP-bound monomers, polar (+ fast, − slow); treadmilling when addition = loss.
  • Thymosin, profilin hold monomers; formins, ARPs promote polymerisation. Cortex: actin + spectrin (red cell shape).
  • Myosins walk to the actin plus end: myosin-I (one head, vesicles, membrane) and myosin-II (two heads, filaments, contraction). Rho: stress fibres; Rac: lamellipodia; Cdc42: filopodia.

Intermediate filaments

  • Great tensile strength: they let cells withstand stretching. The toughest and most durable filaments: they survive concentrated salt and nonionic detergents that destroy the rest of the cytoskeleton.
  • Named “intermediate” because in smooth muscle, where first found, their diameter (~10 nm) lies between thin actin and thick myosin filaments.
  • In epithelia, keratin filaments of neighbouring cells connect through desmosomes, making a continuous mechanical link across the sheet.
  • Four major classes with many subtypes (humans have >50 keratin genes). Abundant in stressed cells: along axons, in muscle, and in skin epithelium.
  1. Monomer: α-helical central rod with unstructured ends.
  2. Two monomers coil into a dimer.
  3. Two dimers form a staggered, antiparallel tetramer.
  4. Eight tetramers pack into a helical array.
  5. These form the rope-like filament.

The rod domains of all types are similar in size and sequence; the end domains vary greatly.

Neurofilaments and ALS

Neurofilaments run along vertebrate axons, giving long axons strength and stability.

In the clinic: amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (Lou Gehrig’s disease)

Abnormal accumulation of neurofilaments in the cell bodies and axons of motor neurons may trigger axon degeneration. Motor neurons die, muscles weaken, twitch (fasciculations) and waste (atrophy): early twitches, cramps, spasticity, weakness, slurred speech; later dysphagia, dysarthria, and breathing failure needing a ventilator. Thinking and memory usually remain. About 10% is hereditary; C9orf72 causes 25–40% of familial cases. Most start between 55 and 75; slightly more men; veterans 1.5–2 times more likely (lead, pesticides suspected). No cure.

Keratins and epidermolysis bullosa simplex

  • Keratins are the most diverse class: every epithelium (tongue, cornea, gut lining) has its own mixture; special keratins form hair.
  • Keratin cables across the epithelial sheet spread the stress of stretching skin.
In the clinic: epidermolysis bullosa simplex

Mutations stop keratin filaments forming in the epidermis: KRT5 (12q), KRT14 (17q) or PLEC (8q). Gentle pressure ruptures basal layer cells and the skin blisters. Treatment cares for blisters and prevents new ones.

Plectin and SUN–KASH linkers

  • Plectin, a giant protein in nearly all mammalian cells, bundles intermediate filaments and links them to microtubules, actin filaments and desmosomes.
  • Plectin mutations cause a disease combining EB simplex, muscular dystrophy and neurodegeneration; plectin-null mice die within days with blistered skin and abnormal skeletal and heart muscle.
  • SUN (inner) and KASH (outer) domain proteins bind each other across the nuclear envelope, connecting the lamina and chromosomes to every cytoskeletal network.
  • They position and move the nucleus, attach centrosomes to the nucleus, and align homologous chromosomes for pairing and recombination in meiosis.

Duchenne muscular dystrophy

  • The commonest severe hereditary myopathy, mainly in males: progressive weakness and atrophy of skeletal and heart muscle.
  • Mutations in the DMD gene for dystrophin, part of a complex linking the muscle cytoskeleton through the membrane to the ECM. Dystrophin is mainly in skeletal and heart muscle, with a little in some brain neurons.
  • X-linked recessive, passed by carrier mothers; about 30% of cases are new mutations.
  • Incurable and fatal, usually from lung or heart failure, often by about age 25, though supportive care helps people live longer.

The nuclear lamina and progeria

  • Intermediate filaments of the nuclear lamina line the inner face of the nuclear envelope, strengthen it and probably anchor chromosomes.
In the clinic: Hutchinson–Gilford progeria

Mutation in LMNA (chromosome 1), which encodes lamin A (shapes the nucleus). An abnormal form, progerin, causes rapid ageing: wrinkled skin, loss of teeth and hair, severe cardiovascular disease by the teens. Most do not live past 13; about 1 in 4 million births.

Actin filaments

  • Present in all eukaryotic cells; needed for crawling, phagocytosis and division. Structures: microvilli, contractile bundles, filopodia, the contractile ring.
  • Thin, flexible threads. Each monomer has a cleft binding ATP or ADP. The filament is a two-stranded helix with a twist every 37 nm; each subunit touches four neighbours.
  • Polarised: fast-growing plus end, slow minus end.
  • Monomers carry ATP, hydrolysed soon after joining; ADP-actin is less stable, and ADP stays trapped until the monomer leaves.
  • Treadmilling: when addition at the plus end equals loss at the minus end, length is constant while subunits move through.
  • Toxins from fungi and marine sponges perturb actin; latrunculins have antiproliferative, antimicrobial and antimetastatic activity.

Actin-binding proteins and the cortex

  • Actin is about 5% of cell protein; about half is in filaments, half free.
  • Thymosin and profilin bind free monomers and keep them in reserve; formins and actin-related proteins (ARPs) promote polymerisation.
  • Other binding proteins cross-link filaments into networks or bundles, attach them to membranes, or slide them.
  • The cell cortex is a meshwork of actin under the plasma membrane giving strength and driving shape change. In red blood cells, spectrin tetramers with a little actin form a mesh attached to transmembrane proteins, keeping the biconcave disc shape.

Cell crawling and Rho GTPases

Neutrophils crawl toward bacterial molecules (chemotaxis): receptor signals change actin assembly. Crawling has three steps, all using actin:

  1. Protrusion at the leading edge.
  2. Adhesion of the protrusion to the surface.
  3. Traction: the rest of the cell pulls forward.

Myosins and muscle contraction

  • Myosins hydrolyse ATP and walk toward the actin plus end. Most abundant: myosin-I and myosin-II.
  • Myosin-I: one head and a tail; moves vesicles along actin or pulls the membrane into new shapes.
  • Myosin-II (muscle): dimer with two heads and a long tail; assembles into bipolar filaments with heads facing opposite ways, sliding oppositely oriented actin filaments. Many heads must stay attached while others reposition.
  • Sarcomere: actin filaments anchored by their plus ends at the Z disc; filaments keep their length while the sarcomere shortens.
  • Trigger: motor neuron → action potential → T-tubule voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channel physically opens the Ca²⁺-release channel of the sarcoplasmic reticulum → sudden rise of cytosolic Ca²⁺ → signal via Ca²⁺-binding proteins on actin filaments.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • Intermediate filament tetramers are antiparallel, so the filament has no polarity.
  • ALS involves neurofilament accumulation, not loss.
  • Keratins are the most diverse class.
  • Actin binds ATP; tubulin binds GTP.
  • Myosins (I and II) walk to the actin plus end.
  • Actin plus ends are anchored at the Z disc.
