The Central Dogma

How information flows from DNA to RNA to protein: the RNA world idea, Garrod’s inborn errors, reverse transcription, split genes, the RNA classes, RNA polymerases in bacteria and eukaryotes, promoters and terminators, and how a pre-mRNA is capped, tailed and spliced.

Labobo topic: MolBio Lec 1: The Central Dogma

In 60 seconds

  • DNA → RNA → protein (Crick, 1958). Retroviruses add RNA → DNA by reverse transcriptase.
  • Eukaryotic genes are split: exons (kept) and introns (spliced out).
  • RNA in a cell: rRNA up to 80%, tRNA ~15%, mRNA <5%.
  • RNA is made 5′→3′; the template is read 3′→5′. The coding strand has the same sequence as the RNA (T for U).
  • Eukaryotes: Pol I rRNA (nucleolus), Pol II all mRNA, Pol III tRNA and 5S rRNA. E. coli: one enzyme, α₂ββ′σ; σ finds the −10 (TATAAT) and −35 (TTGACA) boxes.
  • mRNA processing: 5′ 7-methylguanosine cap, poly(A) tail (signal AAUAAA), splicing (GU…AG, spliceosome); alternative splicing gives several proteins from one gene.

  • compare bacterial and eukaryotic RNA polymerases, promoters and termination;

The central dogma

  • Proposed by Francis Crick in 1958: genetic information flows DNA → RNA → protein.
  • RNA world hypothesis: life began with RNA that could self-replicate; RNA catalysis is still essential in protein synthesis.
  • Archibald Garrod (1908) linked a genetic defect to a missing enzyme: “inborn errors of metabolism”. In alcaptonuria, the enzyme that oxidises homogentisic acid (from phenylalanine and tyrosine breakdown) is missing; the acid is excreted and urine darkens in air.
  • A cell expresses different genes at different rates.
  • Retroviruses (e.g. HIV) carry RNA and copy it into DNA with reverse transcriptase, so the dogma becomes DNA ↔ RNA → protein.

Genes, exons and introns

  • Most eukaryotic protein-coding genes are split: coding exons separated by noncoding introns (intervening sequences). Bacterial genes usually are not.
  • The β-globin gene has 3 exons and 2 introns. Introns are often much longer than exons, so the gene is much longer than its mRNA.
  • Exons are the parts that “exit” the nucleus in mRNA; introns are spliced out first.
  • A gene needs exons, start and stop signals and regulatory elements. Most protein-coding genes are single-copy genes.
  • Globin gene clusters: adults express α, β and δ, with δ very low.
  • Protein synthesis has four steps: transcription → RNA processing → translation → post-translational processing.

  • mRNA, rRNA and tRNA all come from longer primary transcripts (pre-RNA); the DNA segment copied is a transcription unit.
  • Primary transcripts are never naked: proteins bind them as they form. They are cut down by “cut-and-paste” steps using small RNAs (90–300 nt) and proteins.
  • Genes coding for mRNA are protein-coding genes; a gene is expressed when its information reaches mRNA and protein.

Transcription and RNA polymerase

  • DNA-dependent RNA polymerase copies one DNA strand. It searches for the start site, selects the right ribonucleotide, forms phosphodiester bonds, and detects termination signals.
  • Base rules: template T, A, G, C → RNA A, U, C, G.
  • The template strand guides synthesis; the nontemplate (coding) strand has the same sequence as the RNA. Which strand is the template depends on the gene.
  • The polymerase binds the promoter, which says which strand is copied and where to start; it needs transcription factors to recognise it.
  • The polymerase moves along the template 3′→5′; RNA grows 5′→3′. A terminator sequence stops it.
  • The polymerase covers ~35 bp; the transcription bubble of melted DNA is ~15 bp; the DNA–RNA hybrid ~9 bp.
  • Transcription has three phases: initiation, elongation, termination.

Transcription in bacteria

  • Gene notation: the first transcribed nucleotide is +1; downstream is positive (drawn right), upstream negative (left).
  • E. coli uses one RNA polymerase of five subunits: α₂, β, β′, σ.
  • σ factor recognises the promoter: the −10 box (Pribnow box, TATATT/TATAAT) and the −35 box (TTGACA).
  • σ brings the core enzyme to DNA: closed promoter complex. Strands separate (easy at the AT-rich −10 box, two H-bonds per A–T pair): open promoter complex, then the transcription bubble.
  • At about 10 nucleotides of RNA, σ is released. β binds nucleotides and forms the bonds; β′ holds DNA; the two α assemble the enzyme on the promoter.
  • Rho-independent terminator: two GC-rich regions ~10 bp apart, then a run of A. Rho-dependent: the rho protein frees the RNA from the DNA.

Transcription in eukaryotes

  • Happens in the nucleus; translation is in the cytoplasm (in bacteria both are together).
  • Needs many transcription factors for binding, initiation, elongation and termination.
  • Compact chromatin (nucleosomes) must be opened for the polymerase to reach DNA.
  • The primary transcript must be processed into mature mRNA.
  • Two classes of transcription factor: general transcription factors (GTFs) and activators/repressors.

mRNA structure and processing

  • mRNA has a continuous coding sequence, is found in the cytoplasm, and is translated on ribosomes. About 25% of globin mRNA is noncoding (5′ and 3′ UTRs, which regulate).
  • Capping: very early, a guanosine joins the 5′ end by a 5′–5′ triphosphate link and is methylated: 7-methylguanosine cap.
  • Polyadenylation: AAUAAA about 20 nt upstream signals poly(A) polymerase; the tail is 50–250 A.
  • Cap and tail protect the unstable RNA from nucleases and lengthen its life; bacterial mRNA, tRNA and rRNA lack them.
  • Splicing in the nucleus: introns start with GU and end with AG. snRNAs plus proteins form the spliceosome; base pairing loops the intron out and the exons are joined. Then mRNA leaves for the cytoplasm.
  • Alternative splicing also removes some exons, so one gene gives different proteins in different cells or times (e.g. dynein motors for different cargo).

Mitochondrial transcription

  • A nuclear-encoded polymerase, POLRMT, transcribes mitochondrial DNA; it resembles the RNA polymerases of phages T3 and T7.
  • It needs TFAM and TFB2M to start.
  • The circular genome has one promoter per strand; each strand is copied whole, then cut into individual mRNAs, tRNAs and rRNAs.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • All mRNAs are made by Pol II; 5S rRNA is made by Pol III, not Pol I.
  • σ factor recognises the promoter and then leaves; β, not σ, forms the bonds.
  • mRNA is the smallest fraction of cell RNA.
  • The cap is joined 5′–5′, not 3′–5′.
  • Bacterial mRNAs have no cap or poly(A) tail.
  • Alcaptonuria: homogentisic acid accumulates; urine darkens in air.
