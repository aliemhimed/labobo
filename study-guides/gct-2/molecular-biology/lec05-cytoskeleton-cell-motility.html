The Cytoskeleton and Cell Motility

The three filament systems and what the cytoskeleton does, then microtubules in detail: tubulin and polarity, the centrosome and γ-tubulin, dynamic instability and GTP, colchicine and Taxol, kinesins and dyneins, organelle positioning, cilia and flagella, and Kartagener’s syndrome.

Labobo topic: MolBio Lec 5: The Cytoskeleton and Cell Motility

In 60 seconds

  • Three filaments: microtubules (tubulin, hollow, stiff), actin filaments (microfilaments) and intermediate filaments.
  • Microtubules are made of α/β-tubulin dimers; they grow from γ-tubulin ring complexes in the centrosome (minus end anchored, plus end free).
  • Dynamic instability: a GTP cap keeps the plus end growing; if GTP is hydrolysed to GDP first, the cap is lost and the microtubule shrinks fast.
  • Colchicine blocks polymerisation; Taxol blocks depolymerisation; both arrest mitosis and treat cancer.
  • Kinesins walk to the plus end (outward); cytoplasmic dyneins to the minus end (inward, via dynactin). Myosins use actin; no motor uses intermediate filaments.
  • Cilia and flagella: 9 + 2 axoneme bent by ciliary dynein; dynein defects → Kartagener’s syndrome (infections, infertility, situs inversus).

The cytoskeleton and its functions

The cytoskeleton is a network of protein filaments throughout the cytoplasm that gives shape, support and movement. Its three filament types differ in composition, mechanical properties and roles.

  1. A dynamic scaffold that sets cell shape and resists deformation.
  2. An internal framework that positions organelles.
  3. A network of tracks for moving materials (e.g. delivering mRNA to parts of the cell).
  4. The force-generating apparatus for cell locomotion (sperm, white blood cells, fibroblasts).
  5. Part of the division machinery: separating chromosomes in mitosis and meiosis, and cytokinesis.

Microtubules and tubulin

  • Long, relatively stiff hollow tubes that can disassemble in one place and reassemble in another.
  • Built from tubulin, a dimer of two similar globular proteins, α-tubulin and β-tubulin. The tube has structurally distinct plus and minus ends.
  • Found in the cytoplasmic cytoskeleton, mitotic spindle, centrioles and the core of cilia and flagella.
  • They form tracks for vesicles and organelles, e.g. peroxisomes travel along microtubules. In mitosis the cytoplasmic array disassembles and rebuilds as the spindle.

The centrosome

  • The major microtubule-organising centre: a pair of centrioles in a protein matrix.
  • The matrix holds hundreds of γ-tubulin ring complexes; each is the nucleation site for one microtubule.
  • The minus end stays embedded in the centrosome; the plus end grows into the cytoplasm.

Dynamic instability

  • Each microtubule grows and shrinks independently; it may shrink partly then regrow, or vanish and be replaced from the same γ-tubulin ring.
  • GTP-tubulin dimers bind each other more tightly than GDP-tubulin. A plus end capped with new GTP-tubulin (GTP cap) keeps growing.
  • If growth is slow, the cap’s GTP is hydrolysed before new dimers arrive; the cap is lost, protofilaments peel away and the microtubule shrinks rapidly.
  • A microtubule persists only if both ends are protected: minus ends by the organising centre, plus ends by capping proteins in the cortex.
  • Selective stabilisation in one region biases the array and polarises the cell.

In the clinic: antimitotic cancer drugs

Opposite mechanisms, same result: both colchicine and Taxol arrest dividing cells in mitosis, because the spindle must both assemble and disassemble. Such antimitotic drugs are used to treat human cancers.

Axonal transport and organelle positioning

  • Most differentiated cells are polarised. In an axon, all microtubules point the same way, plus ends toward the axon terminal.
  • Outward traffic carries materials made in the cell body to the terminal (spinal cord to shoulder muscle takes about two days); backward traffic carries worn-out mitochondria and ingested material, by a different set of motors.
  • ER reaches almost to the cell edge: kinesins pull it outward along microtubules like a net.
  • Golgi sits near the centrosome: cytoplasmic dyneins pull it inward toward the nucleus.

Motor proteins: kinesins and dyneins

  • Three motor superfamilies: kinesins and dyneins (on microtubules) and myosins (on actin). No motor uses intermediate filaments.
  • Motors turn ATP chemical energy into mechanical work. A chemical cycle (bind ATP, hydrolyse, release ADP + Pᵢ, bind new ATP) is coupled to a mechanical cycle of conformational changes, stepping from site to site in one direction.
  • Kinesin and cytoplasmic dynein are usually dimers: two globular ATPase heads that bind the microtubule stereospecifically, and a tail that binds cargo (directly or via adaptors) and decides what is carried.
  • Kinesin walks hand over hand: ATP hydrolysis and Pᵢ release loosen the rear head; ADP release and ATP binding by the front head swing the rear head forward.

Cilia and flagella

  • Many cilia line the respiratory tract; each beats with a power stroke then a recovery stroke. Flagella propel cells with wave-like motion.
  • Core (axoneme): a “9 + 2” array: nine outer doublets, each with two rows of ciliary dynein arms reaching the next doublet, around two central microtubules.
  • Isolated doublets exposed to ATP slide past each other; in the intact axoneme flexible protein links tie them, so sliding becomes bending.
In the clinic: Kartagener’s syndrome

A form of primary ciliary dyskinesia from hereditary defects in ciliary dynein (many genes can be involved), with cilia immotile or dysmotile, plus situs inversus totalis. Features: frequent respiratory, sinus and ear infections and chronic nasal congestion (cilia cannot clear bacteria and debris); men are infertile because sperm are nonmotile.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • GDP-tubulin binds weakly: losing the GTP cap causes shrinkage.
  • Taxol stabilises microtubules, yet still arrests mitosis.
  • In axons, plus ends point to the terminal.
  • No motor protein moves on intermediate filaments.
  • Kartagener’s: ciliary dynein defect + situs inversus.
  • In the intact axoneme, dynein causes bending, not sliding.
