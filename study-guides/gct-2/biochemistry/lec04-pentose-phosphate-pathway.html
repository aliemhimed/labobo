Pentose Phosphate Pathway and NADPH

The pathway that makes NADPH and ribose 5-phosphate, what NADPH is used for (biosynthesis, glutathione, cytochrome P450, the respiratory burst, nitric oxide), and why G6PD deficiency causes hemolytic anemia.

Labobo topic: Biochem Lec 4: Pentose Phosphate Pathway and NADPH

In 60 seconds

  • The pentose phosphate pathway (hexose monophosphate shunt) runs in the cytosol, makes no ATP and uses none, and is the body’s main source of NADPH.
  • Oxidative phase (irreversible): glucose 6-P → ribulose 5-P + CO₂ (from C1) + 2 NADPH. G6PD is the rate-limiting step, inhibited by NADPH; insulin induces it.
  • Nonoxidative phase (reversible): transketolase (2-C, needs TPP) and transaldolase (3-C) interconvert ribose 5-P and glycolytic intermediates.
  • NADPH is for reductive biosynthesis (fatty acids, cholesterol, steroids), glutathione reductase, cytochrome P450, NADPH oxidase and NO synthase.
  • G6PD deficiency: X-linked, the commonest disease-causing enzyme defect (>400 million people); oxidant drugs, fava beans or infection → Heinz bodies and hemolysis; protects against falciparum malaria.

Overview

The pentose phosphate pathway, or hexose monophosphate shunt, does two jobs: it is the body’s main source of NADPH, a biochemical reductant, and it provides ribose 5-phosphate for nucleotides. It also converts pentoses into glycolytic intermediates.

  • All reactions are in the cytosol.
  • An irreversible oxidative phase is followed by reversible sugar–phosphate interconversions, whose direction depends on supply and demand.
  • The pathway neither produces nor consumes ATP.

Irreversible oxidative reactions

Three irreversible reactions turn each glucose 6-phosphate into ribulose 5-phosphate, CO₂ and two NADPH.

#EnzymeReaction
1Glucose 6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD)Glucose 6-P → 6-phosphogluconolactone; NADP⁺ → NADPH. Committed, rate-limiting, regulated step
26-Phosphogluconolactone hydrolaseLactone → 6-phosphogluconate
36-Phosphogluconate dehydrogenaseOxidative decarboxylation → ribulose 5-P + CO₂ (carbon 1 of glucose) + the second NADPH

Regulation of G6PD

  • NADPH is a potent competitive inhibitor; the NADPH/NADP⁺ ratio usually keeps G6PD largely inhibited. When NADPH is used, the ratio falls and flux rises.
  • Insulin increases expression of the G6PD gene, so flux rises in the absorptive (fed) state.

The oxidative phase matters most where NADPH is in demand: liver, lactating mammary gland and adipose tissue (fatty acid synthesis), testes, ovaries, placenta and adrenal cortex (steroid hormones) and red cells (reducing glutathione).

Reversible nonoxidative reactions

These run in all cells that make nucleotides and nucleic acids, interconverting sugars of 3 to 7 carbons.

  • Ribose 5-phosphate isomerase: ribulose 5-P → ribose 5-P; phosphopentose epimerase: ribulose 5-P → xylulose 5-P.
  • Transketolase moves 2-carbon units; it needs thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP).
  • Transaldolase moves 3-carbon units.
  • Net effect: 5C + 5C ⇌ 7C + 3C ⇌ 4C + 6C, linking the pathway to glycolysis (fructose 6-P, glyceraldehyde 3-P).
Cell needsWhat the pathway does
Mostly NADPHOxidative phase makes NADPH; transketolase and transaldolase turn the ribulose 5-P into fructose 6-P and glyceraldehyde 3-P
Mostly ribose 5-PNonoxidative reactions make ribose 5-P from fructose 6-P and glyceraldehyde 3-P, without the oxidative steps
BothOxidative phase alone: NADPH plus ribulose 5-P → ribose 5-P

TPP is also needed by pyruvate dehydrogenase, α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase and branched-chain α-keto acid dehydrogenase.

NADPH and reductive biosynthesis

NADPH differs from NADH only by a phosphate on one ribose, which lets it work with NADPH-specific enzymes. In liver cytosol, NADP⁺/NADPH is about 0.1 (favouring reduction), while NAD⁺/NADH is about 1,000 (favouring oxidation).

NADPH’s electrons go to reductive biosynthesis, not to the electron transport chain: fatty acids, cholesterol and steroid hormones.

Reduction of H₂O₂: glutathione

Reactive oxygen species (ROS), such as superoxide, H₂O₂ and the hydroxyl radical, form from the partial reduction of O₂: as by-products of aerobic metabolism, from drugs and toxins, or when antioxidants are low (oxidative stress). They damage DNA, proteins and unsaturated lipids and are linked to reperfusion injury, cancer, inflammation and ageing.

  1. Glutathione peroxidase uses reduced glutathione (G-SH) to reduce H₂O₂ to water, forming oxidised glutathione (G-S-S-G).
  2. Glutathione reductase regenerates G-SH using NADPH. So NADPH indirectly supplies the electrons.

Glutathione is a tripeptide thiol, γ-glutamylcysteinylglycine (glutamate is joined through its γ-carboxyl). Superoxide dismutase and catalase remove other ROS.

Antioxidant chemicals (vitamin C, vitamin E, β-carotene) detoxify ROS in the laboratory, and diets rich in them are linked to less cancer, but supplement trials have failed; β-carotene even raised lung cancer in smokers.

Cytochrome P450 monooxygenases

Monooxygenases put one O atom into the substrate (a hydroxyl group) and reduce the other to water: R-H + O₂ + NADPH + H⁺ → R-OH + H₂O + NADP⁺. CYPs are a superfamily of heme proteins named for absorbance at 450 nm.

SystemLocationFunctions
MitochondrialInner mitochondrial membraneSteroid hormone synthesis (placenta, ovaries, testes, adrenal cortex); bile acid synthesis and 25-hydroxylation of vitamin D in liver; 1-hydroxylation of vitamin D in kidney
MicrosomalSmooth ER, mainly liverDetoxification of xenobiotics (drugs, petroleum products, pesticides), e.g. CYP3A4: phase I hydroxylation, often followed by phase II conjugation (glucuronic acid)

Electrons flow NADPH → FAD → FMN of the reductase → heme iron of the CYP. Gene polymorphisms cause differences in drug metabolism.

Phagocytosis and microbe killing

Neutrophils and monocytes/macrophages ingest microbes by receptor-mediated endocytosis and kill them by oxygen-independent (pH, lysosomal enzymes) and oxygen-dependent mechanisms. The myeloperoxidase (MPO) system is the most potent.

  1. After an antibody-coated bacterium is taken in, membrane NADPH oxidase reduces O₂ to superoxide (O₂⁻) using NADPH: the respiratory burst.
  2. Superoxide → H₂O₂, spontaneously or by superoxide dismutase.
  3. MPO (a heme lysosomal enzyme) turns H₂O₂ + Cl⁻ into HOCl (hypochlorous acid, bleach), which kills bacteria. H₂O₂ can also form the hydroxyl radical, or be removed by catalase or glutathione peroxidase.
In the clinic: chronic granulomatous disease

Rare genetic defects of NADPH oxidase cause severe, persistent infections and granulomas that wall off surviving bacteria. MPO deficiency, by contrast, does not increase infections, because peroxide from NADPH oxidase is still bactericidal.

Nitric oxide synthesis

NO is the endothelium-derived relaxing factor: a vasodilator, neurotransmitter, inhibitor of platelet aggregation and macrophage weapon. Its half-life is only 3–10 seconds; it reacts with O₂ to form nitrates, nitrites and peroxynitrite (a reactive nitrogen species). Do not confuse it with nitrous oxide (N₂O, “laughing gas”).

NO synthase (cytosolic) uses arginine, O₂ and NADPH, with FMN, FAD, heme and tetrahydrobiopterin, and makes NO + citrulline.

IsozymeTypeWhere and role
eNOSConstitutive, Ca²⁺–calmodulin-dependentEndothelium: low NO for vasodilation
nNOSConstitutive, Ca²⁺–calmodulin-dependentNeural tissue: neurotransmission
iNOSInducible, Ca²⁺-independentMacrophages, neutrophils: large amounts over hours to days, induced by TNF-α, IFN-γ and bacterial LPS
  • Vascular smooth muscle: NO activates cytosolic guanylyl cyclase → cGMP → protein kinase G → less Ca²⁺ entry → less myosin light-chain kinase activity → relaxation. Nitroglycerin works by being turned into NO.
  • In hypoxia, nitrite can be reduced to NO, which binds deoxyhemoglobin and is released to dilate vessels.
  • In macrophages, NO combines with other radicals to give the bactericidal hydroxyl radical.

G6PD deficiency

Without enough G6PD, red cells cannot make enough NADPH to keep glutathione reduced, so H₂O₂ from oxidant stress damages them. Red cells are hit hardest because the pentose phosphate pathway is their only NADPH source, and having no nucleus or ribosomes they cannot replace unstable enzyme. Other tissues also make NADPH with malic enzyme.

  • Oxidised hemoglobin sulfhydryl groups denature into Heinz bodies on the membrane; rigid red cells are removed by macrophages in the spleen and liver.
  • X-linked; affects mostly males (hemizygous). The most common disease-producing enzyme abnormality: over 400 million people, mainly with Middle Eastern, tropical African, Asian and Mediterranean ancestry.
  • Other features: neonatal jaundice at 1–4 days (unconjugated bilirubin); severe forms may shorten life. It gives resistance to falciparum malaria.

Triggers (in an otherwise symptom-free person)

TriggerDetail
Oxidant drugsMostly categories beginning with “A”: some antibiotics (especially sulfa drugs), antimalarials, analgesics, antipyretics
FavismFava (broad) beans, especially with the Mediterranean variant. Not everyone with G6PD deficiency has favism, but everyone with favism has G6PD deficiency
InfectionThe most common trigger: radicals from inflamed macrophages diffuse into red cells

Variants

More than 400 variants are known, mostly missense point mutations that lower activity, stability or binding of NADP⁺ or glucose 6-P. No large deletions or frameshifts are known: total absence is probably lethal. Severity follows residual activity.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • The pathway makes no ATP.
  • G6PD is inhibited by its product NADPH, not by ATP.
  • Ribose 5-P can be made without the oxidative phase, from glycolytic intermediates.
  • Thiamine deficiency affects transketolase, not transaldolase.
  • Red cells are most affected in G6PD deficiency because they have no other NADPH source and cannot make new enzyme.
  • In G6PD A⁻ hemolysis stops on its own because young red cells still have enough enzyme.
  • β-Carotene supplements increased lung cancer in smokers.

Key terms

Hexose monophosphate shunt: another name for the pentose phosphate pathway · G6PD: rate-limiting enzyme making the first NADPH · Ribulose 5-phosphate: pentose product of the oxidative phase · Transketolase: TPP enzyme moving 2-carbon units · Transaldolase: enzyme moving 3-carbon units · NADPH: reductant for biosynthesis and detoxification · Reactive oxygen species: partially reduced O₂ such as superoxide, H₂O₂, hydroxyl radical · Glutathione: γ-glutamylcysteinylglycine, cellular thiol antioxidant · Glutathione peroxidase: reduces H₂O₂ using G-SH · Glutathione reductase: regenerates G-SH with NADPH · Monooxygenase: adds one O atom to a substrate · Xenobiotic: foreign compound such as a drug or pollutant · Respiratory burst: rapid O₂ use by NADPH oxidase in phagocytes · Myeloperoxidase: makes HOCl from H₂O₂ and Cl⁻ · Chronic granulomatous disease: NADPH oxidase deficiency · NO synthase: makes NO and citrulline from arginine · Heinz bodies: denatured hemoglobin inclusions · Favism: hemolysis after eating fava beans.
