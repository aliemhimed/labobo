Hemostasis: Plasma Proteins, Coagulation and Fibrinolysis

How plasma proteins hold water in the blood, how platelets and the coagulation cascade build a clot only where a vessel is damaged, how thrombin, protein C and antithrombin keep it in check, and how plasmin dissolves it.

Labobo topic: Biochem Lec 7: Hemostasis

In 60 seconds

  • Albumin is about 60% of plasma protein but gives 70–80% of plasma osmotic pressure; too little protein (kwashiorkor) → edema.
  • Adhesion: GPIa binds collagen, GPIb binds vWF, which exposes GPIIb/IIIa; fibrinogen on GPIIb/IIIa links platelets. ADP is essential for aggregation.
  • Tissue factor + VIIa activates X and IX; Xa + Va (with Ca²⁺ on platelet phospholipid) turns prothrombin into thrombin; thrombin makes fibrin (soft clot) and activates XIII (hard clot).
  • Vitamin K → γ-carboxyglutamate on II, VII, IX, X, protein C, protein S; these bind Ca²⁺ and the platelet membrane. Warfarin blocks vitamin K epoxide reductase.
  • Thrombin amplifies itself (V, VIII, XI, XIII) but, bound to thrombomodulin, activates protein C, which with protein S destroys Va and VIIIa.
  • Antithrombin III (a serpin) inactivates thrombin, Xa, IXa, XIa; heparin speeds it up catalytically.
  • t-PA turns fibrin-bound plasminogen into plasmin; free plasmin is killed by α2-antiplasmin. Hemophilia A = factor VIII, B = factor IX, both X-linked.

Plasma proteins and water balance

Plasma is about 25% of the body’s extracellular fluid and has the same electrolytes as other extracellular fluids. Its proteins keep water in the blood, transport substances, defend against infection and keep the circulation sealed by clotting.

Starling’s forces

PressureArteriolar endVenous end
Capillary hydrostatic pressure~37 mm Hg~17 mm Hg
Plasma protein osmotic pressure~25 mm Hg~25 mm Hg
Tissue pressure~1 mm Hg~1 mm Hg
Net fluid movementOut of the capillaryBack into the capillary

Most of the force that brings water back from the tissues is the osmotic pressure of the plasma proteins.

Albumin is the major plasma protein: about 60% of total plasma protein, but because it is small (69 kDa) it gives 70–80% of plasma osmotic pressure.

In the clinic: kwashiorkor and analbuminemia

In kwashiorkor (protein deficiency with enough calories) albumin synthesis falls early, plasma osmotic pressure drops, and fluid stays in the interstitial space: edema, the distended bellies of famine victims.

People born without albumin (analbuminemia, <1 per million) have only moderate edema, because other plasma proteins rise to compensate.

Transport and defence proteins

The liver makes most plasma binding proteins. Many drugs also bind albumin, which lowers their free concentration and lengthens their time in the blood, so doses may need changing when plasma protein is abnormal.

Immune defence

  • Immunoglobulins are secreted by plasma cells (differentiated B lymphocytes).
  • Complement (about 20 proteins) is activated by antigen–antibody complexes, or by bacterial polysaccharides with C3b. The cascade releases peptides that drive inflammation, attract phagocytes and clear immune complexes.

Protease inhibitors

α1-Antiproteinase (α1-antitrypsin) is the main serine protease inhibitor of plasma; with α2-macroglobulin it limits damage from proteases released by neutrophils. At an infection site, neutrophil HOCl (from myeloperoxidase) inactivates these inhibitors so the proteases can work.

In the clinic: α1-antiproteinase deficiency

A point mutation in exon 5 reduces secretion of α1-antiproteinase from the liver. Neutrophil elastase in the lungs is not neutralised, alveolar elasticity is lost: emphysema. Cigarette smoke oxidises Met-358, the residue needed to bind proteases, so smoking raises the risk further.

The platelet

Platelets are non-nucleated cells that plug injured vessels and secrete regulators of clotting and repair. They bud off megakaryocytes in the bone marrow: the megakaryocyte copies its nucleus without dividing, and at about the eight-nucleus stage its granular cytoplasm releases about 4,000 platelets.

The resting platelet’s membrane folds deep inside it (the open canalicular system), adding surface for clotting reactions. On activation, Ca²⁺-dependent actin/myosin changes throw out long pseudopodia.

Forming the hemostatic plug

Platelets do three things: adhesion, aggregation and secretion. Adhesion starts platelet activation, which leads to the other two.

Von Willebrand factor

vWF is a large multimeric glycoprotein (subunit 220,000 Da; circulating forms 500–20,000 kDa) made by endothelial cells and megakaryocytes. It sits in the subendothelial matrix, in platelet granules, and in plasma bound to factor VIII, which it stabilises and protects. Large multimers released from endothelium are trimmed by a plasma metalloprotease (ADAMTS13); large multimers bind platelets best.

Adhesion and activation

  1. Injury exposes collagen and matrix-bound vWF.
  2. GPIa (integrin α2β1) binds collagen: the platelet changes from a flat disc to a sphere with long pseudopods.
  3. GPIb binds subendothelial vWF, which exposes binding sites on GPIIb/IIIa (integrin αIIbβ3) for fibrinogen and vWF.
  4. Adhered platelets release dense and α-granules. ADP (also from damaged red cells) binds its platelet receptor and unmasks more GPIIb/IIIa. Aggregation cannot happen without ADP, which swells activated platelets and brings them together.
  5. Fibrinogen binds activated platelets and links them into an aggregate.

Fibrinogen is two triple helices joined at their N-terminal ends by disulfide bonds. Its terminal peptides carry negative glutamate and aspartate residues that repel each other and stop aggregation. Thrombin cuts these off; the fibrin monomers polymerise into a soft clot. Thrombin is also a potent platelet activator through its own receptor.

In response to collagen and thrombin, platelets also release vasoconstrictors, serotonin (dense granules) and thromboxane A₂, which reduce blood flow to the area, and PDGF, which stimulates proliferation of vascular cells.

The coagulation factors

All are present in plasma as zymogens (proproteins), activated by cleavage at one or more sites; an activated factor gets an “a” (IX → IXa). The key to proper clotting is regulating the proteases that activate them.

  • Serine proteases (active-site serine cuts peptide bonds): VII, IX, X, XI and prothrombin. Thrombin has the same Asp–His–Ser catalytic triad as trypsin and chymotrypsin.
  • Cofactors: tissue factor is an integral membrane protein that needs no cleavage; V and VIII are procofactors activated by cleavage.
  • Regulators: protein C (cleaved; becomes a serine protease), protein S (not cleaved), thrombomodulin (endothelial receptor for thrombin).

The process of coagulation

“Extrinsic” and “intrinsic” are historical names: the two pathways overlap, but the terms remain.

  1. Damaged tissue presents tissue factor. Circulating factor VII binds it and is autocatalytically activated to VIIa.
  2. VIIa activates factor X (extrinsic) and factor IX (intrinsic); IXa also activates X. Both routes end in Xa.
  3. Xa converts prothrombin to thrombin. This is slow at first because little Va and VIIIa exist.
  4. Thrombin speeds its own production by activating V, VIII and XI and stimulating platelet degranulation. The intrinsic pathway sustains what the extrinsic pathway starts; in the book’s scheme factor XI is activated by thrombin.
  5. Thrombin cleaves fibrinogen; fibrin monomers polymerise spontaneously into a soft clot (not cross-linked).
  6. Thrombin activates XIII → XIIIa, which cross-links fibrin into a hard clot.

Cross-linking of fibrin

Factor XIIIa catalyses a transamidation between glutamine and lysine side chains of neighbouring fibrin monomers. The three-dimensional network resists mechanical and proteolytic damage and traps platelets and other cells.

Watch out

Factor XIIIa is the only enzyme of the cascade that is not a serine protease. Also note that the book names no contact (factor XII) step: if your slides start the intrinsic pathway with factor XII, answer from the book for this lecture.

Factor complexes and vitamin K

The key steps happen in complexes on the surface of aggregated platelets, which act as a scaffold.

  • Factors VII, IX, X and prothrombin carry γ-carboxyglutamate (Gla); prothrombin and X have 10 or more each. Gla binds Ca²⁺, and Ca²⁺ bridges to the negatively charged phospholipids of the platelet membrane.
  • Va binds both Xa and prothrombin; prothrombin changes shape and is cut faster. This complex speeds thrombin formation 10,000–15,000-fold.
  • VIIIa does the same for IXa and its substrate factor X. Tissue factor binds VIIa directly in the membrane.
  • Complex assembly raises thrombin formation by up to several hundred thousand-fold and keeps it at the injury, because the inner-leaflet phospholipids are exposed only where cells have ruptured.

The vitamin K cycle (in the hepatocyte)

  1. Vitamin K (quinone) is reduced to KH₂ by a microsomal quinone reductase.
  2. The carboxylase uses KH₂ to add a carboxyl group to glutamate residues of the proenzyme; vitamin K becomes vitamin K epoxide.
  3. Vitamin K epoxide reductase reduces the epoxide to the quinone, then a reductase regenerates KH₂.
FormSource
PhylloquinoneGreen leaves
MenaquinoneMade by intestinal bacteria
MenadioneConverted by humans to an active form

Warfarin resembles vitamin K, competes with it, and blocks the vitamin K epoxide reductase complex (VKOR), so factors II, VII, IX, X and proteins C and S are not carboxylated. The uncarboxylated precursors rise in the blood but cannot bind Ca²⁺ or their membrane sites. Warfarin is slow- and long-acting, and is also a rat poison.

Proteins C and S

Activated protein C (APC) forms a complex with protein S, which anchors it to the clot through Ca²⁺/Gla binding to platelet phospholipids. The APC complex cleaves Va and VIIIa, lowering thrombin production, and makes endothelium secrete more PGI₂, which reduces platelet aggregation. Deficiency of protein C or S raises the risk of venous thromboembolism; homozygotes do not survive the neonatal period without replacement therapy.

In the clinic: factor V Leiden

A point mutation replaces Arg with Gln at the preferred APC cleavage site, so factor Va resists APC. Risk of deep vein thrombosis: heterozygotes 6–8×, homozygotes 30–140×. No extra arterial risk except in young women who smoke. Inherited APC resistance is the most common risk factor for familial thrombosis in the developed world.

Serpins and antithrombin III

Serpins (serine protease inhibitors) make up about 10% of plasma protein. Each has a reactive site that looks like an ideal substrate, so it traps its protease in a tight complex.

  • Antithrombin III: one ATIII irreversibly inactivates one thrombin (an arginine of ATIII reacts with thrombin’s active-site serine). It also inactivates XIa, IXa and Xa, but not VIIa or activated protein C.
  • Heparin (a glycosaminoglycan from mast cell granules) binds lysyl residues of ATIII, changes its shape so the arginine is exposed, and is released afterwards: its action is catalytic.
  • Thrombin bound to a surface such as thrombomodulin is not readily attacked by ATIII.

Why normal endothelium does not clot

Intact endothelium neither activates coagulation nor lets platelets adhere (a non-thrombogenic surface):

  • it is strongly negatively charged, repelling negatively charged platelets;
  • it makes PGI₂ and nitric oxide, vasodilators that strongly inhibit platelet aggregation (PGI₂ synthesis is stimulated by thrombin, epinephrine and local injury);
  • it makes thrombomodulin and heparan sulfate, which inhibit thrombin; heparan sulfate potentiates ATIII, less efficiently than heparin.

Fibrinolysis and its regulation

Plasmin, a serine protease, is the only protease that can dissolve fibrin clots. It forms from plasminogen, which has a high affinity for fibrin and is built into the growing clot.

t-PA (tissue plasminogen activator)Made mainly by vascular endothelium; high affinity for fibrin; the main activator
u-PA (single-chain urokinase)Made by most cells; moderate affinity for fibrin

Activation of plasminogen at the clot surface is far more efficient than in the liquid blood. Fibrin-bound plasmin is protected from α2-antiplasmin until fibrin is dissolved and it is set free. This dissolves pathological or oversized clots while sparing circulating fibrinogen: the fibrin network both starts and regulates fibrinolysis. Physical stress, hypoxia and many small organic compounds increase t-PA and u-PA release.

In the clinic: clot-dissolving drugs

Streptokinase and t-PA were approved for myocardial infarction; cardiac catheterisation is now preferred where available. t-PA is also used for acute stroke and massive pulmonary embolism; streptokinase is rarely used now.

Hemophilia

Hemophilia A (factor VIII deficiency) is the most frequent serious inherited coagulation disorder, in about 1 in 5,000 males, inherited X-linked. Factor VIII orients factor IXa and factor X so X can be activated.

  • Signs: bleeding into soft tissues (hematomas) and body spaces; repeated bleeding into joints (hemarthrosis) can deform and stiffen the joint.
  • Treatment: factor VIII, once from many donors (plasma level only 0.3 nM vs 8,800 nM for fibrinogen; in the 1980s many patients caught HIV and hepatitis C), now recombinant. Emicizumab (approved 2017) is a bispecific antibody that grips factor IXa with one arm and factor X with the other, replacing factor VIII; it has no on/off switch.
  • Hemophilia B: mutations in factor IX, also X-linked.
  • Factor VIII assays: a clotting assay (patient plasma added to factor VIII-deficient plasma) or a chromogenic assay (IXa, Ca²⁺, phospholipid and X; Xa cleaves a coloured substrate).

In the clinic: heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT)

Heparin binds platelet factor 4 (PF4); the changed complex is seen as foreign, antibodies form, bind platelets and activate them: thrombosis with a low platelet count. Stop heparin and use another antithrombotic.

In the clinic: VKORC1 mutations

Most mutations cause warfarin resistance. One, R98W (Arg → Trp in an ER-retention signal), loses the protein from the ER and causes a bleeding disorder, combined deficiency of vitamin K-dependent factors type 2, treated with extra vitamin K.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • Albumin is “only” 60% of plasma protein but 70–80% of the osmotic pressure: the difference is its small size.
  • vWF deficiency affects both platelet adhesion and coagulation, because factor VIII is cleared without vWF.
  • Warfarin blocks the anticoagulants C and S as well as II, VII, IX and X.
  • Thrombin is both pro- and antithrombotic; which one depends on whether it is bound to thrombomodulin.
  • ATIII–heparin does not inactivate VIIa or activated protein C.
  • HIT gives a low platelet count with clots, not bleeding.
  • An inactivating mutation of protein C causes thrombosis; of VIII or IX causes bleeding.

    2. Factor VIII
    3. Large vWF multimers
    4. Plasminogen

    2. A transglutaminase
    3. A procofactor
    4. An integral membrane receptor

    2. Vitamin K epoxide reductase
    3. Microsomal quinone reductase
    4. Transglutaminase

    2. Serine
    3. Arginine
    4. Methionine

    2. Heparan sulfate
    3. Nitric oxide
    4. Tissue factor
