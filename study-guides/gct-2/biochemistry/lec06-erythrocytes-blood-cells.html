Erythrocytes and Other Blood Cells

The cells of the blood, how anemias are classified, how the red cell makes energy and protects itself, methemoglobin, G6PD and pyruvate kinase deficiency, heme and iron, the red cell membrane, how blood cells are made, and the hemoglobinopathies.

Labobo topic: Biochem Lec 6: Erythrocytes and Other Blood Cells

In 60 seconds

  • Red cells have no organelles: ATP comes only from glycolysis; the Rapoport–Luebering shunt makes 2,3-BPG (4–5 mM); the HMP shunt (5–10% of glucose) makes NADPH for glutathione.
  • NADH from glycolysis reduces methemoglobin (Fe³⁺) back to hemoglobin (Fe²⁺) via cytochrome b₅ reductase. Congenital methemoglobinemia: b₅ reductase deficiency or HbM; acquired: nitrites, aniline, sulfonamides; treat with methylene blue or vitamin C.
  • PK deficiency: ATP falls ~50%, cells lose K⁺ and water and become rigid → hemolysis, eased by a 2- to 3-fold rise in 2,3-BPG.
  • G6PD deficiency: X-linked, the commonest enzyme deficiency; oxidant stress (e.g. primaquine) → hemolysis.
  • Anemia by morphology: microcytic hypochromic (iron, thalassemia, lead), macrocytic (B₁₂, folate), normocytic (blood loss, sickle cell, enzyme and membrane defects). MCV normal 80–100 fL.
  • Heme: glycine + succinyl CoA → δ-ALA (δ-ALA synthase, PLP, rate-limiting, inhibited by heme) → … → ferrochelatase adds Fe²⁺. Lead blocks δ-ALA dehydratase and ferrochelatase.
  • Membrane: spectrin, ankyrin, band 3, band 4.1; defects → spherocytes destroyed in the spleen.

Cells of the blood

All blood cells come from hematopoietic stem cells in the bone marrow, on demand: leukocytes release interleukins in infection, and low tissue O₂ makes the kidney release erythropoietin.

Anemia and its classification

A patient is anemic when hemoglobin falls below normal (13.5–17.5 g/dL in men, 11.5–15.5 g/dL in women). A complete blood count, done by flow cytometry, gives red cell count, hemoglobin, hematocrit, MCV and the white cell count and differential.

  • MCV (mean corpuscular volume): normal 80–100 fL; <80 microcytic, >100 macrocytic.
  • MCHC (mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration): normal 32–37; <32 hypochromic.
MorphologyDefectCauses
Microcytic, hypochromicImpaired hemoglobin synthesisIron deficiency, thalassemia, lead poisoning
Macrocytic, normochromicImpaired DNA synthesisVitamin B₁₂ or folate deficiency, erythroleukemia
Normocytic, normochromicRed cell lossAcute bleeding, sickle cell disease, red cell enzyme and membrane defects

Erythrocyte metabolism

Mature red cells have no organelles, so all their enzymes are cytoplasmic. They make ATP only by glycolysis, using it for ion pumps (Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺), phosphorylation of membrane proteins and the priming steps of glycolysis.

PathwayProductPurpose
Glycolysis → lactateATP, NADHEnergy; lactate regenerates NAD⁺
Rapoport–Luebering shunt2,3-BPG (4–5 mM in red cells, trace elsewhere)Stabilises deoxyhemoglobin, releasing O₂ to tissues
HMP shunt (5–10% of glucose)NADPHKeeps glutathione reduced: chief defence against ROS
Cytochrome b₅ reductaseUses NADHReduces methemoglobin to hemoglobin

The trace 2,3-BPG in other cells is a catalytic cofactor of phosphoglycerate mutase in glycolysis.

Methemoglobin

Hemoglobin binds O₂ only with iron in the ferrous (Fe²⁺) state. ROS oxidise it to ferric (Fe³⁺) methemoglobin. The NADH–cytochrome b₅ methemoglobin reductase system reverses this: cytochrome b₅ reduces the Fe³⁺, and a flavin enzyme, cytochrome b₅ reductase (methemoglobin reductase), re-reduces cytochrome b₅ with NADH.

TypeCauseNotes
CongenitalCytochrome b₅ reductase deficiency, or hemoglobin M (a substitution in the heme pocket stabilises Fe³⁺)Patients look cyanotic but have few problems
AcquiredOxidants: nitrites, quinones, aniline, sulfonamidesTreat with reducing agents: ascorbic acid or methylene blue

Pyruvate kinase deficiency

Inherited red-cell PK deficiency causes hemolytic anemia (hemoglobin often 4–10 g/dL).

  1. ATP from glycolysis can fall by about 50%, so ion pumps fail.
  2. Cells gain Ca²⁺ and lose K⁺ and water; hemoglobin becomes concentrated and the cell viscous and rigid.
  3. Rigid cells are damaged by shear forces and removed: hemolysis.

The anemia is softened by a 2- to 3-fold rise in 2,3-BPG (intermediates pile up above the PK block), which lowers hemoglobin’s O₂ affinity so the remaining cells unload O₂ efficiently.

G6PD deficiency

G6PD runs the first step of the HMP shunt. Red cells have no ribosomes and cannot make new G6PD, so red cell lifetime tracks its activity: as it falls, oxidative damage builds up and the cell lyses. When hemolysis outpaces production, hemolytic anemia results.

  • The most common enzyme deficiency in humans, kept common by resistance to malaria.
  • X-linked: in the Korean War about 10% of soldiers of African ancestry given the antimalarial primaquine developed anemia.
  • All known variants are small in-frame deletions or missense mutations; complete absence is thought to be embryonic lethal.

Heme synthesis and degradation

Heme is a porphyrin ring (four pyrroles joined by methenyl bridges) holding one iron. Its eight side chains read M V M V M P P M (methyl, vinyl, propionate), the type III series. Heme is in hemoglobin, myoglobin and the cytochromes, including P450.

Synthesis (in red cell precursors)

  1. Glycine + succinyl CoA → δ-aminolevulinic acid (δ-ALA) by δ-ALA synthase, which needs pyridoxal phosphate (glycine is decarboxylated). Eight of each make one heme.
  2. δ-ALA dehydratase (zinc enzyme) joins two δ-ALA into porphobilinogen.
  3. Four porphobilinogens form a chain and then porphyrinogens; acetates → methyls, two propionates → vinyls (protoporphyrinogen), bridges oxidised → protoporphyrin IX.
  4. Ferrochelatase (heme synthase) inserts Fe²⁺ → heme.
  • Regulation: heme represses synthesis of δ-ALA synthase and inhibits it allosterically; heme also keeps the globin translation initiation complex active. Drugs like phenobarbital induce cytochrome P450, using up heme, so δ-ALA synthase is induced.
  • Lead inactivates δ-ALA dehydratase and ferrochelatase: δ-ALA and protoporphyrin IX accumulate, heme falls → anemia and less cytochrome for energy.
  • Vitamin B₆ deficiency slows the first step → microcytic hypochromic anemia with high iron stores.
  • Porphyrias: inherited deficiencies of pathway enzymes; intermediates are neurotoxic (neuropsychiatric symptoms) and porphyrins make the skin photosensitive.

Degradation

After about 120 days, red cells are taken up by the reticuloendothelial system. Globin → amino acids; iron → stores; heme is cleaved, releasing CO and iron, to biliverdin, reduced (NADPH) to bilirubin, which travels on albumin to the liver. There UDP-glucuronate makes bilirubin diglucuronide, excreted in bile. Gut bacteria make urobilinogen: some goes to urine, most is oxidised to stercobilin, colouring feces brown.

In the clinic: pigment gallstones

In hemolysis (sickle cell crisis, spherocytosis), unconjugated bilirubin can exceed the liver’s conjugating capacity, more of it reaches the bile, and it precipitates as calcium bilirubinate gallstones.

Iron

  • Intake: RDA 10 mg (men, postmenopausal women), 15 mg (premenopausal women); diet has 10–50 mg but only 10–15% is absorbed. Heme iron (meat) is absorbed well; plant iron less so (oxalates, phytates, tannins); vitamin C increases non-heme iron uptake.
  • Absorbed as Fe²⁺, oxidised to Fe³⁺ by ceruloplasmin (copper ferroxidase) for transport.
  • Transferrin (apotransferrin + Fe³⁺) carries iron; it is usually one-third saturated; total iron-binding capacity about 300 μg/dL. Cells take it up by receptor-mediated endocytosis; in the acidic endosome iron is reduced and leaves via DMT-1.
  • Ferritin stores iron (liver, spleen, marrow); serum ferritin is the most sensitive indicator of iron stores. Excess iron is stored as hemosiderin, which is hard to mobilise.
  • Men lose about 1 mg/day; premenopausal women lose more (menstruation, pregnancy). Iron-deficiency anemia in a man on a Western diet suggests GI bleeding (ulcer, colon cancer).
  • Mutation of DMT-1 (SLC11A2) traps iron in endosomes → refractory microcytic hypochromic anemia.

The red cell membrane

The red cell is a biconcave disc about 7.5 μm across, wider than many capillaries. It passes through the spleen about 120 times a day, squeezing through ~3 μm slits in about 30 s; non-deformable cells are trapped and destroyed there. Its surface (~140 μm²) is larger than a sphere would need (~98 μm²), so it can stretch.

ProteinRole
Spectrin (band 1)Main skeleton protein: α/β heterodimer; dimers join at their heads
AnkyrinLinks spectrin to band 3 (integral membrane protein)
Band 4.2Stabilises the ankyrin–band 3 link
Band 4.1Binds actin and glycophorin C, anchoring the spectrin–actin junction
ActinShort filaments where many spectrins meet

Under stress the spectrin network rearranges (some stretch, some compress), changing shape but not surface area. The red cell cannot make new membrane proteins or lipids, but exchanges lipids with lipoproteins; glutathione protects them from oxidation.

In the clinic: hereditary spherocytosis

Defects in skeleton proteins let shear forces strip pieces of membrane. The cells become spherocytes (small, dark, round), lose deformability and are destroyed in the spleen: hemolytic anemia with jaundice, splenomegaly, reticulocytosis and bilirubin gallstones.

Hematopoiesis and erythropoiesis

All lineages descend from self-renewing hematopoietic stem cells (only 1–10 per 10⁵ marrow cells). Differentiation is hierarchical; progenitors are colony-forming units (CFU), or burst-forming units (BFU) for very large colonies. Marrow stromal cells (fibroblasts, endothelial cells, adipocytes, macrophages) make matrix and growth factors. Leukemias arise when a differentiating cell stays immature and proliferative.

Cytokine signalling: JAK–STAT

  1. Cytokine binding aggregates its receptor and activates JAKs (cytoplasmic tyrosine kinases), which phosphorylate the receptor.
  2. STATs dock, are phosphorylated, dimerise and enter the nucleus to switch on genes; Ras/Raf/MAP kinase and anti-apoptosis pathways are also activated.
  3. Signals are switched off by SOCS proteins (block docking or JAKs), the phosphatase SHP-1 (dephosphorylates JAK2) and PIAS proteins (block STAT dimers).
In the clinic: signalling defects

X-linked SCID: the common γ-chain of the IL-2 receptor cannot activate JAK3, so no mature T cells form. An Epo receptor that cannot bind SHP-1 keeps JAK2 and STAT5 on, giving too many red cells.

Erythropoiesis

Low tissue O₂ → kidney releases erythropoietin. Stem cell → CFU-GEMM → BFU-E → CFU-E → normoblast, which divides four more times as its nucleus condenses, then extrudes it → reticulocyte (still has ribosomes and mRNA, makes hemoglobin), which circulates 1–2 days and matures in the spleen. We make about 10¹² red cells a day.

Hemoglobinopathies and fetal hemoglobin

More than 700 mutant hemoglobins are known, mostly single amino acid changes. Hereditary anemias are among the commonest genetic diseases because heterozygotes resist malaria.

DisorderKey facts
HbS (sickle cell)The commonest hemoglobin mutation; devastating in homozygotes
HbCGlu → Lys at the same position as HbS. Activates K⁺ efflux (cell dehydrates) and lowers solubility (precipitates, no sickling). Homozygotes: mild hemolytic anemia; heterozygotes: unaffected. HbS/HbC is worse than sickle trait because HbC raises HbS concentration
α-ThalassemiaUsually gene deletions; 4 α genes (2 on each chromosome 16). Loss of 1: minimal; 2: microcytic hypochromic, usually not anemic; 3: moderate anemia (Hb 7–10 g/dL), splenomegaly, HbH (β₄, high O₂ affinity, inclusions); 4: hydrops fetalis, fatal in utero. Two genes lost on one chromosome is commoner in Asians; one from each, in Africans
β-ThalassemiaDeletions, promoter and splice-junction mutations; β⁺ (some chains) or β⁰ (none). Excess α-chains precipitate in precursors: ineffective erythropoiesis and membrane damage (band 4.1). β⁰/β⁰ is severe

Fetal hemoglobin and switching

  • HbF (α₂γ₂) binds 2,3-BPG less than HbA, so it has higher O₂ affinity and takes O₂ from the mother.
  • Sites of erythropoiesis: yolk sac (~day 15) → liver (after 6 weeks; also spleen) → bone marrow (from just before birth; the sole site by 8–10 weeks after birth).
  • α-locus on chromosome 16 (ζ, α2, α1); β-locus on chromosome 11 (ε, Gγ, Aγ, δ, β), in order of expression. Embryonic: Gower 1 (ζ₂ε₂), Portland (ζ₂γ₂), Gower 2 (α₂ε₂); adult: HbA (α₂β₂), minor HbA₂ (α₂δ₂).
  • Hereditary persistence of fetal hemoglobin (HPFH): high HbF after birth, clinically normal. Non-deletion forms are γ-promoter point mutations; deletion forms remove δ and β, and enhancers then drive γ. High HbF eases sickle cell disease and β-thalassemia.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • Red cells make ATP only by glycolysis; PK deficiency hurts red cells but spares the liver.
  • Methemoglobin is reduced by NADH (from glycolysis), not NADPH, in the main system.
  • The most sensitive test of iron stores is serum ferritin.
  • Lead inhibits δ-ALA dehydratase and ferrochelatase, not δ-ALA synthase.
  • δ-ALA synthase is the rate-limiting step and needs vitamin B₆.
  • Reticulocytes still have ribosomes and mRNA; mature red cells do not.
  • HbF binds 2,3-BPG less, so it binds O₂ more tightly.

Key terms

Granulocyte: polymorphonuclear leukocyte with secretory granules · MCV: mean red cell volume · MCHC: mean hemoglobin concentration in red cells · Rapoport–Luebering shunt: glycolytic branch making 2,3-BPG · Methemoglobin: hemoglobin with Fe³⁺ · Cytochrome b₅ reductase: NADH enzyme reducing methemoglobin · Hemoglobin M: variant that stabilises Fe³⁺ · Porphyrin: ring of four pyrroles · δ-ALA synthase: PLP enzyme, first and rate-limiting step of heme synthesis · Ferrochelatase: inserts Fe²⁺ into protoporphyrin IX · Porphyria: inherited defect of heme synthesis · Transferrin: plasma iron carrier · Ferritin: iron storage protein · Hemosiderin: poorly mobilised excess iron store · DMT-1: endosomal iron exporter · Spectrin: main red cell skeleton protein · Spherocyte: small round red cell that has lost membrane · Erythropoietin: kidney hormone driving red cell production · Reticulocyte: young red cell with ribosomes · JAK–STAT: cytokine signalling pathway · Ineffective erythropoiesis: destruction of precursors in the marrow · Hemoglobin switching: developmental change of globin genes · HPFH: hereditary persistence of fetal hemoglobin.
