Carbohydrate Digestion and Glycolysis

How dietary carbohydrate is digested and absorbed, how glucose enters cells, the ten reactions that turn glucose into pyruvate, how the pathway is controlled, and what happens to pyruvate next, with arsenic poisoning, pyruvate kinase deficiency and lactic acidosis.

Labobo topic: Biochem Lec 1: Glycolysis

In 60 seconds

  • Starch is digested by salivary and pancreatic α-amylase, then brush-border disaccharidases; only monosaccharides are absorbed. Glucose and galactose enter by SGLT-1 (with Na⁺), fructose by GLUT-5, and all leave into the portal blood by GLUT-2.
  • Glucose enters other cells by GLUT facilitated diffusion (GLUT-4 is insulin-sensitive, in muscle and fat) or by Na⁺-dependent cotransport (SGLT).
  • Glycolysis: glucose → 2 pyruvate, net 2 ATP and 2 NADH. Three irreversible steps: hexokinase/glucokinase, PFK-1 (rate-limiting) and pyruvate kinase.
  • Fructose 2,6-bisphosphate is the strongest PFK-1 activator; insulin raises it, glucagon lowers it in the liver.
  • Without O₂ or mitochondria, pyruvate → lactate (regenerates NAD⁺). Shock and circulatory collapse cause lactic acidosis.
  • Arsenate replaces phosphate at glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase and cancels the ATP of that step; PK deficiency causes hemolytic anemia.

Carbohydrate digestion

Digestion happens mainly in the mouth and the intestinal lumen, by glycoside hydrolases (glycosidases) that cut glycosidic bonds. Because diets contain little free monosaccharide, the enzymes are mostly endoglycosidases (for polysaccharides) and disaccharidases. The end products are glucose, galactose and fructose.

  • Sucrase and isomaltase are one protein cut into two subunits: the sucrase–isomaltase (SI) complex. Maltase is part of the uncut maltase–glucoamylase; glucoamylase cuts α(1→4) bonds in dextrins.

Absorption and disaccharide intolerance

The upper jejunum absorbs most of the monosaccharides, each by its own transporter.

SugarInto the enterocyteOut to the portal blood
Glucose, galactoseSGLT-1: secondary active transport with Na⁺ (symport); the Na⁺ gradient comes from the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPaseGLUT-2
FructoseGLUT-5: Na⁺- and energy-independent

Only monosaccharides are absorbed. If a disaccharidase is missing, the sugar reaches the large intestine: it draws water in (osmotic diarrhea), and bacteria ferment it to small osmotically active compounds plus CO₂ and H₂ (cramps, flatulence).

DeficiencyKey facts
Lactose intoleranceOver 60% of adults worldwide; up to 90% of adults of African or Asian descent; least in Northern Europeans. Lactase falls from about age 2 because less enzyme is made (a regulatory region on chromosome 2). Treatment: less milk; yogurt, some cheeses and green vegetables for calcium; lactase-treated products or lactase pills

AcquiredIntestinal disease, malnutrition, mucosa-damaging drugs; brush-border enzymes are lost quickly in severe diarrhea

Diagnosis: oral tolerance tests with each disaccharide, and breath H₂, which measures carbohydrate fermented by gut bacteria instead of being absorbed.

Glucose transport into cells

Glucose cannot diffuse into cells. It uses one of two systems.

1. Na⁺- and ATP-independent: GLUTs

Fourteen GLUT isoforms (GLUT-1 to GLUT-14) carry one glucose at a time (uniporters) by facilitated diffusion, down its concentration gradient. Each GLUT has 12 transmembrane α-helices and switches between two shapes.

TransporterLocationFunction
GLUT-1Most tissues (red cells, blood–brain barrier)Basal glucose uptake
GLUT-2Liver, kidney, pancreatic β cellsMoves glucose in or out depending on blood glucose; high Km
GLUT-3Most tissues (neurons)Basal glucose uptake
GLUT-4Muscle and fatNumber in the membrane raised by insulin
GLUT-5Small intestine, testesTransports fructose

2. Na⁺- and ATP-dependent cotransport: SGLTs

In the intestinal epithelium, renal tubules and choroid plexus, glucose is moved against its gradient together with Na⁺ moving down its gradient. ATP is used indirectly, by the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase that keeps Na⁺ low inside the cell (secondary active transport).

In the clinic: SGLT2 inhibitors

SGLT2 in the kidney is the main transporter that reabsorbs glucose. Gliflozins block it, so more glucose is lost in the urine and blood glucose falls in type 2 diabetes.

Glycolysis overview

Glycolysis is used by all tissues to oxidise glucose for ATP and to make intermediates for other pathways. It is the hub of carbohydrate metabolism: almost every sugar can be turned into glucose.

Aerobic glycolysisAnaerobic glycolysis
End productPyruvateLactate
WhereCells with mitochondria and enough O₂Cells without mitochondria (red cells, parts of the eye) or without enough O₂
NADHReoxidised by the electron transport chain (needs O₂)Reoxidised by lactate dehydrogenase
NextPyruvate → acetyl CoA → TCA cycleLactate goes to the blood

The ten reactions fall into an energy-investment phase (reactions 1–5, ATP spent to make phosphorylated intermediates) and an energy-generation phase (reactions 6–10, ATP made by substrate-level phosphorylation).

Hexokinase and glucokinase

Phosphorylated sugars cannot cross membranes (no carriers, too polar), so phosphorylation traps glucose and commits it to the cell.

Hexokinase I–IIIGlucokinase (hexokinase IV)
WhereMost tissuesLiver parenchymal cells, pancreatic β cells (also hypothalamic neurons)
Km for glucoseLow (high affinity): works even at low glucoseHigh: works only when glucose is high (after a meal)
VmaxLow: does not trap more phosphate or glucose than the cell can useHigh: liver clears the flood of portal glucose
Inhibited byGlucose 6-phosphate (product)Not by glucose 6-P; indirectly by fructose 6-P via GKRP
RoleBasal glucose useGlucose sensor: sets the threshold for insulin secretion

GKRP (glucokinase regulatory protein): with fructose 6-phosphate present, glucokinase binds GKRP tightly and is moved into the nucleus, inactive. When glucose rises, glucokinase is released back to the cytosol. GKRP is a competitive inhibitor of glucose use by glucokinase.

In the clinic: MODY 2

Inactivating mutations of glucokinase cause maturity-onset diabetes of the young type 2: the β cell senses glucose poorly, so insulin secretion is impaired and blood glucose rises.

PFK-1: the main control point

  • Inhibited by high ATP (energy-rich signal) and citrate (favours glycogen synthesis).
  • Activated by AMP (energy depleted) and, most strongly, by fructose 2,6-bisphosphate, which works even when ATP is high.

PFK-2 is a bifunctional enzyme: its kinase makes fructose 2,6-bisphosphate and its phosphatase (FBP2) removes it. In the liver isozyme, phosphorylation turns the kinase off and the phosphatase on; the cardiac isozyme does the opposite, and skeletal muscle PFK-2 is not covalently regulated.

StateHormonesLiver PFK-2Fructose 2,6-bisPResult
Well fedInsulin ↑, glucagon ↓Dephosphorylated (kinase active)↑Glycolysis ↑
FastingGlucagon ↑, insulin ↓Phosphorylated (phosphatase active)↓Glycolysis ↓, gluconeogenesis ↑

Fructose 2,6-bisphosphate also inhibits fructose 1,6-bisphosphatase of gluconeogenesis, so the two pathways are not fully active together (no futile cycle).

Energy-generation phase

Each step happens twice per glucose (two glyceraldehyde 3-phosphates).

#ReactionEnzymeNotes
6Glyceraldehyde 3-P + Pᵢ + NAD⁺ → 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate + NADHGlyceraldehyde 3-P dehydrogenaseFirst oxidation; the new phosphate bond is high-energy
71,3-BPG + ADP → 3-phosphoglycerate + ATPPhosphoglycerate kinaseSubstrate-level phosphorylation; reversible, unlike most kinases; replaces the 2 ATP spent
83-PG ⇌ 2-phosphoglyceratePhosphoglycerate mutaseFreely reversible
92-PG → phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) + H₂OEnolaseMakes a high-energy enol phosphate; reversible; inhibited by fluoride
10PEP + ADP → pyruvate + ATPPyruvate kinaseThird irreversible step; substrate-level phosphorylation
  • Cells have little NAD⁺, so the NADH from step 6 must be reoxidised: by lactate dehydrogenase (anaerobic) or the electron transport chain (aerobic). NADH cannot cross the inner mitochondrial membrane, so the malate–aspartate and glycerol 3-phosphate shuttles carry its electrons in.
  • Fluoride in drinking water inhibits enolase in mouth bacteria, lowering their lactate and dental caries.

2,3-Bisphosphoglycerate in red cells

In red cells, bisphosphoglycerate mutase turns some 1,3-BPG into 2,3-BPG, which raises O₂ delivery; a phosphatase turns it into 3-phosphoglycerate. This shunt skips the ATP of step 7. 2,3-BPG is only a trace in other cells.

In the clinic: arsenic poisoning

Trivalent arsenic (arsenite) inhibits enzymes that need lipoic acid, such as the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex. Pentavalent arsenic (arsenate) competes with Pᵢ at glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase; the product hydrolyses spontaneously to 3-phosphoglycerate, so the 1,3-BPG step is bypassed: the pathway is not inhibited, but the cell loses the energy glycolysis normally yields. Arsenate also competes with Pᵢ at ATP synthase.

Pyruvate kinase and its deficiency

  • Feedforward activation: PK is activated by fructose 1,6-bisphosphate, the product of PFK-1, so the two kinases work together. PK is inhibited by ATP.
  • Covalent regulation in the liver only: glucagon → cAMP → PKA phosphorylates and inactivates hepatic PK, so PEP goes into gluconeogenesis instead. A phosphatase reactivates it.
  • Liver and red-cell PK come from the same gene, using different transcription start sites.
In the clinic: pyruvate kinase deficiency

Mature red cells have no mitochondria and depend entirely on glycolysis for ATP, which runs their ion pumps and keeps them flexible. Low PK activity (usually 5–35% of normal) means less ATP, so red cells change shape and are removed by splenic macrophages: chronic hemolytic anemia, from mild to transfusion-dependent. PK deficiency is the most common inherited glycolytic enzyme defect. The liver is spared because it can make more PK and use oxidative phosphorylation. Severity depends on how low the enzyme is and how much 2,3-BPG rises to compensate. Most mutant enzymes have altered kinetics or lower stability. Heterozygotes resist the most severe forms of malaria.

Lactate and lactic acidosis

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) reduces pyruvate to lactate and oxidises NADH to NAD⁺. Lactate is the end product of anaerobic glycolysis and the main fate of pyruvate in poorly vascularised tissue (lens, cornea, kidney medulla) and in red cells.

  • Exercising muscle: NADH production exceeds what the electron transport chain can handle, the NADH/NAD⁺ ratio rises and pyruvate → lactate. Lactate lowers intracellular pH (cramps) and passes into the blood; the liver can make glucose from it.
  • Liver and heart have a lower NADH/NAD⁺ ratio and oxidise lactate back to pyruvate. The liver makes glucose or acetyl CoA from it; the heart burns it completely to CO₂ and water.
In the clinic: lactic acidosis

A metabolic acidosis with high plasma lactate, when the circulation collapses: myocardial infarction, pulmonary embolism, uncontrolled hemorrhage, shock. Tissues get too little O₂, oxidative phosphorylation fails and cells survive on anaerobic glycolysis, making lactic acid. The extra O₂ needed to recover is the O₂ debt; blood lactate gives an early measure of it and of recovery.

Hormonal regulation

Allosteric and covalent control act within minutes to hours. Hormones also change how much enzyme is made: 10- to 20-fold changes over hours to days.

  • Carbohydrate-rich meals or insulin raise transcription of glucokinase, PFK-1 and PK in the liver (absorptive state). Insulin acts through SREBP-1c and glucose through ChREBP, which also switch on fatty acid synthesis genes.
  • High glucagon, low insulin (fasting, diabetes) lower expression of these three enzymes.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • The rate-limiting step is PFK-1, not hexokinase.
  • Glucokinase is not inhibited by glucose 6-phosphate; it is controlled by GKRP and fructose 6-phosphate.
  • Phosphoglycerate kinase makes ATP yet is reversible.
  • Liver PK is inactivated by phosphorylation (glucagon); red-cell PK is not covalently regulated.
  • Anaerobic glycolysis has no net NADH; aerobic glycolysis has 2.
  • Fructose 2,6-bisphosphate activates PFK-1 but inhibits fructose 1,6-bisphosphatase.
  • Lactase deficiency comes from less enzyme being made, not a faulty enzyme.

Key terms

Glycosidase: enzyme that hydrolyses glycosidic bonds · Dextrins: short branched and unbranched oligosaccharides left by α-amylase · Disaccharidase: brush-border enzyme splitting a disaccharide · SGLT: sodium-dependent glucose cotransporter · GLUT: glucose uniporter for facilitated diffusion · Facilitated diffusion: carrier-mediated transport down a gradient · Secondary active transport: uphill transport driven by an ion gradient · Glucokinase: hexokinase IV of liver and β cells · GKRP: protein that holds glucokinase inactive in the nucleus · Committed step: first irreversible step unique to a pathway · Bifunctional enzyme: one protein with two activities, like PFK-2/FBP2 · Substrate-level phosphorylation: ATP made from a high-energy substrate · Feedforward activation: activation by an earlier product of the pathway · 2,3-BPG: red-cell metabolite that releases O₂ from hemoglobin · Lactic acidosis: metabolic acidosis from excess lactate · O₂ debt: extra O₂ needed to recover from hypoxia · MODY 2: diabetes from glucokinase mutations · Gliflozin: SGLT2 inhibitor.
