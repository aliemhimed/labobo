Glycogen Metabolism

Why the liver and muscle store glycogen, how glycogen is built and broken down, how hormones and metabolites switch between the two, and the glycogen storage diseases.

Labobo topic: Biochem Lec 2: Glycogen Metabolism

In 60 seconds

  • Liver glycogen keeps blood glucose up early in a fast (less than 24 h); muscle glycogen fuels the muscle itself.
  • Glycogen: α-D-glucose in α(1→4) chains with α(1→6) branches every 8–14 residues, around a glycogenin core.
  • Synthesis: UDP-glucose → glycogen synthase (needs a primer) + branching enzyme (4:6 transferase).
  • Breakdown: glycogen phosphorylase (needs PLP) gives glucose 1-phosphate; debranching enzyme (4:4 transferase + α(1→6)-glucosidase) frees glucose at branches.
  • Only the liver has glucose 6-phosphatase, so only the liver releases glucose to the blood.
  • Glucagon/epinephrine → cAMP → PKA: phosphorylation activates phosphorylase but inactivates glycogen synthase. Insulin does the opposite.
  • GSDs: I von Gierke (glucose 6-phosphatase), II Pompe (lysosomal acid maltase), III Cori (debranching), IV Andersen (branching), V McArdle (muscle phosphorylase), VI Hers (liver phosphorylase).

Why store glycogen

Blood glucose is essential: the brain prefers it, and cells with few or no mitochondria (mature red cells) depend on it. Exercising muscle uses it for anaerobic glycolysis. Blood glucose comes from three sources:

SourceStrengthWeakness
DietStarch, disaccharides, monosaccharidesSporadic, unreliable
Glycogen degradationRapidly mobilisedLiver stores last less than a day of fasting
GluconeogenesisSustained (from glycerol, lactate, pyruvate, amino acids)Slow to respond to a falling glucose

Because muscle mass is larger, most of the body’s glycogen is in muscle. Synthesis and degradation go on all the time; the balance between them sets the store.

  • Glycogen is a branched polymer of α-D-glucose only.
  • Main bond α(1→4); a α(1→6) branch every 8–14 residues.
  • Up to 55,000 glucose residues per molecule, in large spherical cytoplasmic granules that also hold most of the enzymes.

Glycogenesis

Glycogen is made in the cytosol from α-D-glucose, using ATP (to phosphorylate glucose) and UTP.

  1. Glucose 6-phosphate ⇌ glucose 1-phosphate by phosphoglucomutase (glucose 1,6-bisphosphate is an obligatory intermediate).
  2. Glucose 1-P + UTP → UDP-glucose + PPᵢ by UDP–glucose pyrophosphorylase. Pyrophosphatase splits PPᵢ into 2 Pᵢ; this exergonic step drives the reaction forward. UDP-glucose donates every glucose added.
  3. Primer: glycogen synthase cannot start a chain. A glycogen fragment can serve, or glycogenin (a homodimer) attaches glucose to its own tyrosine-194 (autoglucosylation) and adds at least four more, making a short α(1→4) chain. Glycogenin stays at the core of the granule.
  4. Elongation: glycogen synthase adds glucose from UDP-glucose to the nonreducing end, making α(1→4) bonds (C1 of the new glucose to C4 of the chain). The UDP released is recharged to UTP by nucleoside diphosphate kinase.
  5. Branching: branching enzyme, amylo-α(1→4)→α(1→6)-transglycosylase, cuts a block of 6–8 residues off a chain end and reattaches it by an α(1→6) bond (a 4:6 transferase). Both the new and the old ends can now grow.

Branching makes glycogen far more soluble than unbranched amylose (plants) and multiplies the nonreducing ends, so glucose can be added and removed much faster.

Glycogenolysis

Breakdown is not the reverse of synthesis; it uses a separate set of cytosolic enzymes. The main product is glucose 1-phosphate; each branch point gives one free glucose.

  1. Chain shortening: glycogen phosphorylase cuts α(1→4) bonds at the nonreducing ends by phosphorolysis (adding Pᵢ), releasing glucose 1-phosphate, until four residues remain before a branch. The result is a limit dextrin. Phosphorylase needs pyridoxal phosphate (vitamin B₆).
  2. Branch removal by one bifunctional debranching enzyme: its oligo-α(1→4)→α(1→4)-glucantransferase moves the outer three of the four residues onto another chain end (a 4:4 transferase); its amylo-α(1→6)-glucosidase then hydrolyses the last α(1→6)-linked glucose, releasing free glucose.
  3. Glucose 1-P → glucose 6-P by phosphoglucomutase.
  4. Liver only: glucose 6-phosphate enters the ER by glucose 6-phosphate translocase, is dephosphorylated by glucose 6-phosphatase (the same enzyme as the last step of gluconeogenesis), and the glucose goes to the blood. Muscle lacks glucose 6-phosphatase, so its glucose 6-phosphate enters glycolysis.

Lysosomal degradation

A small part (1–3%) of glycogen is degraded in lysosomes by acid α(1→4)-glucosidase (acid maltase); its purpose is unknown. Its deficiency causes Pompe disease (GSD II), the only GSD that is a lysosomal storage disease.

Hormonal (covalent) regulation

In the liver, glycogenesis speeds up in the fed state and glycogenolysis in fasting; in muscle, glycogenolysis runs during exercise and glycogenesis at rest. Two levels of control: hormones (phosphorylation, for the whole body) and allosteric effectors (for the tissue).

The cAMP cascade (glucagon, epinephrine)

  1. Glucagon or epinephrine binds a G protein-coupled receptor (liver: both; muscle: epinephrine) → adenylyl cyclase makes cAMP.
  2. cAMP binds the two regulatory subunits of tetrameric PKA, freeing two active catalytic subunits.
  3. PKA phosphorylates phosphorylase kinase b → a (active).
  4. Phosphorylase kinase a, the only enzyme that does so, phosphorylates glycogen phosphorylase b → a (active): glycogenolysis starts.
  • Amplification: each step activates many molecules of the next, so a few hormone molecules mobilise much glycogen.
  • Keeping it on: protein phosphatase-1 (PP1), which removes the phosphates, is blocked by inhibitor proteins that are themselves activated by cAMP. Insulin activates the phosphodiesterase that degrades cAMP, opposing glucagon and epinephrine.

Glycogen synthase: the opposite logic

Glycogen synthase a (active) is dephosphorylated; synthase b (inactive) is phosphorylated at several sites, and more phosphate means less activity. It is phosphorylated by PKA, phosphorylase kinase and other kinases, and reactivated by PP1.

EnzymePhosphorylated formDephosphorylated form
Glycogen phosphorylaseActive (a)Inactive (b)
Phosphorylase kinaseActive (a)Inactive (b)
Glycogen synthaseInactive (b)Active (a)

Net effect: insulin → glycogenesis ↑, glycogenolysis ↓; glucagon or epinephrine → the reverse.

Allosteric regulation and calcium

Allosteric effectors give a fast response to the cell’s own needs and can override hormonal control. They shift each enzyme between its R (more active) and T (less active) forms without changing its phosphorylation.

EffectorGlycogen synthaseGlycogen phosphorylaseWhere
Glucose 6-phosphateActivates (synthase b)Inhibits (phosphorylase a)Liver and muscle, fed state
ATPInhibitsLiver and muscle
Free glucoseInhibitsLiver only
AMPActivates phosphorylase b without phosphorylationMuscle only (anoxia, ATP depletion)

Calcium and calmodulin

Calmodulin (CaM) is a small Ca²⁺-binding protein; when four Ca²⁺ bind, it changes shape and activates its target. CaM is the δ subunit of phosphorylase kinase, so Ca²⁺ activates phosphorylase kinase b without PKA.

  • Muscle: nerve impulses release Ca²⁺ from the sarcoplasmic reticulum → glycogen is broken down for contraction.
  • Liver: epinephrine at α₁-adrenergic receptors → phospholipid cascade → Ca²⁺ leaves the ER → phosphorylase kinase activated. The Ca²⁺ also helps activate protein kinase C, which inactivates glycogen synthase. (Epinephrine at β-receptors acts through cAMP instead.)

AMP also activates PFK-1, so glucose from muscle glycogen can be oxidised at once.

Glycogen storage diseases

GSDs are genetic defects of glycogen degradation (rarely synthesis). They cause either abnormal glycogen structure or too much normal glycogen in a tissue. Common signs: hypoglycemia, enlarged liver, slow growth, muscle weakness or cramps. A liver defect causes hypoglycemia; a muscle defect causes weakness. There are 15 types.

TypeDeficient enzymeMain features
I: von GierkeGlucose 6-phosphataseFasting hypoglycemia, lactic acidosis, hyperuricemia, impaired growth, bone thinning; both glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis fail
II: PompeAcid α-glucosidase (acid maltase, lysosomal)Glycogen in lysosomes; normal blood sugar; enlarged liver and heart, muscle weakness
III: CoriDebranching enzyme (4:4 transferase)Enlarged liver, growth delay, fasting hypoglycemia, abnormal glycogen (short outer branches), high blood fat, possible weakness
IV: AndersenBranching enzyme (4:6 transferase)Growth delay, enlarged liver, cirrhosis, myopathy; usually death by age 5
V: McArdleMuscle glycogen phosphorylaseWeakness and cramps after exercise; usually benign and chronic
VI: HersLiver glycogen phosphorylaseEnlarged liver, hypoglycemia, developmental delay
VII: TaruiMuscle phosphofructokinaseExercise cramps, developmental delay, hemolytic anemia in some

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • Phosphorylation activates phosphorylase but inactivates glycogen synthase.
  • Muscle cannot release glucose to the blood: it lacks glucose 6-phosphatase.
  • Most of the body’s glycogen is in muscle, although the liver has the higher percentage.
  • Pompe disease is the only GSD that is lysosomal, and blood sugar is normal.
  • Phosphorylase needs pyridoxal phosphate, not biotin or thiamine.
  • Glycogen synthase needs a primer; glycogenin is itself an enzyme (autoglucosylation).
  • Epinephrine works through Ca²⁺ at α₁ receptors and through cAMP at β receptors.

Key terms

Glycogen: branched storage polymer of α-D-glucose · Nonreducing end: chain end where glucose is added or removed · UDP-glucose: activated glucose donor for synthesis · Glycogenin: protein that primes glycogen on Tyr-194 · Glycogen synthase: makes α(1→4) bonds · Branching enzyme: 4:6 transferase making α(1→6) branches · Phosphorolysis: bond cleavage by adding phosphate · Limit dextrin: what phosphorylase leaves four residues from each branch · Debranching enzyme: 4:4 transferase plus α(1→6)-glucosidase · Phosphoglucomutase: interconverts glucose 1-P and glucose 6-P · Glucose 6-phosphatase: ER enzyme that frees glucose, liver only · Acid maltase: lysosomal α(1→4)-glucosidase · Phosphorylase kinase: only enzyme that activates phosphorylase · PP1: phosphatase reversing these phosphorylations · Calmodulin: Ca²⁺-binding subunit of phosphorylase kinase · Signal amplification: each cascade step activating many enzymes · GSD: glycogen storage disease.
