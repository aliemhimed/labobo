Gene Duplication in Evolution

How new genes arise by duplication (Ohno’s thesis, neofunctionalisation), gene families such as trypsin and chymotrypsin and SRGAP2, copy number variations, and inversions: paracentric and pericentric, inversion loops and what crossing over inside them produces.

Labobo topic: MG Lec 7: Gene Duplication in Evolution

In 60 seconds

  • Gene duplication is thought to be the major source of new genes.
  • Ohno’s thesis: a single-copy essential gene cannot change freely; once duplicated, the extra copy can mutate and may gain a new, adaptive function (neofunctionalisation).
  • Gene families share sequence but have distinct products (e.g. trypsin and chymotrypsin); humans have at least 4 copies of SRGAP2.
  • CNVs: copy number of large sequences varies between individuals; 5–10% of the human genome.
  • Inversion: segment turned 180°, two breaks, no loss of information. Pericentric includes the centromere; paracentric does not.
  • Inversion heterozygotes pair with an inversion loop; a crossover inside it gives abnormal chromatids (paracentric: dicentric + acentric; pericentric: duplication + deletion).

Gene duplication and Ohno’s thesis

  • How do new genes arise? Some gene products exist in recently evolved organisms but not in ancestors.
  • Gene duplication is hypothesised to be the major source of new genes.
  • Ohno’s thesis: genes present as a single copy are indispensable, so they cannot change much. If an essential gene is duplicated in the germline, mutations in the extra copy are tolerated in later generations.
  • The duplicate is free to accumulate many changes over long periods; it may change enough to gain a new function with an adaptive advantage.
  1. Duplication: gene A → A + A.
  2. Neofunctionalisation: one copy keeps A, the other gains a new function (A + B).
  3. Specialisation: the copies become A and B.

Gene families

  • Some genes share much DNA sequence but have distinct products, e.g. trypsin and chymotrypsin. They arose from a common ancestral gene by duplication.
  • Gene family: a group of genes whose products do the same or very similar job.
  • SRGAP2 in primates: humans have at least four similar copies; several duplications trace back millions of years.

Copy number variations

  • Duplications of large sequences (often thousands of base pairs) happen regularly.
  • Within one species the number of copies of a sequence varies: sometimes many, sometimes deleted.
  • These differences are copy number variations (CNVs); they occur in coding and noncoding regions and occupy 5–10% of the human genome.

Inversions

  • A structural variation in which a segment is turned 180° within the chromosome.
  • No loss of genetic information; only the linear order changes.
  • Needs two breaks; the chromosome forms a loop before breaking, and the new sticky ends rejoin in reverse.
  • Pericentric: the centromere is inside the inverted segment. Paracentric: the centromere is not included.

Inversion heterozygotes

  • An organism with one inverted and one normal homolog is an inversion heterozygote.
  • In meiosis the two can pair only by forming an inversion loop.
  • No crossover inside the inverted segment: two normal and two inverted chromatids, all viable.
  • Crossover inside the loop: abnormal chromatids.
InversionSingle crossover (SCO) products
ParacentricOne dicentric chromatid and one acentric chromatid, each with duplication and deletion; plus one normal and one inverted (non-crossover) chromatid
PericentricTwo chromatids with duplication and deletion (one centromere each); plus one normal and one inverted

Commonly confused

PairHow to tell them apart
Pericentric vs paracentric“Peri” = around the centromere (includes it); “para” = beside it (excludes it)
Dicentric vs acentricTwo centromeres (torn at anaphase) vs none (lost)
Inversion vs deletionInversion loses nothing; deletion loses a segment
Inversion loop vs deletion loopInversion loop: both homologs loop to pair reversed regions. Deletion loop: only the normal homolog bulges

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • Dicentric and acentric products come from paracentric inversions.
  • Inversion heterozygotes are fine themselves; the problem is abnormal gametes after crossing over in the loop.
  • Ohno: the duplicate must be in the germline to matter in evolution.
  • CNVs occur in both coding and noncoding regions.

Key terms

Gene duplication: copying of a gene in the genome · Ohno’s thesis: duplication frees a copy to evolve · Neofunctionalisation: duplicate gains a new function · Gene family: related genes with similar functions · SRGAP2: duplicated primate gene · Copy number variation: varying number of copies of a sequence · Inversion: segment reversed 180° · Pericentric inversion: includes the centromere · Paracentric inversion: excludes the centromere · Inversion heterozygote: one normal and one inverted homolog · Inversion loop: pairing structure in meiosis · Dicentric: two centromeres · Acentric: no centromere.
