Human Genome Project

The origins and goals of the Human Genome Project, SNPs and CNVs, the major features of the human genome (size, gene number, alternative splicing, gene density, repeats), genome databases and disease-gene maps, and the projects that followed.

Labobo topic: MG Lec 3: Human Genome Project

In 60 seconds

  • HGP started in 1990 (James Watson, then Francis Collins): a 15-year, $3 billion plan. Draft published February 2001 (~96% of euchromatin); judged complete in 2003.
  • Goals: find all genes and their functions, study variation (SNPs), sequence model organisms, develop new sequencing technology, share data.
  • ~3.1 billion nucleotides; only ~2% codes for protein; ~20,000 genes (not 80,000–100,000).
  • ~94–95% of genes are alternatively spliced → about 200,000 to 1 million proteins.
  • Humans are ~99.9% identical; differences come from SNPs (~1 per 1,000 nt, 4–5 million per person) and CNVs.
  • Follow-ups: Cancer Genome project, Human Epigenome project, pharmacogenomics.

Origins of the project

  • The largest genomics project completed to date: determine the sequence of the human genome and identify all its genes.
  • Humans and other species share a common set of genes essential for cell function; all living organisms arose from a common ancestor.
  • Started in 1990, led by James Watson, later Francis Collins; a 15-year plan with a $3 billion budget.
  • The gene number was first expected to be 80,000–100,000.

Primary goals

  1. Establish functional categories for all human genes.
  2. Analyse variation between humans: SNPs.
  3. Map and sequence the genomes of model organisms.
  4. Develop new sequencing technologies (high-throughput sequencers).
  5. Disseminate genome information to scientists and the public.

Single nucleotide polymorphisms

  • The most common genetic variation among people: a single base differs.
  • About once every 1,000 nucleotides, so roughly 4–5 million SNPs per genome.
  • Mostly in DNA between genes; most have no effect on health.
  • Uses: predicting drug response, susceptibility to environmental factors, and as genetic markers (e.g. prenatal paternity tests).

Major features of the human genome

  • February 2001: analysis of about 96% of the euchromatic genome, with a 0.01% error rate (~600,000 errors).
  • Remaining gaps clustered around centromeres, telomeres and repetitive sequences; in 2003 sequencing and error-fixing were judged sufficient.
  • The sequence pools haploid genomes from different people: a reference genome of elements widely shared among human populations.
  • ~3.1 billion nucleotides, but protein-coding sequence is only ~2%.
  • The genome is dynamic: at least 50% comes from transposable elements (e.g. LINE and Alu) and other repeats.
  • Average gene ~25 kb (with regulatory regions, introns and exons); average mRNA ~3,000 nt.
  • Genes are uneven across the 24 chromosomes: gene-rich clusters separated by gene-poor “deserts” (~20% of the genome), matching G bands. Chromosome 19 is most gene-dense; 13 and Y least. Chromosome 1 has the most genes; Y the fewest.
  • Human genes are larger with more and bigger introns than in invertebrates. Largest gene: dystrophin (2.5 Mb, mostly introns). Introns per gene range from 0 (histone genes) to 234 (titin).
  • >50% of genes are highly similar to genes in other organisms, but >40% had no known function.

Coding genes and alternative splicing

  • Genes were estimated at about 100,000, but there are only about 20,000.
  • Through alternative splicing and post-translational modification, they make roughly 200,000 to 1 million proteins.
  • Alternative splicing was first thought to affect ~50% of genes; now known to be ~94–95%.

Individual variation: SNPs and CNVs

  • About 99.9% of the sequence is the same in all humans.
  • Differences come from SNPs and copy number variations (CNVs).
  • CNVs: duplications or deletions of relatively large DNA sections (hundreds to thousands of base pairs); they can delete exons or cause frameshifts.

Databases and disease genes

  • Online databases show maps of every chromosome. A cytogenetic map displays contigs, UniGene (a histogram of gene density), gene symbols, loci and gene names.
  • The most valuable result: identifying disease genes and treatment strategies. Disease-gene maps show genes thought to be involved in genetic disease, e.g. chromosome 21 (48 million bases) with Alzheimer disease (APP), ALS, Down syndrome critical region, and others.

What came next

  • Cancer Genome project: comparing normal genome (from blood) with tumour genome to find cancer-causing mutations and responses to chemotherapy.
  • Human Epigenome project: maps of epigenetic changes such as DNA methylation and histone modification, affected by development, environment, drugs, ageing and diet.
  • Pharmacogenomics: medicine tailored to a person’s genes.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • The 2001 draft covered 96% of euchromatin, not the whole genome.
  • SNPs are mostly between genes and usually harmless.
  • The reference genome is a pool of several people, not one person.
  • Most of the genome does not code for protein (only ~2%).

Key terms

Human Genome Project: project to sequence the human genome · Euchromatin: gene-rich, less condensed chromatin · Reference genome: pooled standard sequence · SNP: single-base variation · CNV: copy number variation · Model organism: species used in experimental genetics · Transposable element: mobile DNA sequence · Alu / LINE: common human repeats · Gene desert: gene-poor region · G band: chromosome staining band · Alternative splicing: several mRNAs from one gene · Cytogenetic map: chromosome map with loci · UniGene: gene-density display · Disease-gene map: map of disease-associated genes · Epigenome: map of epigenetic marks · Pharmacogenomics: gene-based drug choice.
