The omics revolution

The HGP and genomics techniques started the era of “omics”.

  • Proteomics: all proteins in a cell or tissue.
  • Metabolomics: proteins and enzymatic pathways of cell metabolism.
  • Pharmacogenomics: customised medicine based on a person’s genetic profile.

Personal genomics and cost

  • Next- and third-generation sequencing greatly reduced cost; companies proposed whole-genome sequencing for individuals: personal genomics.
  • The NIH challenged scientists to sequence a human genome for $1000 by 2014.
  • Even below $1000, interpreting the data for medical use is still very expensive.

Charcot–Marie–Tooth and the Lupski genome

  • Charcot–Marie–Tooth (CMT): hereditary motor and sensory neuropathy, from mutations in a gene expressed in Schwann cells.
  • Smaller, weaker muscles, loss of sensation, foot deformities (hammertoes, high arches), foot drop, tripping; starts in the feet and legs, later hands and arms.
  • Dr Richard Gibbs (Baylor) sequenced the genome of colleague Dr James Lupski, who has CMT.
  • Lupski had two different mutations in SH3TC2: a nonsense mutation in one allele and a missense mutation in the other (compound heterozygote).
  • Parents and some siblings had only one of the two; only siblings with both were affected.
  • The first clinically relevant success of personal genome sequencing.

Somatic genome mosaicism

  • Personal genome projects sequence a diploid genome: millions of short fragments from maternal and paternal chromosomes.
  • One person can have >4 million variations between maternal and paternal chromosomes; differences between people may be closer to 0.5% than 0.1%.
  • Cells in one individual do not have identical genomes. Causes: DNA replication errors, CNVs, SNPs and other changes accumulating as cells divide.
  • Passed to daughter cells in mitosis, not to offspring.
  • Single-cell sequencing has found SNP and CNV mosaicism in skin, brain, blood and stem cells of the same person; it affects health and disease.

The pangenome

A single reference genome cannot show everyone’s variation. Scientists now use the pangenome: all distinct genes and variations in a species, drawn as a graph with shared and alternative paths.

Whole-exome sequencing

  • Sequences only the ~180,000 exons (protein-coding parts).
  • More disease-related variants lie in the exome than elsewhere, and it is cost-effective.
  • Limit: it misses mutations in regulatory regions.

ENCODE

  • Encyclopedia of DNA Elements: identify and analyse all functional elements (regulatory sites), focusing on non-gene sequences.
  • Studied gene expression in 147 cell types over almost 10 years.
  • Klug summary: about 80% of the genome is considered functional (much is transcribed, mostly into noncoding RNAs; ~17,000 lncRNA genes); ~70,000 promoter regions and ~400,000 enhancer regions; 20,687 protein-coding genes and 11,224 pseudogenes, some active in certain cells.

Nutrigenomics

The study of interactions between genes and nutrients: genes influence how the body reacts to food (nutrigenetics) and food influences how genes behave. It helps regulate blood pressure, blood sugar and heart rate.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • CMT gene SH3TC2 is expressed in Schwann cells, not neurons.
  • Lupski needed both mutations (recessive) to be affected.
  • Somatic mosaic changes are not inherited by children.
  • Cheap sequencing does not mean cheap interpretation.

Key terms

Omics: whole-system biology fields · Metabolomics: study of metabolic pathways · Pharmacogenomics: genotype-based medicine · Personal genomics: sequencing individual genomes · Charcot–Marie–Tooth: hereditary motor and sensory neuropathy · SH3TC2: CMT gene in Schwann cells · Nonsense mutation: creates a stop codon · Missense mutation: changes an amino acid · Somatic mosaicism: genetically different body cells · Pangenome: all genes and variants of a species · Whole-exome sequencing: sequencing protein-coding exons · ENCODE: Encyclopedia of DNA Elements · Pseudogene: gene copy usually inactive · lncRNA: long noncoding RNA (>200 nt) · Nutrigenomics: gene–nutrient interactions.
