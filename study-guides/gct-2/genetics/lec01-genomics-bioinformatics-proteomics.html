Genomics, Bioinformatics & Proteomics

How whole genomes are sequenced and assembled, how bioinformatics finds and annotates genes (BLAST, identity and E-values, hallmarks of a gene, ORFs), and how proteomics studies the full set of proteins with 2-D gels and mass spectrometry.

Labobo topic: MG Lec 1: Genomics, Bioinformatics & Proteomics

In 60 seconds

  • Genomics studies whole genomes. Old way: positional cloning, one gene at a time. New way: whole-genome shotgun sequencing, then overlapping reads are assembled into contigs.
  • First genome sequenced this way: Haemophilus influenzae, 1.83 million bp, in 1995.
  • Genomes are read many times (coverage); joining the runs is compiling.
  • Bioinformatics finds genes; annotation labels them, often with BLAST. A lower E-value means a more significant match.
  • Gene hallmarks: promoter boxes (TATA, GC, CAAT), splice sites, termination signals, and open reading frames.
  • Proteomics studies the proteome: ~20,000 genes → at least 290,000 proteins. Tools: 2-D gel electrophoresis and mass spectrometry (MALDI, m/z).

Genomics and shotgun sequencing

  • Genomics is the study of genomes; it lets us sequence entire genomes.
  • Earlier, positional cloning mapped DNA sequences to specific chromosomes, identifying one gene at a time, and produced DNA libraries.
  • Whole-genome (shotgun) sequencing: DNA is cut (e.g. by different restriction enzymes) into overlapping fragments, each is sequenced, and computer programs align the overlaps into continuous stretches called contigs, building up a whole chromosome.
  • In 1995, the 1.83 million bp genome of the bacterium Haemophilus influenzae was sequenced this way.
  • Genomes are always sequenced more than once (multiple reads) for accuracy. Assembling the final sequence from many runs is compiling; coverage (depth) is how many times a nucleotide appears at the same position across reads.

Bioinformatics and annotation

Bioinformatics uses computer software and mathematics to organise, share and analyse data on gene structure, sequence and expression. It is used to:

  • compare DNA sequences and align contigs;
  • identify genes in a genomic sequence;
  • find gene-regulatory sequences;
  • identify structural sequences such as telomeres.

Annotation means identifying genes, regulatory and other sequences after a genome is sequenced and compiled, mainly by comparing it with sequences already in databases. BLAST (Basic Local Alignment Search Tool) searches DNA and protein sequence banks.

ValueMeaning
Identity valueIdentical matches between aligned sequences ÷ total bases aligned (e.g. 93%)
Expect (E) valueNumber of matching sequences expected by chance; the lower (closer to 0), the more significant the match

This only works if similar sequences are already in a database.

Hallmarks of a gene and ORFs

  • Software can search for the hallmark features of genes:
    • regulatory sequences upstream: TATA box, GC box, CAAT box;
    • splice sites between introns and exons;
    • termination sequences downstream.
  • Protein-coding genes contain one or more open reading frames (ORFs): sequences that, after transcription and splicing, are translated into a protein.
Watch out: intron splice sites

The slides say introns begin with CT. Klug’s figure on the same slide set labels the 5′ splice site GT and the 3′ splice site AG, which matches the GU…AG rule in RNA. Learn GT…AG in DNA, but recognise that the lecture wrote CT.

Proteomics

  • Proteomics: identification, characterisation and quantitative analysis of proteomes. The proteome is the complete set of proteins encoded by a genome.
  • It tells us about protein structure (primary to quaternary), post-translational modifications, protein–protein and protein–nucleic acid interactions, and cellular localisation.
  • Proteomes are larger than genomes, overturning the 1940s “one gene – one enzyme” idea: genes have multiple transcription start sites and make different transcripts (plus alternative splicing). About 20,000 protein-coding genes make at least 290,000 proteins.

2-D gels and mass spectrometry

MethodHow it works
2-D gel electrophoresis (2DGE)1st dimension: isoelectric focusing separates by isoelectric point (pH where charge is zero). 2nd: SDS-PAGE separates by molecular weight. Gives up to several thousand spots, compared with reference gels
Mass spectrometryIonised samples in gas form; measures the mass-to-charge (m/z) ratio. Spectra are matched to databases of known proteins
MALDIMatrix-assisted laser desorption ionisation: a UV laser heats, vaporises and ionises peptides; peaks show m/z and abundance

Typical workflow: cut a spot from a 2-D gel → digest with a protease such as trypsin → mass spectrometry of the peptides → compare spectra with a database to identify the protein (e.g. a peptide from T. rex matched a synthetic version).

Commonly confused

PairHow to tell them apart
Positional cloning vs shotgun sequencingOne gene at a time, mapped to a chromosome vs whole genome in random overlapping pieces
Compiling vs coverageCompiling: assembling the final sequence. Coverage: how many reads cover each base
Identity vs E-valueIdentity: % identical. E-value: chance matches; lower is better
Genome vs proteomeAll genes vs all proteins (proteome is larger)
2DGE dimensionsFirst isoelectric point, then molecular weight

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • A low E-value means a significant match.
  • BLAST cannot find a gene with no similar sequence in the database.
  • Annotation comes after sequencing and compiling.
  • MALDI measures m/z, not mass alone.
  • The proteome is bigger than the genome.

Key terms

Genomics: study of whole genomes · Positional cloning: mapping one gene to a chromosome · Shotgun sequencing: sequencing random overlapping fragments · Contig: assembled continuous sequence · Compiling: assembling the final sequence · Coverage: read depth per base · Bioinformatics: computational analysis of biological data · Annotation: labelling genes and features · BLAST: sequence similarity search tool · Identity value: percentage of identical bases · E-value: matches expected by chance · ORF: open reading frame · Proteome: all proteins of a genome · Proteomics: study of proteomes · 2DGE: two-dimensional gel electrophoresis · Isoelectric focusing: separation by isoelectric point · Mass spectrometry: m/z measurement · MALDI: laser ionisation of peptides.
