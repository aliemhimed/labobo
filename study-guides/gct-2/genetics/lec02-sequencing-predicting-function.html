Sequencing & Predicting Function

How DNA is sequenced (Sanger chain termination, automated capillary sequencers, next-generation sequencing), how draft and reference genomes are built and annotated, the hallmarks of a gene and ORFs, and how gene and protein function is predicted from homologues, protein domains and ChIP.

Labobo topic: MG Lec 2: Sequencing & Predicting Function

In 60 seconds

  • Sanger sequencing uses dideoxynucleotides (ddNTPs): they lack the 3′-OH, so chain growth stops. Fluorescent ddNTPs + capillary electrophoresis + laser detector give the sequence.
  • Automated capillary sequencers made the Human Genome Project feasible: >2 million bases per 24 hours.
  • Draft sequences have gaps; a reference genome accepts some error. Multiple reads (coverage) are compiled.
  • Annotation with BLAST: identity % and E-value (lower = more significant; shorter queries match by chance more easily).
  • ORFs start with ATG and end with TAA, TAG or TGA.
  • Function prediction: homologues (paralogs = same species, orthologs = different species), protein domains, and ChIP for DNA-binding proteins.

Sanger sequencing

The Human Genome Project first used the Sanger (chain-termination) method.

  1. Reaction mix: template DNA, a primer, DNA polymerase, normal dNTPs and fluorescently labelled ddNTPs (a different colour for each base).
  2. The primer is extended; whenever a ddNTP is added, synthesis stops, because a dideoxynucleotide has no 3′-OH for the next bond.
  3. This makes fragments of every length, each ending in a coloured ddNTP.
  4. Capillary gel electrophoresis separates them by size; a laser and detector read the colour of each fragment end.
  5. A computer builds a chromatogram and the sequence.

High-throughput sequencing

  • Sequencing billions of base pairs quickly and cheaply was the major problem of the Human Genome Project.
  • The breakthrough: computer-automated sequencers with multiple capillary gels, running around the clock and producing over 2 million bases in 24 hours.
  • Newer (next-generation) sequencing, shown in the lecture, fragments DNA, adds adapters, amplifies fragments on a flow cell (bridge amplification) and reads many fragments in parallel cycle by cycle, then aligns the reads.

Draft and reference genomes

  • Draft sequences often have gaps in regions that are hard to analyse.
  • A reference genome carries a degree of error that researchers are willing to accept.
  • Genomes are sequenced more than once; compiling assembles the final sequence; coverage is how many times a nucleotide appears at the same position after compiling.

Annotation with BLAST

  • Annotation: after sequencing and compiling, gene-regulatory and other sequences are identified, mainly by comparison with sequences in databases using BLAST.
  • Identity value: percentage of exact matches (e.g. 93%).
  • E-value: matches expected by chance. It accounts for query length: shorter sequences are more likely to match by chance. The lower (closer to 0), the more significant.
  • Comparing genes across species, e.g. human LEP and mouse Lep (leptin), shows high similarity.

Hallmarks of a gene and ORFs

  • BLAST works only if a similar sequence is already in the database, so software also searches for hallmarks:
    • upstream regulatory sequences: promoters, enhancers, silencers; TATA, GC and CAAT boxes;
    • intron splice sites;
    • downstream termination sequences.
  • Protein-coding genes contain open reading frames (ORFs), typically starting with ATG and ending with TAA, TAG or TGA.
Watch out: intron splice sites

The slides say most introns begin with CT and end with AG. Klug’s gene diagram in the same lecture labels the 5′ splice site GT, matching the GU…AG rule. Expect GT…AG in standard sources, and know the lecture’s wording.

Predicting gene and protein function

  • Sequence similarity searches (BLAST) identify homologous genes, evolutionarily related genes:
    • Paralogs: homologues in the same species (arise by duplication);
    • Orthologs: homologues in different species from a common ancestor.
  • The gene predicts the polypeptide, which can be scanned for structural domains: ion channels, DNA-binding regions (e.g. helix-turn-helix with a recognition helix), membrane-spanning regions. These predict function.
  • Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) maps protein–DNA interactions and finds genes regulated by a transcription factor:
  1. Isolate chromatin (proteins cross-linked to DNA) and fragment it.
  2. Add an antibody to the protein and purify the protein–DNA complexes.
  3. Reverse the cross-links, isolate the DNA and sequence it.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • ddNTPs stop synthesis because they lack the 3′-OH (not the 2′).
  • Short query sequences give higher E-values for the same match.
  • Orthologs are in different species.
  • ChIP finds where a protein binds DNA; it needs an antibody to that protein.

Key terms

Sanger sequencing: chain-termination method · ddNTP: chain-terminating nucleotide · Capillary electrophoresis: size separation in thin tubes · Chromatogram: coloured peak trace of the sequence · High-throughput sequencing: fast automated sequencing · Draft sequence: genome with gaps · Reference genome: accepted standard sequence · Compiling: assembling reads · Coverage: reads per base · Annotation: identifying genes and features · E-value: chance-match measure · ORF: open reading frame · Homologue: evolutionarily related gene · Paralog: homologue in same species · Ortholog: homologue in another species · Domain: structural unit of a protein · ChIP: chromatin immunoprecipitation.
