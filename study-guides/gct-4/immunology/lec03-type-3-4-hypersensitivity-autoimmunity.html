Type III and IV hypersensitivity, autoimmunity

Immune complex disease (serum sickness, the Arthus reaction), T cell-mediated injury (delayed-type hypersensitivity, contact sensitivity, granulomas, CTL killing), and how autoimmunity arises: tolerance, its failure, genes such as HLA, infections and other triggers.

Labobo topic: Immuno Lec 3: Type III and IV Hypersensitivity, Autoimmunity

In 60 seconds

  • Type III: antigen–antibody complexes (mostly IgG) deposit in vessel walls → complement → neutrophils → vasculitis. Usually systemic. Small complexes deposit more than large ones.
  • Serum sickness (systemic, 1–2 weeks after foreign protein: fever, rash, arthritis, nephritis) vs Arthus reaction (local skin vasculitis and necrosis at an injection site).
  • Type IV: T cells injure by cytokines (Th1 IFN-γ → macrophages; Th17 IL-17 → neutrophils) or by CTL killing. DTH appears in 24–48 h; chronic DTH → granulomas and fibrosis.
  • Tolerance: central (deletion in thymus/marrow) and peripheral (anergy, Tregs with CD25/FoxP3, IL-10, TGF-β, and deletion). CTLA-4 and PD-1 are inhibitory receptors.
  • Autoimmunity = genes (strongest: HLA class II; HLA-B27 → ankylosing spondylitis >100×) + environment (infection, molecular mimicry, tissue injury). Often chronic through epitope spreading; commoner in women.

Learning objectives

Abbas, Lichtman & Pillai, Cellular and Molecular Immunology, chapters 19 (hypersensitivity) and 15 (tolerance and autoimmunity). By the end you can:

  • explain how immune complexes cause disease and compare serum sickness with the Arthus reaction;
  • describe how CD4+ and CD8+ T cells injure tissue, the time course of DTH and how granulomas form;
  • outline central and peripheral tolerance and the genetic and environmental causes of autoimmunity.

Type III: immune complex disease

  • Complexes may contain self or foreign antigens. Almost all these diseases are systemic; a few are restricted to the kidney.
  • Complexes form in most normal responses; they cause disease only when made in excess, not cleared and deposited in tissue.
  • Small complexes escape phagocytosis and deposit in vessels more than large complexes, which phagocytes clear.
  • Often in joints and kidneys, but they can deposit in small vessels almost anywhere.
  • Deposited complexes activate complement and Fc receptors on leukocytes and mast cells → neutrophil recruitment, lysosomal enzymes → vasculitis with fibrinoid necrosis.
  • Examples: SLE, polyarteritis nodosa, poststreptococcal glomerulonephritis, serum sickness, the Arthus reaction.

In both, vessel-wall inflammation damages endothelium, which promotes thrombosis, impairs blood flow and causes ischaemic necrosis.

Type IV: T cell-mediated injury

T cells injure tissue by producing cytokines that cause inflammation or by directly killing cells. They may be autoreactive, specific for foreign proteins bound to tissue, or responding to persistent intracellular microbes that resist phagocytes and antibodies.

  • Th17 → IL-17 → neutrophil recruitment.
  • Th1 → IFN-γ → macrophage activation.
  • TNF recruits and activates many leukocytes.
  • (Th2 cytokines give eosinophil-rich allergic inflammation, which is type I.)
  • Damage comes from lysosomal enzymes and reactive oxygen species of the recruited cells. The inflammation is typically chronic and causes fibrosis through cytokines and growth factors that activate fibroblasts.

Contact sensitivity

Skin reactions to topical chemicals (poison ivy urushiol, drugs). Chemicals bind self proteins, including MHC, forming neoantigens; both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells supply cytokines. Chronic forms are called eczema.

Delayed-type hypersensitivity and granulomas

DTH is a cytokine-mediated inflammatory reaction driven mainly by CD4+ T cells, appearing 24–48 hours after antigen in a sensitised person (versus minutes for allergy). Sensitisation comes from infection, contact chemicals, or injected protein antigens.

  1. ~4 h: neutrophils gather around postcapillary venules at the site.
  2. ~12 h: T cells and blood monocytes infiltrate.
  3. 24–48 h: full reaction (induration).

Losing DTH to universal antigens (for example Candida) indicates deficient T cell function: anergy.

Chronic DTH: when a Th1 response activates macrophages but cannot eliminate phagocytosed microbes (tuberculosis), prolonged cytokine signals form granulomas: activated macrophages, multinucleate giant cells and T cells, sometimes with central necrosis, with fibroblasts laying down collagen.

CTL-mediated injury

  • CTLs kill virus-infected cells even when the virus itself does little harm: they cannot tell cytopathic from noncytopathic viruses.
  • In autoimmunity, CTLs destroy specific host cells, for example islet β cells in type 1 diabetes.
  • CTLs also injure allografts during rejection.

Tolerance

Immunological tolerance is unresponsiveness to an antigen induced by exposure of specific lymphocytes to it. Autoimmunity begins with self-reactive lymphocytes recognising self antigen and becoming effector cells.

Central tolerancePeripheral tolerance
WherePrimary lymphoid organs (thymus, bone marrow)Peripheral tissues
HowElimination of self-reactive lymphocytesAnergy, suppression by Tregs, deletion
Why neededFirst lineCentral tolerance is imperfect: self-reactive cells exist in healthy people
  • Full T cell activation needs signal 1 (TCR sees antigen) and signal 2 (CD28 binds B7-1/B7-2). Antigen without co-stimulation or innate immunity makes the cell anergic.
  • Self antigen without innate activation engages inhibitory receptors of the CD28 family, CTLA-4 and PD-1, which end T cell responses.
  • Tregs arise from Th0, constitutively express CD25 and the transcription factor FoxP3, secrete IL-10 and TGF-β, inhibit Th1 function and are critical to prevent autoimmunity.
  • T cells matter most because most autoantigens are proteins, and helper T cells are needed for high-affinity autoantibodies.

How autoimmunity develops

Contributing factors: genetic susceptibility and environmental triggers (infections, tissue injury). Susceptibility genes disrupt tolerance; infection or necrosis brings in and activates autoreactive lymphocytes.

  • Defective self-tolerance: inadequate elimination or regulation of T or B cells; the underlying cause of all autoimmune diseases.
  • Abnormal display of self antigens: enzymatic modification or cell stress creates neoantigens to which there is no tolerance.
  • Inflammation or innate responses: APCs express more co-stimulators, overcoming regulation.

Diseases are systemic or organ-specific depending on where the autoantigen is (myasthenia gravis: muscle). Injury comes from immune complexes, circulating autoantibodies or autoreactive T cells. They tend to be chronic, progressive and self-perpetuating: injury releases and alters other antigens, activating more lymphocytes (epitope spreading).

Genes and autoimmunity

  • The strongest associations are with MHC (HLA) genes, and class II more often than class I. The disease-associated MHC molecule may present a self peptide to pathogenic T cells (proved in a few cases).
  • HLA-B27: over 100 times more likely to develop ankylosing spondylitis. Still, an HLA allele alone neither causes nor predicts disease.
  • Most autoimmune diseases are complex polygenic traits; for example, repeat polymorphisms in the insulin gene and type 1 diabetes.

Infections and other triggers

  • Local infection recruits leukocytes and activates tissue APCs, which express co-stimulators and cytokines and break T cell tolerance (bystander activation).
  • Molecular mimicry: microbial antigens cross-react with self antigens (for example rheumatic fever).
  • The gut and skin microbiome influences autoimmunity.
  • Paradoxically, some infections protect against autoimmune disease; how is unknown.
  • Anatomical alterations (inflammation, ischaemia, trauma) expose normally hidden self antigens.
  • Sex: many autoimmune diseases are more common in women.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • Type III injury happens where complexes deposit, not where the antigen came from.
  • The tuberculin (PPD) skin test and contact dermatitis are type IV; the Arthus reaction is type III.
  • Type 1 diabetes is mediated by CTLs killing β cells (type IV), not by antibodies.
  • Tregs are CD4+CD25+FoxP3+; FOXP3 mutation causes IPEX, AIRE mutation causes APS.
  • HLA associations are strongest with class II, but the most famous single association, B27, is class I.
  • Absent DTH to Candida means anergy (T cell deficiency), not absence of infection.

Key terms

Immune complex: antigen bound to antibody · Serum sickness: systemic immune complex disease · Arthus reaction: local immune complex vasculitis · Fibrinoid necrosis: vessel wall damage in vasculitis · DTH: T cell reaction peaking at 24–48 h · Contact sensitivity: T cell reaction to skin chemicals · Neoantigen: altered self protein · Granuloma: nodule of activated macrophages, giant cells and T cells · Anergy: functional unresponsiveness · Central tolerance: deletion in primary lymphoid organs · Peripheral tolerance: control in tissues · Treg: CD25+FoxP3+ suppressor T cell · CTLA-4, PD-1: inhibitory receptors · Epitope spreading: widening of the autoimmune response · Molecular mimicry: microbial antigen resembling self · AIRE: gene for thymic self-antigen expression.
