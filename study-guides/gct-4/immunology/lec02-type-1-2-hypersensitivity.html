Type I and type II hypersensitivity

When immune responses injure the host. Type I (immediate, IgE and mast cell) reactions: sensitisation, mediators, the immediate and late-phase reactions, anaphylaxis, asthma, food allergy, eczema and their treatment. Type II (antibody-mediated) reactions: how IgG and IgM against cells and tissues cause destruction, inflammation or dysfunction.

Labobo topic: Immuno Lec 2: Type I and II Hypersensitivity

In 60 seconds

  • Four types (ABCD): Anaphylactic/atopic (I), antiBody-mediated (II), immune Complex (III), Delayed T cell (IV). Types I–III are antibody-mediated.
  • Type I: Th2/Tfh cells (IL-4, IL-13) drive IgE; IgE binds FcεRI on mast cells (sensitisation); re-exposure cross-links IgE → degranulation within minutes; late phase 2–4 h later (eosinophils, neutrophils, Th2), peak ~24 h.
  • Mediators: histamine (permeability, vasodilation, smooth muscle contraction), tryptase, heparin; newly made PGD₂, leukotrienes (prolonged bronchoconstriction), PAF; cytokines for the late phase.
  • Anaphylaxis: systemic vasodilation, leak, shock; treat with epinephrine. About 20% have a second episode up to 12 h later.
  • Type II: IgG/IgM on cell or matrix antigens → opsonisation and phagocytosis, complement/Fc-mediated inflammation, or receptor dysfunction (myasthenia gravis, Graves).

Definitions and classification

Disorders caused by immune responses are hypersensitivity diseases. Responses normally clear pathogens without serious injury, but they can be poorly controlled, aimed at host tissues, or triggered by harmless commensals or environmental antigens.

  • Hypersensitivity reaction: an exaggerated or pathological immune response to foreign or self antigens.
  • Allergy: an abnormal response to a harmless environmental stimulus (food, pollen, dander).
  • Autoimmune disease: an abnormal response against self antigens (failure of self-tolerance).
  • Drug hypersensitivity reaction: adverse drug effects resembling allergy; may be allergic or nonallergic.

Type I: overview

  • Appears within minutes of re-exposure in a previously sensitised person. Clinically called allergy or atopy; the antigens are allergens.
  • Effector cells: mast cells, basophils, eosinophils; the substances they release cause the symptoms.
  • Mast cells are abundant in connective tissue and under epithelia, so these are the commonest sites.
  • The IgE response is the normal defence against parasites too large to be phagocytosed.
  • Allergy is the prototypic type 2 inflammatory disease, mediated by IL-4, IL-5 and IL-13 from Th2 cells, Tfh cells and a few other cells.
  • Some reactions are triggered without antigen or IgE (exercise, cold, some drugs): nonatopic reactions.

Allergens, Th2 cells and IgE

  1. First exposure: allergen is presented to CD4+ T cells, which become IL-4/IL-13-producing Th2 and Tfh cells.
  2. These help B cells switch to IgE.
  3. IgE binds FcεRI on mast cells: sensitisation.
  4. Re-exposure: allergen cross-links the bound IgE → mast cells release mediators → the reaction.
  • Allergens are proteins or chemicals bound to proteins: pollen, house dust mites, animal dander, foods, drugs.
  • The reaction depends on CD4+ T cells, so T cell-independent antigens (polysaccharides) cannot cause it unless attached to a protein.
  • Repeated exposure is needed: switching to IgE and sensitising mast cells must happen before a reaction is possible.
  • Atopic people make high IgE to environmental allergens; others make mainly IgM and IgG and little IgE. Atopic people have more allergen-specific IL-4-secreting T cells in blood, and Th2 cells accumulate in skin and bronchial lesions.
  • ILC2s make many of the same cytokines (IL-5, IL-13).
  • Allergy results from poorly understood gene–environment interactions, with a genetic predisposition.

Mast cell activation

Activation happens when multivalent antigen cross-links FcεRI-bound IgE. It produces three responses:

  1. Degranulation: exocytosis of preformed granules, started by activated protein kinase C.
  2. Lipid mediators: synthesis controlled by cytosolic phospholipase A₂.
  3. Cytokines: from newly induced gene transcription.

Other triggers: complement fragments C3a and C5a (hence “anaphylatoxins”), neuropeptides, and cold and intense exercise (mechanism unknown).

Wheal and flare

Intradermal allergen in a sensitised person: plasma leak gives a swollen wheal; dilated vessels at its margin give a red flare. It appears in 5–10 minutes and fades in under an hour. It needs allergen-specific IgE on dermal mast cells and prior sensitisation; histology shows degranulated mast cells, and histamine is the key mediator. A late-phase reaction can occur without an obvious immediate one.

Anaphylaxis

  • An acute, potentially fatal systemic reaction from immunological or nonimmunological activation of mast cells and basophils, releasing mediators into the circulation.
  • Features: urticaria, GI upset, respiratory distress, oedema, and falling blood pressure from vasodilation and vascular leak → anaphylactic shock; airway narrowing can cause asphyxia.
  • Common triggers: penicillins, peanuts, tree nuts, fish, shellfish, milk, eggs, bee venom; also exercise or idiopathic.
  • Occurs within seconds to an hour. In about 20% symptoms recur up to 12 hours later without re-exposure, so patients are observed for several hours.
  • Treatment: epinephrine, which constricts vascular smooth muscle, raises cardiac output and relaxes bronchi.
Watch out

The recurrence after anaphylaxis is sometimes called a “late-phase anaphylactic reaction”, but it is not the late-phase allergic reaction. The late-phase allergic reaction is localised inflammation, not a second systemic shock.

Asthma, food allergy, atopic dermatitis

Asthma

Goals: prevent and reverse inflammation, and relax airway smooth muscle.

  • Inhaled corticosteroids block inflammatory cytokines (systemic steroids during an attack).
  • Long-acting β₂ agonists raise cAMP in smooth muscle and stop contraction.
  • Leukotriene (LTC₄) receptor antagonists reduce bronchoconstriction.
  • Humanised anti-IgE monoclonal antibody lowers serum IgE.
  • Antihistamines do not help: histamine has little role in airway constriction, and anticholinergic antihistamines can thicken mucus and worsen obstruction.

Food allergy

Mediators from intestinal mucosal and submucosal mast cells (including the oropharynx): itching, oedema, increased peristalsis and fluid secretion, oropharyngeal swelling, vomiting, diarrhoea.

Atopic dermatitis (eczema)

Acute flares of itchy red exudative papules on chronically dry scaly skin. More antigen exposure and keratinocyte cytokines promote type 2 responses, leading to chronic late-phase reactions in skin. Being cytokine-driven, it is not inhibited by antihistamines but responds to corticosteroids.

Treatment of allergy

  • Cromolyn inhibits mast cell degranulation; antihistamines block histamine on vessels and smooth muscle.
  • Cytokine blockers: anti-IL-5 and anti-IL-5R (asthma), anti-IL-4R (atopic dermatitis).
  • Allergen-specific immunotherapy (desensitisation): gradual exposure to controlled doses of the exact allergen (pollen, mites, venom). How it works is unclear: possibly T cell tolerance, a Th2 → Th1 shift, allergen-specific IgG, or regulatory T cells.

Type II hypersensitivity

IgG or IgM antibodies against antigens on cells or extracellular matrix cause injury in four ways: activating complement, targeting cells for phagocytosis, recruiting inflammatory cells, and interfering with normal cell function.

  1. Opsonisation and phagocytosis: antibody (and the complement it fixes) coats circulating cells, which phagocytes with Fc and complement receptors destroy. Also NK-cell ADCC. Examples: autoimmune haemolytic anaemia, immune thrombocytopenia, transfusion reactions, haemolytic disease of the newborn.
  2. Inflammation: antibody deposited in basement membranes and matrix activates complement; C5a and C3a recruit neutrophils and macrophages. Example: Goodpasture syndrome (anti-glomerular basement membrane antibody that also binds lung basement membrane), rheumatic fever, hyperacute rejection.
  3. Cellular dysfunction without inflammation: antibodies to receptors block or stimulate them. Myasthenia gravis (anti-acetylcholine receptor, weakness); Graves disease (anti-TSH receptor stimulates the thyroid, hyperthyroidism); pernicious anaemia (anti-intrinsic factor).
  • Coombs tests: direct detects antibody already on the red cell surface; indirect detects free antibody in serum.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • A first exposure does not cause an allergic reaction; sensitisation must come first.
  • Polysaccharides alone cannot cause type I reactions; they need a protein carrier (CD4 T cell help).
  • First-line treatment of anaphylaxis is epinephrine, not antihistamines or steroids.
  • Antihistamines do not treat asthma or the late phase of eczema; steroids do.
  • C3a and C5a are anaphylatoxins: they degranulate mast cells without IgE.
  • Charcot-Leyden crystals are made of galectin-10 from eosinophils.
  • Graves disease is type II though nothing is destroyed: the antibody stimulates the receptor.

Key terms

Hypersensitivity: immune response that injures the host · Allergen: antigen that triggers allergy · Atopy: tendency to make IgE to environmental antigens · Sensitisation: IgE binding to mast cell FcεRI · FcεRI: high-affinity IgE receptor · Degranulation: exocytosis of mast cell granules · Anaphylatoxins: C3a and C5a · Late-phase reaction: inflammation 2–4 h after the immediate reaction · Wheal and flare: skin swelling with a red rim · Anaphylaxis: systemic immediate hypersensitivity · Charcot-Leyden crystals: galectin-10 crystals from eosinophils · Desensitisation: allergen-specific immunotherapy · Cromolyn: mast cell stabiliser · Opsonisation: coating that promotes phagocytosis · ADCC: NK killing of antibody-coated cells · Goodpasture syndrome: anti-basement membrane disease of kidney and lung · Coombs test: detects anti-red cell antibody.
