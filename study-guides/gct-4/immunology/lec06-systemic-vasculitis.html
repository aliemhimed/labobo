Systemic vasculitis

Inflammation and damage of blood vessel walls, classified by vessel size (Chapel Hill 2012). The three mechanisms (immune complexes, ANCA, T cells and granulomas), then each syndrome: GPA, microscopic polyangiitis, EGPA, IgA vasculitis, giant cell and Takayasu arteritis, Kawasaki disease, Buerger disease, polyarteritis nodosa, cryoglobulinaemic vasculitis and Behçet syndrome.

Labobo topic: Immuno Lec 6: Systemic Vasculitis

In 60 seconds

  • Vasculitis = inflammation and damage of vessels → narrowed lumen → ischaemia. Primary or secondary (HBV, HCV, HIV, drugs, cancer, SLE). Classified by vessel size.
  • Large: giant cell arteritis (women >50, temporal headache, jaw claudication, blindness, ESR ≥50, PMR) and Takayasu (Asian women <40, pulseless). Medium: PAN (HBV, spares lungs, rosary aneurysms), Kawasaki (children <5, coronary aneurysms), Buerger (smokers).
  • Small, ANCA: GPA (c-ANCA/PR3; sinus, lung, kidney, granulomas), MPA (p-ANCA/MPO; no sinus, no granulomas), EGPA (asthma, eosinophilia, MPO-ANCA).
  • Small, immune complex: IgA vasculitis (child after URI: palpable purpura on buttocks/legs, arthritis, abdominal pain, nephritis), cryoglobulinaemic (HCV, RF, low complement).
  • Variable: Behçet (oral and genital ulcers, uveitis, pathergy, HLA-B51). Most are treated with glucocorticoids ± cyclophosphamide, methotrexate or rituximab.

Learning objectives

Harrison’s Principles of Internal Medicine, chapter on the vasculitis syndromes (the lecture’s main source); background in Abbas, chapter 19. By the end you can:

  • define and classify vasculitis by vessel size and cause;
  • explain immune complex, ANCA and T cell/granuloma mechanisms of vessel injury;
  • recognise each syndrome by patient, organs, serology, biopsy or imaging, and treatment.

Definition, causes, classification

Vasculitis is inflammation of and damage to blood vessels; the lumen is usually compromised, causing ischaemia of the tissue supplied. Any vessel type, size or site can be involved, so syndromes are diverse. It may be the main disease or part of another, and may affect one organ (skin) or many.

  • Primary (idiopathic).
  • Secondary: infections (HBV, HCV, HIV, infective endocarditis, TB, syphilis); drugs (hydralazine, cocaine); malignancy (myeloma, lymphoproliferative disease); autoimmune disease (SLE, Sjögren’s, sarcoidosis, IBD).

Palpable purpura: raised, non-blanching red-purple lesions from blood leaking out of inflamed small vessels (leukocytoclastic vasculitis). Non-palpable purpura (petechiae, ecchymoses) is usually non-inflammatory.

Mechanisms of vessel injury

Most vasculitides are at least partly immune-mediated, shaped by genes, environmental exposures and immune regulation.

1. Immune complexes

  • The first proposed mechanism, like serum sickness: complexes formed in antigen excess deposit in vessel walls made leaky by histamine, bradykinin and leukotrienes (from platelets or IgE-triggered mast cells).
  • Complement, especially C5a, attracts neutrophils, which phagocytose the complexes and release enzymes that damage the wall; later mononuclear cells infiltrate.
  • Circulating complexes do not always deposit, and many patients have none detectable. The antigen is known in few cases, notably hepatitis B antigen in PAN-like vasculitis.

2. ANCA (next section)

3. T cells and granulomas

  • Granulomatous vasculitis implies cell-mediated injury. IFN-γ makes endothelial cells express HLA class II, so they can present antigen to CD4+ T cells.
  • Endothelial IL-1 activates T cells; IL-1 and TNF-α induce ELAM-1 (E-selectin) and VCAM-1, increasing leukocyte adhesion.

ANCA

Antibodies against proteins in the cytoplasmic granules of neutrophils and monocytes; frequent in active GPA and MPA, less in EGPA.

  1. PR3 and MPO normally sit inside resting cells, hidden from antibody.
  2. TNF-α or IL-1 primes neutrophils/monocytes → PR3 and MPO move to the cell surface.
  3. ANCA binds → neutrophils degranulate, make reactive oxygen species, form NETs, release IL-1 and IL-8, and adhere to and kill endothelial cells.

Atypical p-ANCA (not anti-MPO) occurs without vasculitis: ulcerative colitis, primary sclerosing cholangitis, other autoimmune disease, some drugs, endocarditis, airway infection in cystic fibrosis.

Granulomatosis with polyangiitis (Wegener)

ANCA-associated vasculitis of small and medium vessels with necrotising granulomatous inflammation of the upper airways, lungs and kidneys. Adults 40–60; idiopathic. PR3 is aberrantly expressed on the neutrophil membrane; PR3-ANCA activates neutrophils (mediators, NETs, complement) → endothelial damage.

  • Upper airway: chronic sinusitis/rhinitis, nasopharyngeal ulcers → septal perforation → saddle nose; chronic otitis media and mastoiditis.
  • Lung: treatment-resistant “pneumonia”: cough, dyspnoea, haemoptysis; X-ray/CT: multiple bilateral cavitating nodules.
  • Kidney: rapidly progressive (pauci-immune) glomerulonephritis.
  • Skin: papules, vesicles, ulcers, purpura of the legs; digital dry gangrene.
  • Diagnosis: PR3-ANCA (c-ANCA) and biopsy (granulomatous necrotising vasculitis). Differentiate from lymphomatoid granulomatosis (EBV+ angioinvasive B cell proliferation of lung, skin, CNS, kidney).
  • Treatment: glucocorticoids + methotrexate, cyclophosphamide or rituximab.
Watch out

The text slide says GPA is commoner in men (♂ > ♀), but the summary table on slide 5 says it affects men and women equally. If asked, the safer answer is middle-aged adults of either sex.

Microscopic polyangiitis

Necrotising ANCA-associated vasculitis of small vessels (also capillaries and venules) with lung, kidney and skin disease: “like GPA except” no upper airway disease, p-ANCA (MPO) not c-ANCA, and no granulomas. Few immunoglobulin deposits (pauci-immune), so immune complexes play no role; the renal lesion equals GPA’s.

  • Kidney ~90% (pauci-immune GN with hypertension); nerves ~70% (mononeuritis multiplex, polyneuropathy); lung ~50% (diffuse alveolar haemorrhage, haemoptysis); abdominal pain ~50%, GI bleeding ~25%; skin ~40% (palpable purpura).
  • ANCA in 50–75%: MPO-ANCA >50%, PR3 ~25%. Urine: proteinuria, haematuria, RBC casts. CT: ground-glass, nodules. Biopsy: fibrinoid necrosis with neutrophils, no granulomas.
  • Treatment: steroids and cyclophosphamide.

Eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (Churg-Strauss)

Necrotising granulomatous small-vessel vasculitis with eosinophilia, mostly of lungs and skin (also kidney, heart, gut, nerves). ANCA-associated; usually idiopathic.

  1. Prodromal: severe allergic asthma (96–100%), allergic rhinitis/sinusitis.
  2. Eosinophilic: eosinophil infiltration of lung, pericardium, gut (bleeding, ischaemia, perforation).
  3. Vasculitic: skin nodules, palpable purpura, mononeuritis multiplex (wrist or foot drop), pauci-immune GN.

Phases can overlap. Labs: ↑ESR/CRP, eosinophils >1500/µL or >10%, ANCA in ~40% (mostly MPO), ↑IgE and IgG4. Biopsy: eosinophilic necrotising vasculitis, extravascular granulomas. Treat: glucocorticoids + cyclophosphamide.

IgA vasculitis (Henoch-Schönlein purpura)

Acute immune complex small-vessel vasculitis, most often in children. Antigen (infection, drug) → more IgA → IgA complexes deposit in skin, gut, joint and kidney vessels → complement → inflammation. Related to IgA nephropathy.

  • Triggers: up to 90% follow an infection 1–3 weeks earlier, most often group A streptococcal URI, also GI infections; drugs (ACE inhibitors, clarithromycin), vaccines; genetics; solid tumours in adults.
  • Features: low fever, malaise; palpable purpura on buttocks and legs; arthritis of ankles and knees; abdominal pain, melena (can cause intussusception); nephritis. Classic case: child with recent URI, purpura, melena.
  • Diagnosis: skin biopsy with leukocytoclastic vasculitis and IgA and C3 in superficial dermal vessels (hallmark); kidney: mesangial IgA, crescents if severe; raised serum IgA.
  • Treatment: mild, NSAIDs; severe, glucocorticoids + IV hydration.

Cryoglobulinaemic vasculitis

Cryoglobulins are immunoglobulins that precipitate in the cold; types I, II, III by composition and clonality. The vasculitis is a small-vessel disease mainly from types II and III (mixed, 90%).

  • Cause: HCV in 70–90% of mixed cases: IgG and IgM rheumatoid factor form complexes with HCV antigen → complement → vasculitis. Also Sjögren’s, SLE, RA, lymphoproliferative disease, idiopathic (essential). Type I (10%): myeloma, CLL.
  • Features: cutaneous vasculitis (purpura), arthritis, peripheral neuropathy, glomerulonephritis (renal disease in 10–30%); rarely RPGN or CNS, GI, cardiac vasculitis.
  • Labs: circulating cryoprecipitates; RF almost always present; hypocomplementaemia in 90%; ↑ESR/CRP, anaemia. Always test for HCV antibody and RNA.

Giant cell (temporal) arteritis

Chronic autoimmune inflammation of large arteries: carotid branches (especially the temporal), the aorta and vertebral arteries. Most common in women over 50 of northern European descent.

  1. Adventitial dendritic cells activate and recruit Th1, CD8+ T cells and monocytes.
  2. Macrophages and giant cells make IL-6 and TNF-α → focal granulomatous inflammation.
  3. Metalloproteinases destroy wall structures (internal elastic lamina).
  4. PDGF and VEGF drive concentric intimal hyperplasia → narrowing, ischaemia.
  • Features: fever, weight loss, night sweats; new temporal headache; tender hard temporal artery; jaw claudication; vision loss (ophthalmic artery occlusion).
  • Polymyalgia rheumatica in up to 50%: shoulder, hip and neck pain with morning stiffness in women >50; 10–20% of PMR patients develop GCA.
  • Diagnosis: ESR ≥50 mm/h, ↑CRP; mild thrombocytosis, anaemia, ↑ALP, ↑fibrinogen; temporal artery biopsy (gold standard, granulomas); duplex ultrasound (halo sign, non-compressible artery).
  • Treatment: high-dose steroids at once, before the biopsy, to save vision.

Takayasu arteritis

“Aortic arch syndrome”: granulomatous inflammation of the aorta and its major branches → thickening and stenosis. Mostly Asian women under 40 (peak 15–45).

  • Constitutional symptoms; weak or absent brachial and radial pulses (“pulseless disease”); BP difference between arms; bilateral carotid bruits; syncope, angina, visual loss; limb claudication; Raynaud; hypertension from renal artery stenosis.
  • Diagnosis: clinical, confirmed by imaging (MR angiography: wall thickening and enhancement, stenosis) or biopsy; ↑ESR/CRP.
  • Treatment: high-dose glucocorticoids; surgery for stenoses.

Kawasaki disease

Acute necrotising vasculitis of medium vessels in children under 5, commoner in Asians and siblings; cause unknown (infectious and genetic factors).

  • Diagnosis needs fever ≥5 days plus ≥4 specific signs, or fewer than 4 with coronary involvement. Signs (“CRASH and burn”): bilateral non-exudative “injected” Conjunctivitis; polymorphous Rash from the trunk; cervical Adenopathy (mostly unilateral); Strawberry tongue, cracked red lips; Hand-foot erythema and oedema (week 1), fingertip desquamation after 2–3 weeks.
  • Complications: coronary artery aneurysm (highest risk in weeks 2–3; rupture or thrombosis can kill), MI, myocarditis, ventricular dysfunction.
  • Labs: ↑ESR/CRP, leukocytosis, thrombocytosis, ↑AST/ALT. Echocardiography at diagnosis, 2 weeks and 6–8 weeks.
  • Treatment: IV immunoglobulin and aspirin.
In the clinic: Reye syndrome

Aspirin given to a child (<19) with a viral illness such as influenza or varicella can cause Reye syndrome: salicylate metabolites injure hepatic mitochondria, stopping ATP production → liver failure (hyperammonaemia, metabolic acidosis, steatosis) and acute encephalopathy. Kawasaki disease is the exception where children do receive aspirin.

Polyarteritis nodosa

Systemic vasculitis of medium vessels affecting skin, nerves, muscles, joints, gut and kidneys but sparing the lungs. Immune complex (type III); mostly idiopathic, but associated with hepatitis B (about 30%).

  • Fever, weight loss, myalgia, arthralgia. Kidney ~60% (hypertension, impairment); coronary ~35% (MI risk); skin ~40% (rash, ulcers, nodules); mononeuritis multiplex, stroke; abdominal pain, melena.
  • Classic case: HBV+, nerve defects, skin nodules, purpura, renal failure.
  • Angiogram: many microaneurysms and constrictions of renal, hepatic and mesenteric arteries (“rosary sign”, string of pearls). Biopsy: transmural inflammation with fibrinoid necrosis, lesions of different ages.
  • Treatment: glucocorticoids + cyclophosphamide; antivirals for HBV-associated PAN.

Buerger disease (thromboangiitis obliterans)

Inflammatory, non-atherosclerotic occlusion of small and medium vessels of the limbs, mostly in adult men with heavy tobacco use (smoking, chewing, vaping).

  • Acute: endarteritis with neutrophils and microabscesses, highly cellular inflammatory thrombus; the internal elastic lamina stays intact. Subacute: mononuclear cells, giant cells, organising thrombus. Chronic: fibrous sheath encasing artery, vein and nerve.
  • Features: recurrent migratory superficial thrombophlebitis (tender nodules), Raynaud, claudication, rest pain, absent pulses, ulcers and gangrene/autoamputation of digits.
  • Angiography: segmental distal occlusions with corkscrew collaterals, normal proximal arteries.
  • Treatment: stop all tobacco (most effective; lowers amputation risk), protect digits from cold and injury.

Behçet syndrome

A variable-vessel vasculitis affecting arteries and veins of any size, commonest along the old Silk Road (Middle East, Mediterranean, Far East); highest in Turkey (1 in 250 adults). Rare before the late teens and after 50; sexes equally affected, but men have worse disease.

  • Pathogenesis unknown: possible triggers HSV or parvovirus; strong HLA-B51 association. Unlike other autoimmune diseases: no autoantibodies, Raynaud, serositis or photosensitivity.
  • Recurrent painful oral aphthous ulcers (95–100%, often first, last 1–4 weeks); genital ulcers (60–90%, vulva and scrotum, scar); uveitis (50–80%, bilateral, worse in men, 2–3 years after ulcers); skin (erythema nodosum, papulopustules, pyoderma gangrenosum, dermatographism).
  • Pathergy test: papule or pustule 48 h after a forearm needle prick.
  • Arthritis (30–70%): non-erosive, asymmetric, oligoarticular; GI disease; thrombophlebitis, DVT, Budd-Chiari, arterial thrombosis, pulmonary artery aneurysms; neuro-Behçet (5–10%).
  • Treatment: eye, CNS or vascular disease, systemic steroids + sparing agents (azathioprine, infliximab, cyclosporin, cyclophosphamide); ulcers, topical steroids; colchicine to prevent recurrence and first line for arthritis.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • ANCA vasculitis glomerulonephritis is pauci-immune: little or no immunoglobulin on immunofluorescence.
  • PAN spares the lungs; lung haemorrhage points to MPA or GPA.
  • In suspected GCA, start steroids before the biopsy.
  • Kawasaki’s killer is the coronary aneurysm; follow with echocardiography.
  • Aspirin is contraindicated in children with viral illness (Reye), except in Kawasaki disease.
  • Behçet has no autoantibodies; think HLA-B51 and a positive pathergy test.
  • Cryoglobulinaemic vasculitis: test every patient for hepatitis C.

Key terms

Vasculitis: inflammation and damage of vessel walls · Palpable purpura: raised non-blanching purpura of small-vessel vasculitis · Leukocytoclastic vasculitis: neutrophil-rich small-vessel vasculitis with nuclear debris · ANCA: antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody · PR3: c-ANCA antigen · MPO: p-ANCA antigen · Pauci-immune GN: glomerulonephritis without deposits · Saddle nose: collapsed nasal bridge in GPA · Mononeuritis multiplex: separate nerve infarcts · Cryoglobulin: immunoglobulin that precipitates in cold · Polymyalgia rheumatica: girdle pain and stiffness linked to GCA · Halo sign: ultrasound wall oedema in GCA · Pulseless disease: Takayasu arteritis · Rosary sign: aneurysm chain in PAN · Corkscrew collaterals: Buerger angiographic sign · Pathergy: skin hyper-reactivity to needle prick · Reye syndrome: aspirin-related liver failure and encephalopathy.
