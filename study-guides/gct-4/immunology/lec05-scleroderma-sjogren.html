Systemic sclerosis (scleroderma) and Sjögren’s syndrome

Systemic sclerosis: autoimmunity, a noninflammatory obliterative vasculopathy and progressive fibrosis of skin and organs; Raynaud’s phenomenon, limited (CREST) vs diffuse disease, the specific autoantibodies, and organ involvement. Sjögren’s syndrome: lymphocytic destruction of tear and salivary glands with dry eyes and mouth, anti-Ro/La, extraglandular disease, diagnosis and treatment.

Labobo topic: Immuno Lec 5: Scleroderma and Sjögren Syndrome

In 60 seconds

  • Systemic sclerosis (SSc): triad of autoimmunity, vasculopathy (noninflammatory obliterative microangiopathy) and fibrosis. Mostly women, onset 40–60. Profibrotic TGF-β, PDGF, CTGF.
  • Limited (CREST: Calcinosis, Raynaud, Esophageal dysmotility, Sclerodactyly, Telangiectasia): anti-centromere, risk of pulmonary arterial hypertension, better prognosis. Diffuse: anti-Scl-70 (topoisomerase I, lung fibrosis) and anti-RNA polymerase III (rapid skin, renal crisis).
  • Lung disease is the leading cause of death. Scleroderma renal crisis: malignant hypertension, treat with ACE inhibitors. Raynaud: calcium channel blockers first.
  • Sjögren’s: lymphocytic infiltration of exocrine glands → dry eyes (keratoconjunctivitis sicca) and dry mouth (xerostomia); women 40–60; primary or with RA/SLE/SSc. Anti-Ro/SSA and anti-La/SSB, RF; risk of MALT lymphoma.
  • Diagnose Sjögren’s with the ACR/EULAR score (≥4): lip biopsy with focal sialadenitis (3), anti-Ro (3), ocular staining, Schirmer ≤5 mm in 5 min, low saliva flow (1 each).

Systemic sclerosis: what it is

An acquired chronic multisystem disease with autoimmunity and inflammation, widespread abnormalities of small blood vessels, and progressive fibrosis of skin and viscera. Age, sex and ancestry shape susceptibility and outcome; it is commoner in women (about 75%), with onset usually at 40–60.

Hallmark pathology: noninflammatory obliterative microangiopathy plus fibrosis. Collagen-rich matrix (with fibulins, elastin, proteoglycans, tenascin-C, EDA-fibronectin) distorts tissue architecture and progressively impairs organs.

  • Skin: dermal expansion obliterates hair follicles, sweat and sebaceous glands.
  • Lung: early patchy lymphocytes, plasma cells, macrophages and eosinophils; then thickened alveolar septa in a nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP) pattern. Intimal thickening of pulmonary arteries is the hallmark of pulmonary hypertension.
  • GI tract: changes anywhere from mouth to rectum.
  • Heart: myocardial contraction band necrosis (ischaemia–reperfusion) and patchy fibrosis.
  • Kidney: vascular lesions predominate; glomerulonephritis is rare. Scleroderma renal crisis is uncommon, sometimes with microangiopathic haemolysis and thrombocytopenia.

Genes and triggers

  • Polygenic, not Mendelian; most susceptibility genes regulate immunity.
  • Microbes: CMV, EBV, parvovirus B19, Helicobacter pylori.
  • Environmental and occupational: polyvinyl chloride, trichloroethylene, organic solvents, heavy metals; dietary factors.
  • Drugs causing SSc-like illness: bleomycin, taxanes, pentazocine, cocaine, anorexigens (with pulmonary arterial hypertension).

Pathogenesis: vessels, immunity, fibrosis

  1. Endothelial injury (early): microvessels lose pericytes, become leaky, let leukocytes through, activate fibrinolysis and platelets → thrombosis. Adventitial fibrosis stiffens and narrows vessels.
  2. Immune activation: activated T cells and monocytes/macrophages in skin and lung release TGF-β, PDGF, CTGF, cytokines and chemokines (a cytokine burst). Virtually all patients have ANA, and many autoantibodies are highly SSc-specific.
  3. Fibrosis: sustained, amplified fibroblast activation (myofibroblasts) gives excessive matrix, replacing normal tissue with dense noncompliant connective tissue → organ failure.

Raynaud’s phenomenon

Well-demarcated colour changes of the digits triggered by cold or emotional stress, from an abnormal vasomotor response and altered vasodilators (NO, prostacyclin) and vasoconstrictors (endothelins).

  1. White: vasospasm, pallor.
  2. Blue: cyanosis from venous pooling and deoxygenated blood.
  3. Red: rewarming, rebound vasodilation, hyperaemia.
  • Primary Raynaud: no associated disease. In SSc it is severe, with many prolonged daily episodes and structural vessel disease → digital ulcers, infarction, gangrene. The vasculopathy is systemic (lung, kidney, gut, coronary vessels).
  • Treatment: avoid cold, reduce sympathetic tone (stress); calcium channel blockers are the first choice.

Clinical patterns and autoantibodies

Skin thickening (“hard skin”) is the most specific finding and separates SSc from other rheumatic diseases. Subtypes by skin extent, though outcome is better predicted by a composite of skin, autoantibodies and organ disease.

  • ANA: present but not specific.
  • Anti-topoisomerase I (Scl-70): diffuse disease, pulmonary fibrosis, worse prognosis.
  • Anti-centromere: limited disease (“CREST = centromere”), PAH, better prognosis.
  • Anti-RNA polymerase III: diffuse, rapidly progressive skin, renal crisis, cancer risk.
  • SSc sine scleroderma: no skin thickening, but Raynaud, visceral disease and autoantibodies.
  • Morphea (localised scleroderma): skin and underlying tissue only, in children and adults alike; internal organs unusual. Circumscribed (commonest, adults), generalised, and linear (children; arms, legs, forehead). En coup de sabre and Parry-Romberg syndrome can involve the CNS (headache, visual disturbance, seizures).

Early disease and skin

  • Earliest symptoms are nonspecific: fatigue, musculoskeletal stiffness or pain, feeling ill; plus reflux and dysphagia and Raynaud.
  • Swollen “puffy fingers”, intense itching and burning, pitting oedema; hyperpigmentation or vitiligo-like “salt and pepper” hypopigmentation; dilated nailfold capillaries.
  • Raynaud + abnormal nailfold capillaries + puffy fingers + an SSc-specific antibody → suspect SSc before obvious signs.
  • Skin evolves from an inflammatory phase (dermal infiltrate) to a fibrotic phase over weeks to months: loss of hair, oil and sweating, flexion contractures, tendon friction rubs, and late ulcers and dystrophic calcification. Taut skin gives a face without wrinkles and fingertip pitting.

GI tract, lung, heart, kidney, muscles

Gastrointestinal

  • Any level from oropharynx to bowel. Oesophageal smooth muscle atrophy → distal peristaltic failure, low LES pressure → GERD (heartburn, dysphagia, regurgitation), strictures, Barrett oesophagus, aspiration; delayed gastric emptying (gastroparesis). Autoantibodies to enteric neurons may contribute.
  • Bowel: diarrhoea, bloating, fat malabsorption from atony and small intestinal bacterial overgrowth, weight loss; severe pseudo-obstruction (pain, distension, vomiting).
  • Treatment: eating habits and posture, PPI or H2 blocker, prokinetics (metoclopramide, domperidone), fibre and laxatives, intermittent antibiotics; bowel rest and TPN for pseudo-obstruction.

Lung (leading cause of death)

  • Interstitial lung disease: alveolitis then fibrosis → restrictive defect and impaired gas exchange; aggressive life-threatening disease in 10–20%.
  • Pulmonary arterial hypertension: often silent for years (9–12 years after onset), then exertional dyspnoea, hypoxia and right heart failure.

Heart

Myocardial fibrosis or myocarditis, microvascular disease, LV systolic/diastolic dysfunction, RV failure, conduction defects and arrhythmias, valve disease, pericardial effusions (rarely tamponade).

Kidney

Scleroderma renal crisis: a life-threatening complication of diffuse disease with acute renal failure and marked (malignant) hypertension; proliferative vasculopathy, thrombosis, glomerular ischaemia and RAAS overactivity. Treat with ACE inhibitors. Some recover function months later and leave dialysis; transplantation is an option. Also interstitial nephritis, GN and ANCA vasculitis.

Musculoskeletal

A leading cause of disability, early, mostly in the hands: arthralgia, myalgia, stiffness, contractures, inflammatory arthritis, myopathy, carpal tunnel syndrome, tendon fibrosis, deconditioning.

Managing SSc

  • Assess: skin score (subtype), GI history, blood pressure (renal crisis), pulmonary function tests (early ILD), Doppler echo (PAH), serology (prognosis).
  • Organ-specific: vasodilators for Raynaud and PAH; PPI for reflux; ACE inhibitor for renal crisis; anti-inflammatories for arthritis; immunosuppression for ILD.
  • Disease-modifying: nintedanib (oral multikinase inhibitor) is approved for SSc-ILD; antifibrotic, biological agents and autologous stem cell transplantation are being studied.
  • Differential diagnosis: other connective tissue diseases, primary Raynaud, and skin-thickening disorders (scleredema, nephrogenic fibrosing dermopathy, eosinophilic fasciitis, chronic GVHD, drugs such as bleomycin and pentazocine).

Sjögren’s syndrome: pathogenesis

An autoimmune destruction of exocrine glands, especially lacrimal and salivary, by lymphocytic infiltrates. Mainly women of 40–60. Primary, or secondary to RA, SLE or systemic sclerosis.

  • Possible viral initiators: CMV, EBV, HHV-6, HHV-8, HTLV-1, HIV, HCV, Coxsackie.
  • Peak in menopausal women suggests low estrogen contributes; families (especially siblings) show clustering; HLA-DR alleles and genes of the IFN pathway and B cell survival (BAFF) are linked.
  • Key lesions: periepithelial lymphocytic infiltrates and B cell hyperactivity. Mild lesions are mostly CD4 T cells; advanced ones are B cell-dominated.
  • Autoreactive B cells make anti-Ro (SSA), anti-La (SSB) and RF.
  • Epithelial cells drive it (“autoimmune epithelitis”): they supply autoantigens, make cytokines and chemokines, act as APCs and activate B cells. Immune complexes cause many systemic features.
  • Chronic antigen stimulation of B cells explains the risk of lymphoma.

Sjögren’s: clinical features

Usually benign; nonspecific symptoms may precede the full syndrome by about a decade.

  • Glandular: dry eyes (blurred vision, burning, itching, gritty feeling; keratoconjunctivitis sicca), dry mouth (xerostomia; atrophic fissured tongue, candidiasis, dental caries), bilateral parotid enlargement, dry skin, nose and trachea (xerotrachea).
  • Joints: aches in small joints, non-erosive (unlike RA).
  • Lung: dry cough, rarely dyspnoea; pleurisy; peribronchiolar inflammation; ILD.
  • Liver: primary biliary cholangitis (“Sjögren’s of the liver”).
  • Kidney: tubulointerstitial nephritis with distal renal tubular acidosis (hypokalaemia, low urine specific gravity, alkaline urine, nephrocalcinosis); GN is rarer, with vasculitis, low complement and cryoglobulins.
  • Vasculitis in ~15%: mostly skin (palpable purpura); low complement and cryoglobulinaemia mark a higher risk of lymphoma and death.
  • Nervous system: sensory and sensorimotor neuropathies, mononeuritis multiplex; CNS lesions, aseptic meningitis, transverse myelitis; anxiety.

Sjögren’s: diagnosis and treatment

  • Labs: anaemia of chronic disease, leukopenia (<4000/µL), raised ESR, hypergammaglobulinaemia, ANA (speckled; may be missed on Hep2 cells, so request a solid-phase anti-Ro test), RF in ~50%, low C4, anti-Ro in about two-thirds.
  • Eyes: Schirmer test, filter paper under the lower lid, wetting measured over 5 minutes; ocular surface staining; tear break-up time.
  • Salivary glands: sialometry (saliva amount), sialography and scintigraphy (shape and function), ultrasonography, elastography.
  • Minor (labial) salivary gland biopsy: focal lymphocytic sialadenitis; valuable for diagnosis and prognosis but not obligatory.
  • Classification: older scheme of 4 of 6 (eye symptoms, oral symptoms, Schirmer, salivary tests, biopsy, anti-SSA/SSB) that must include biopsy or antibodies; the 2016 ACR/EULAR score ≥4. Exclusions: head and neck radiation, active hepatitis C, AIDS, amyloidosis, sarcoidosis, GVHD, IgG4-related disease. Drugs (antidepressants, anticholinergics, neuroleptics) also cause dryness.

Treatment: eye lubricants; for the mouth, meticulous hygiene, saliva substitutes, sugar-free gum, xylitol, and secretagogues pilocarpine and cevimeline (muscarinic M3 agonists); analgesics and short NSAID courses first for pain; glucocorticoids for active systemic disease.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • The SSc microangiopathy is noninflammatory and obliterative.
  • Scleroderma renal crisis is treated with ACE inhibitors, and anti-RNA polymerase III flags its risk.
  • Lung disease, not kidney, is now the leading cause of death in SSc.
  • First-line drug for Raynaud: calcium channel blocker.
  • Pilocarpine and cevimeline are muscarinic agonists (they stimulate secretion).
  • Sjögren’s greatest late danger is MALT lymphoma; new unilateral parotid swelling should raise it.
  • Sjögren’s kidney disease is usually tubulointerstitial with distal RTA and hypokalaemia, not GN.
