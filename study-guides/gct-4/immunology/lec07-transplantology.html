General principles of transplantology

Why grafts are rejected: graft types, MHC (HLA) molecules as the main alloantigens, direct and indirect allorecognition by T cells, matching and cross-matching, graft-versus-host disease, and the hyperacute, acute (cellular and antibody-mediated) and chronic forms of rejection.

Labobo topic: Immuno Lec 7: Transplantology

In 60 seconds

  • Grafts: autograft (same person), isograft (identical twin), allograft (same species), xenograft (other species).
  • The strongest alloantigens are MHC (HLA) molecules (chromosome 6; class I HLA-A, B, C; class II HLA-DR, DQ, DP), so polymorphic that only identical twins match completely.
  • Direct recognition: recipient T cells see intact donor MHC on donor APCs (many T cells cross-react). Indirect: recipient APCs present donor MHC peptides on self class II to CD4+ T cells.
  • Hyperacute (minutes): preformed antibodies (ABO, HLA) → complement, thrombosis; prevented by cross-match. Acute (weeks–months): T cells (tubulitis, endotheliitis) and antibodies (C4d). Chronic (years): intimal smooth muscle proliferation, vessel occlusion, fibrosis.
  • GVHD: donor T cells attack the host (skin rash, diarrhoea, liver), mainly after bone marrow transplant; a little gives a helpful graft-versus-leukaemia effect.

Learning objectives

  • name the graft types and explain why MHC molecules are the main transplant antigens;
  • compare direct and indirect allorecognition and describe how alloreactive T cells are activated;
  • explain matching, cross-matching and GVHD;
  • describe the timing, mechanism and pathology of hyperacute, acute and chronic rejection.

Transplantation and graft types

Transplantation moves cells, tissues or organs (a graft) from a donor to a recipient (host), or to another site in the same person. Transfusion transfers circulating blood cells or plasma. HSC, kidney, liver and heart transplants are routine; lung and pancreas are increasingly common.

The immune system is built to detect small changes in self (microbes, cancer); the same power makes it see other people’s tissue as “altered self”.

Alloantigens: MHC

  • Alloantigens provoke both cellular and humoral responses. Most are proteins of polymorphic genes: histocompatibility molecules (histo = tissue).
  • Strong, rapid rejection is caused by MHC molecules, which present peptides to T cells. Grafts between any two people except identical twins are rejected because MHC is so polymorphic that no two people inherit the same set.
  • In humans MHC molecules are HLA (human leukocyte antigens), encoded on chromosome 6. Class I: HLA-A, -B, -C. Class II: HLA-DR, -DQ, -DP (also -DM, -DO). Highly polymorphic: more than 50 HLA-A alleles alone, numbered A1, A2, A3…
  • Donor cells express class I, which CD8+ T cells react to; class II is on donor APCs carried in the graft and on activated vascular endothelium, which CD4+ T cells react to.

Why so many T cells react directly

  • TCRs have some intrinsic affinity for MHC, self or foreign.
  • Positive selection keeps T cells with weak self-MHC reactivity, and many of these bind allogeneic MHC strongly.
  • Negative selection removes cells with high affinity for self MHC but not those for allogeneic MHC, which is absent from the thymus. So the mature repertoire contains many high-affinity alloreactive T cells.
  • Each allogeneic MHC molecule can also yield several foreign peptides for indirect presentation, each recognised by different clones.

Activation of alloreactive T cells

  1. Most organs contain resident donor dendritic cells, which migrate to the draining lymph nodes and present unprocessed donor class I and II to recipient CD8+ and CD4+ T cells (direct).
  2. Recipient DCs also enter the graft, pick up alloantigen and carry it to the nodes (indirect).
  3. Naive T cells proliferate and become effector helper cells and CTLs: sensitisation to alloantigens.
  4. Effectors migrate back into the graft and reject it.

Many responding cells are cross-reactive memory T cells made earlier against environmental antigens. Unlike naive cells, they can go straight into the graft and be activated there without lymph node DCs.

Matching and cross-matching

The goal is to make donor and recipient tissue as alike as possible; a mismatch leads to rejection.

  • Same ABO blood group.
  • Same or close HLA class I and II.
  • A negative cross-match: donor cells are tested against recipient plasma to screen for preformed antibodies.

Bone marrow transplants and GVHD

Chemotherapy first destroys the recipient’s marrow, so grafted cells must rebuild every blood cell line. Donors are matched for HLA-A, -B, -DR and -C (sometimes -DQ, -DP). A mismatch causes two problems: rejection of the new cells and graft-versus-host disease.

  • GVHD: immunocompetent donor T cells (CD8) proliferate in an immunocompromised host and attack host cells as foreign, the opposite of rejection; a type IV reaction.
  • Targets: skin (maculopapular rash), GI tract (diarrhoea, abdominal pain), liver (raised LFTs and bilirubin, jaundice), hepatosplenomegaly.
  • Mostly after bone marrow and liver transplants (organs rich in lymphocytes).
  • Some GVHD helps: donor cells kill residual cancer cells (graft-versus-leukaemia effect), with fewer relapses and better survival.
  • Prevention for immunocompromised patients: irradiate blood products before transfusion.

Hyperacute rejection

  • Occurs within minutes (to hours) of joining the graft vessels to the host’s.
  • Cause: preformed antibodies in the recipient against donor ABO or HLA antigens on endothelium, from earlier exposure: pregnancy, transfusion, previous transplant.
  • Antibody activates complement → endothelial injury exposes basement membrane → platelets activate; endothelium secretes high-molecular-weight von Willebrand factor; shed membrane particles promote coagulation; endothelium loses heparan sulfate that normally works with antithrombin III.
  • Result: vessel spasm, intravascular thrombosis, irreversible ischaemic necrosis (“white rejection”). Rare, usually untreatable; the graft must be removed.
  • Prevented by cross-matching before grafting. It is a type II reaction.

Acute rejection

Injury of graft parenchyma and vessels by alloreactive T cells and antibodies, usually weeks to months after transplant (the time to make effectors), but possible years later if immunosuppression is reduced. Cellular and humoral forms usually coexist.

Acute cellular rejection

  • CTLs kill parenchymal and endothelial cells; helper T cells (IFN-γ/TNF-secreting Th1, IL-17-secreting Th17) activate macrophages and endothelium.
  • Kidney biopsy: lymphocyte and macrophage infiltrates in tubules (tubulitis, tubular necrosis) and vessels (endotheliitis, necrosis of capillary and small artery walls).
  • Adoptive transfer of either CD4+ or CD8+ alloreactive T cells causes rejection in mice.

Acute antibody-mediated rejection

  • Alloantibodies (mainly anti-HLA, made after transplant) bind endothelium → complement (lysis, neutrophils, thrombosis); Fc receptors on neutrophils and NK cells kill endothelial cells; signalling makes endothelium proinflammatory and procoagulant.
  • Kidney: acute inflammation of glomeruli and peritubular capillaries with focal thrombosis. C4d in capillaries marks classical complement activation and humoral rejection.

Acute rejection can be prevented or reversed with immunosuppressants.

Chronic rejection

  • Months to years after transplant; complex, involving both cellular and humoral immunity (types II and IV).
  • Dominant lesion: arterial occlusion from intimal smooth muscle proliferation (graft arteriosclerosis); grafts fail from ischaemia, with parenchymal atrophy and interstitial fibrosis.
  • Organ examples: kidney, fibrosis of capillaries and glomeruli (chronic allograft nephropathy); heart, narrowed coronaries (accelerated atherosclerosis); lung, bronchiolitis obliterans; liver, vanishing bile duct syndrome.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • Sensitising events for hyperacute rejection: pregnancy, transfusion, previous graft.
  • The cross-match (donor cells + recipient serum) prevents hyperacute rejection; HLA typing does not detect antibodies.
  • Hyperacute rejection is type II; acute cellular is type IV; chronic is both.
  • GVHD is most common after bone marrow transplant and attacks skin, gut and liver.
  • Graft-versus-leukaemia is a benefit of mild GVHD.
  • Chronic lung rejection = bronchiolitis obliterans; chronic heart rejection = coronary narrowing.
