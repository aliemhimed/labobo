Acquired immunodeficiencies

Immunodeficiency that develops during life rather than being inherited: malnutrition, cancer, drugs, loss of the spleen and, above all, HIV. How HIV enters and destroys CD4+ T cells, the three phases of the disease, how it is diagnosed and treated, and why the immune response fails to clear it.

Labobo topic: Immuno Lec 1: Acquired Immunodeficiencies

In 60 seconds

  • Primary immunodeficiencies are genetic and usually show in infancy; secondary (acquired) ones come from malnutrition, cancer, immunosuppressive drugs, splenectomy or infection, most importantly HIV.
  • HIV gp120 binds CD4 plus a coreceptor, CCR5 (macrophages, early) or CXCR4 (T cells, late); gp41 fuses the membranes. Homozygous CCR5 mutation = resistance.
  • Reverse transcriptase makes DNA, integrase inserts it as a provirus, protease cuts polyproteins to make mature virus.
  • Three phases: acute HIV syndrome (3–6 weeks) → clinical latency (years, CD4 falls slowly) → AIDS (CD4 <200 cells/mm³, opportunistic infections, tumours, wasting, CNS disease).
  • Screen with the HIV-1/2 antibody + p24 antigen immunoassay; viral load (RT-PCR) is the best prognostic marker. Treat with combination antiretroviral therapy (HAART).

Learning objectives

Abbas, Lichtman & Pillai, Cellular and Molecular Immunology, chapter 21 (acquired immunodeficiencies and HIV). By the end you can:

  • list the causes of secondary immunodeficiency and the mechanism of each;
  • describe the HIV virion, its genes, receptors and life cycle;
  • explain how HIV depletes CD4+ T cells and why the immune response fails;
  • describe the clinical course, AIDS-defining conditions, diagnosis and treatment.

Primary vs secondary immunodeficiency

Primary (congenital)Secondary (acquired)
CauseGenetic defects of the immune systemNot inherited: a consequence of another condition or its treatment
When it showsUsually infancy and early childhood, sometimes laterAny time in life
Examples(Covered in GCT III)Malnutrition, disseminated cancer, immunosuppressive drugs, infection of immune cells (HIV → AIDS)

Acquired deficiencies arise in three ways: as a biological complication of another disease, as an iatrogenic complication of therapy, or from an infection that targets immune cells.

Causes of acquired immunodeficiency

  • Protein-calorie malnutrition (common in lower-income countries): metabolic derangements impair maturation and function of immune cells, so both cellular and humoral immunity suffer. Deficient protein, fat, vitamins and minerals all contribute.
  • Cancer: tumours in the marrow (metastases, leukaemias) crowd out normal leukocyte development; tumours can also secrete substances that impair lymphocytes.
  • Infections: HIV above all. Chronic Mycobacterium tuberculosis and fungal infections cause anergy to many antigens; chronic parasitic infections also suppress immunity.
  • Iatrogenic: drugs that kill or inactivate lymphocytes or block cytokines, given on purpose for inflammatory disease or to prevent graft rejection. Cancer chemotherapy kills proliferating cells, including lymphocytes and leukocyte precursors, so it is almost always followed by a period of immunosuppression and infection risk.
  • Absent spleen (after trauma, as treatment of haematological disease, or functional loss in sickle cell disease): high risk from encapsulated bacteria, especially pneumococci and meningococci, whose polysaccharide capsules are normally cleared by opsonisation and phagocytosis in the spleen.

The HIV virion

AIDS is the disease caused by HIV: profound immunosuppression with opportunistic infections (infections by microbes that do not usually harm healthy people), malignant tumours, wasting and neurocognitive disorders.

  • A retrovirus with a diploid genome: two identical strands of (+) RNA inside a protein core, surrounded by a lipid envelope taken from the host cell membrane during budding, studded with viral glycoproteins.
  • env → gp160, cleaved into gp120 (attachment to CD4) and gp41 (fusion and entry).
  • gag → p24 (capsid) and p17 (matrix).
  • pol → Reverse transcriptase, Integrase, Protease (“RIP Pol”).
  • Regulatory genes: tat upregulates transcription; rev moves viral RNA to the cytoplasm; nef (long-term survivors may carry virus lacking functional nef).

Receptors and entry

HIV infects only cells that carry CD4 and a chemokine coreceptor. The main target is the CD4+ T cell; macrophages and dendritic cells are also infected.

CoreceptorCellsStage
CCR5Macrophages (M-tropic strains)Early infection
CXCR4T cellsLate infection
  1. gp120 binds CD4.
  2. A conformational change in gp120 lets it bind the chemokine receptor (CCR5 or CXCR4).
  3. gp41 changes shape and exposes its hydrophobic fusion peptide, which inserts into the cell membrane.
  4. The viral and cell membranes fuse; the core enters the cytoplasm.
  • Macrophages carry about 20 times less CD4 than CD4+ T cells, and less CCR5 and CXCR4. Strains whose Env has very high CD4 affinity can still infect them (M-tropic strains).
  • Homozygous CCR5 mutation → resistance to infection; heterozygous → slower course.

Life cycle

  1. Binding and fusion (above); the virus disrupts its own core with the protease it carries, releasing RNA.
  2. Reverse transcription: the virion’s reverse transcriptase copies ss(+)RNA into dsDNA with long terminal repeats.
  3. Integration: DNA and integrase move to the nucleus; the DNA is inserted into host DNA as the provirus.
  4. Latency: the provirus may stay silent for months or years, so infection of a single cell can be latent.
  5. Activation: cytokines or other signals that activate the cell (and co-infections such as mycobacteria) switch on transcription of the provirus.
  6. Transcription gives ss(+)RNA: spliced (cleaved) RNA is used as mRNA, unspliced RNA becomes new genomes.
  7. Translation yields polyproteins that HIV protease cleaves.
  8. Assembly and budding; the new virion matures and infects other cells.

Transmission

Virus in blood, semen or other body fluids enters a new host. Reservoir: human T helper cells and macrophages.

  • Sexual contact is the most frequent route, heterosexual (most frequent in Africa and Asia) or between men. In sub-Saharan Africa more than half of those infected are women.
  • Mother-to-child: most paediatric AIDS; mostly in utero or during birth, also through breast milk.
  • Blood: mostly shared needles among intravenous drug users; HIV stays infectious in a used needle for up to 6 weeks in temperate climates. Screening has made transfusion a negligible source.

Pathogenesis

HIV disease starts as an acute infection, only partly controlled, and progresses to chronic infection of peripheral lymphoid tissues.

  1. Dendritic cells in the epithelium at the site of entry capture the virus and carry it to lymph nodes, where they pass it to CD4+ T cells by direct contact.
  2. Within days, replication is detectable in lymph nodes and gut lymphoid tissue; this produces viraemia with an acute HIV syndrome of nonspecific viral symptoms.
  3. Viraemia spreads the virus to helper T cells, macrophages and DCs throughout peripheral lymphoid tissue.
  4. Humoral and cell-mediated responses partly control it: viraemia falls to low but detectable levels by about 12 weeks.
  5. Chronic phase: lymph nodes, spleen and gut keep replicating virus and destroying cells, yet few symptoms appear (clinical latency). Blood CD4+ counts fall steadily over years until the immune system fails.
  • More than 90% of plasma virus comes from short-lived activated and memory CD4+ T cells.
  • About two-thirds of the HIV reservoir is in mucosal immune tissue, mainly the gastrointestinal tract; about one-third is in lymph nodes.

How HIV causes immunodeficiency

  • Direct killing of infected T helper cells by apoptosis or osmotic lysis.
  • CTLs kill infected T helper cells; antibodies mark them for ADCC.
  • CD4 bound by gp120 cannot interact with MHC class II on APCs, so T cell responses are inhibited; gp120 binding may also send signals that switch helper function down.
  • Macrophages are infected but relatively resistant to being killed, so they become a reservoir (with dendritic cells); infected macrophages present antigen and secrete cytokines poorly.

Clinical course and AIDS

Followed by two measures: plasma viral load and blood CD4+ T cell count. The CDC stage is based mainly on the CD4 count; the percentage is used only if the count is missing.

PhaseWhat happens
Acute HIV syndrome3–6 weeks after infection; viraemia; fever, headache, sore throat with pharyngitis, generalised lymphadenopathy, rash. CD4 falls modestly, often back to normal
Clinical latencyMay last many years; virus contained in lymphoid tissue, CD4 losses replaced from progenitors; no symptoms or minor infections. Other infections stimulate HIV production and speed the decline
AIDSCD4 <200 cells/mm³; opportunistic infections, neoplasms, cachexia (wasting), HIV nephropathy, CNS disease

AIDS-defining conditions

  • Protozoa: Toxoplasma, Cryptosporidium. Bacteria: Mycobacterium avium, Nocardia, Salmonella. Fungi: Candida, Cryptococcus neoformans, Coccidioides, Histoplasma, Pneumocystis. Viruses: CMV, herpes simplex, varicella-zoster.
  • Tumours, many virus-driven: EBV-associated B cell lymphoma and Kaposi sarcoma (a herpesvirus, HHV-8) are the commonest; also cervical carcinoma.
  • Cachexia from inflammatory cytokines such as TNF acting on appetite and metabolism.
  • CNS disease from neuronal damage by the virus or shed proteins (gp120, Tat) and cytokines from infected microglia.

Because CD4+ helper cells are needed for both cell-mediated and humoral immunity, their loss is the main reason patients become susceptible to so many infections.

Diagnosis

  • After infection comes an eclipse period when no test detects HIV. Earliest markers: HIV RNA at 9–12 days, p24 antigen at 14–19 days; antibody at about 3 weeks, persisting for life. The time to antibody detection is the seroconversion window period.
  • Screening: HIV-1/2 antigen/antibody combination immunoassay (p24 + IgG to HIV-1/2), very sensitive and specific. If positive, an antibody differentiation assay distinguishes HIV-1 from HIV-2; if that is negative or indeterminate, an HIV-1 nucleic acid test (NAT) decides between acute infection and no infection. Western blot is no longer recommended.
  • Viral load (RT-PCR for plasma RNA): best prognostic indicator and used to monitor therapy. PCR for provirus: diagnosis in newborns (maternal antibody makes antibody tests useless). CD4:CD8 ratio: tracks progression.
  • AIDS diagnosis: CD4 ≤200 cells/mm³ (normal 500–1500) or HIV positive with an AIDS-defining condition (for example Pneumocystis pneumonia).

Immune responses and evasion

Innate

The infected cell detects the virus, makes interferons, and turns on antiviral genes that make host restriction factors, proteins that block HIV at different steps (and that HIV proteins have evolved to counter). Also: defensins, NK cells, plasmacytoid DCs making type I IFN, and complement.

Adaptive

  • First: expansion of HIV-specific CD8+ CTLs, which control early infection but fail as escape mutants appear. CD4+ T cells also help control the virus.
  • Antibodies appear 6–9 weeks after infection; the most immunogenic targets are the envelope glycoproteins, with high titres of anti-gp120 and anti-gp41.
  • About 1% of infected people never develop disease for at least 10–15 years (high CD4 and CD8 counts, no therapy needed).

Evasion

  • Extremely high mutation rate: gp120 changes rapidly, so antibodies against it become ineffective.
  • HIV downregulates MHC class I on infected cells, so CTLs cannot kill them in chronic disease.
Watch out

The slides write “p120” and “p41”; they mean the envelope glycoproteins gp120 and gp41. Slide 38 also says the immune response “reverses the spread”; in the book the response only partly controls HIV and never eliminates it.

Treatment and vaccines

The goal is to control viral proliferation and the infectious complications. Highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART) combines drugs against reverse transcriptase, protease and integrase, started early.

Life-cycle stepDrug class (example)
BindingCCR5 antagonist (maraviroc)
FusionFusion inhibitor (enfuvirtide)
Reverse transcriptionNRTIs and NNRTIs (names often end in “-ine”)
IntegrationIntegrase inhibitor (raltegravir)
Maturation (budding)Protease inhibitors (“-inavir”)

Resistance is common for RT and protease inhibitors (cross-resistance). A successful vaccine would need innate activation, high-titre neutralising antibodies, strong T cell and mucosal immunity, and cover all HIV subgroups; none exists yet. Prevention: safe sex and education, blood and organ screening, infection control.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • Homozygous CCR5 deletion gives resistance; heterozygous only slows the disease.
  • Asplenic patients are at risk from encapsulated bacteria (pneumococcus, meningococcus), not from viruses.
  • Clinical latency is not viral latency: the virus keeps replicating in lymphoid tissue.
  • Antibody tests fail in newborns of HIV-positive mothers; use PCR/viral load.
  • The commonest AIDS tumours are EBV B cell lymphoma and Kaposi sarcoma.
  • CTLs fail late because of MHC class I downregulation; antibodies fail because gp120 mutates.
  • Chronic TB and fungal infections cause anergy, a recognised secondary immunodeficiency.

Key terms

Secondary immunodeficiency: acquired, non-inherited loss of immune function · Iatrogenic: caused by treatment · Anergy: failure to respond to antigens · Opportunistic infection: disease from a microbe harmless to healthy people · gp120: HIV envelope protein that binds CD4 · gp41: HIV fusion protein · p24: capsid antigen · CCR5 / CXCR4: HIV coreceptors · Provirus: integrated HIV DNA · Reverse transcriptase: RNA-to-DNA enzyme · Integrase: inserts viral DNA · Protease: cuts viral polyproteins · Clinical latency: symptom-free chronic phase · Viral load: plasma HIV RNA · Window period: infection to antibody detection · Restriction factors: host antiviral proteins · Escape mutant: virus with altered epitopes · HAART: combination antiretroviral therapy · Cachexia: wasting syndrome.
