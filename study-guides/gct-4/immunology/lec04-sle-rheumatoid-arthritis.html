Systemic lupus erythematosus and rheumatoid arthritis

Two classic systemic autoimmune diseases. SLE: autoantibodies to nuclear antigens, immune complex injury of skin, joints, kidneys, blood, serosa and brain, its criteria, drug-induced lupus and antiphospholipid antibodies. RA: a symmetric destructive synovitis with rheumatoid factor and anti-CCP antibodies, pannus, hand deformities, extra-articular disease and treatment.

Labobo topic: Immuno Lec 4: SLE and Rheumatoid Arthritis

In 60 seconds

  • SLE: chronic relapsing multisystem disease, mostly women of reproductive age. Hallmark: autoantibodies, above all ANA and anti-dsDNA; organ damage mainly type III (complexes), partly type II (cytopenias).
  • Low C3/C4; deficiency of early complement (C1q, C2, C4) in ~5% → poor clearance of complexes and apoptotic cells. UV → apoptosis → nuclear antigens; pDCs make lots of IFN-α.
  • “RASH OR PAIN”: rash, arthritis, serositis, haematologic, oral ulcers, renal, ANA, photosensitivity, immunologic, neurologic. Classified with 4 of 11 criteria. Deaths: renal disease, infection, cardiovascular disease.
  • Drug-induced lupus (INH, hydralazine, procainamide): anti-histone, kidney/CNS spared, resolves on stopping.
  • RA: symmetric synovitis of MCP, PIP and wrist (DIP spared), morning stiffness >1 h, pannus erodes cartilage and bone. RF (IgM anti-IgG Fc) and anti-CCP/ACPA (more specific). HLA-DR4, smoking. Treat with DMARDs (methotrexate) and biologics (anti-TNF).

Learning objectives

Abbas, Lichtman & Pillai, Cellular and Molecular Immunology, chapter 15 (autoimmune diseases). By the end you can:

  • explain the pathogenesis of SLE, its autoantibodies and the role of complement, IFN-α and sex hormones;
  • describe SLE’s skin, joint, renal, cardiac, pulmonary, haematological and neuropsychiatric features and its classification;
  • explain how RA damages joints, its antibodies, criteria, deformities and extra-articular features, and outline treatment.

SLE: overview and autoantibodies

A chronic, remitting and relapsing multisystem autoimmune disease, mainly of women. The cause is unknown; genes and environment (viruses, UV light) break self-tolerance. Main features: rashes, arthritis, glomerulonephritis; haemolytic anaemia, thrombocytopenia and neuropsychiatric disease are also common. Flares and remissions; fever, weight loss, fatigue, lymphadenopathy.

  • ANA (antinuclear antibodies), especially anti-DNA, are the most frequent; others target ribonucleoproteins, histones and nucleolar antigens.
  • ANA is sensitive but not specific: also found in ~5% of healthy people and in other autoimmune diseases. A negative ANA makes SLE very unlikely. Reported as a titre; often 1:160 is taken as positive.
  • Immune complexes deposit in small arteries and capillaries → glomerulonephritis, arthritis, vasculitis (type III). Antibodies against red cells and platelets → haemolytic anaemia and thrombocytopenia (type II).
  • Complement is consumed: low C3, C4 and CH50.
  • In an anti-SSA (Ro)-positive pregnant woman the baby may get neonatal lupus (congenital heart block, rash, cytopenias).

Antiphospholipid antibodies

Occur with lupus or alone. Antibodies against phospholipid-binding proteins cause: antiphospholipid syndrome; venous and arterial thrombosis (DVT, stroke, fetal loss); a prolonged PTT; and a false-positive syphilis test (RPR/VDRL).

SLE: pathogenesis

  1. UV light and other insults cause apoptosis (UV breaks keratinocyte DNA), releasing nuclear antigens.
  2. Defective clearance of apoptotic cells and complexes (for example C1q, C2, C4 deficiency, ~5% of patients) keeps the antigens around.
  3. Self-reactive B cells that escaped tolerance take up nucleic acid-containing antigens; TLRs that recognise DNA and RNA activate them.
  4. Plasmacytoid DCs make abnormally large amounts of IFN-α, further activating B cells and APCs.
  5. IgG autoantibodies form complexes that deposit and inflame vessels (vasculopathy).
  • Genetic factors include particular HLA alleles; disordered B cell tolerance, antigen presentation to effector T cells and Tregs, and cytokines are central.
  • Sex hormones: estrogen and prolactin promote loss of B cell tolerance in mice; estrogen may inhibit activation-induced T cell death by lowering Fas ligand. Prolactin is raised in ~20% of patients.

Chronic (CCLE)Discoid lesions: plaques with follicular plugging and scale → scarring annular lesions with atrophy and telangiectasia

Triggers: UV light (UVA and UVB), infections, drugs. Autoantibodies at the dermal–epidermal junction may mediate ADCC. Also oral or nasal ulcers (usually painless) and Raynaud phenomenon (white/blue painful fingertips in cold from arterial vasospasm, possibly ulcers).

  • Arthritis: symmetric, small joints of hands, wrists and knees, non-erosive; large-joint or single-joint disease is less typical. Myalgia is very common.
  • Tendinitis is attributed to SLE only with tendon rupture (Achilles, knee).
  • Avascular necrosis in up to 30%, mostly the femoral head (groin pain on weight bearing).

Kidney, heart, lung, blood, brain

  • Kidney (lupus nephritis, DNA–anti-DNA complexes in glomeruli): nephritic or nephrotic syndrome or both; a common cause of death. Diffuse proliferative GN is the commonest and most severe (nephritic); membranous GN gives nephrotic syndrome.
  • Heart: Libman-Sacks endocarditis, nonbacterial verrucous vegetations on both sides of the mitral leaflet (tricuspid less often); pericarditis is frequent; myocardial dysfunction is usually from other causes (hypertension, drugs, CAD), sometimes immune myocarditis.
  • Lung: pleuritis (tachypnoea, friction rub, effusions); lupus pneumonitis in up to 10% (dyspnoea, cough, pleuritic pain, fever); interstitial lung disease, pulmonary hypertension, pulmonary haemorrhage (with or without haemoptysis).
  • Blood (“penias”, type II): autoimmune haemolytic anaemia by warm IgG opsonising antibodies cleared in the spleen; leukopenia (autoantibodies); thrombocytopenia; aplastic anaemia from antibodies to marrow progenitors.
  • Nervous system (lupus cerebritis): cognitive dysfunction, mood disorder, psychosis, seizures, stroke, aseptic meningitis, myelopathy; peripheral neuropathies.

Classification, drug-induced lupus, treatment

Classified as SLE with 4 of 11 criteria: malar rash, discoid rash, photosensitivity, oral ulcers, arthritis, serositis, renal disorder, neurologic disorder, haematologic disorder (“penias”), ANA, immunologic (anti-dsDNA, anti-Sm or antiphospholipid). Anti-dsDNA and anti-Sm are the specific ones.

CauseUnknownIsoniazid, hydralazine, procainamide

Treatment aims to stop organ inflammation, prevent irreversible damage and flares: steroids, other immunosuppressants, avoiding sunlight. Causes of death: renal failure, infection (from immunosuppression), coronary disease (accelerated). Mixed connective tissue disease: features of SLE, systemic sclerosis and/or polymyositis with anti-U1 RNP.

RA: what it is

An autoimmune inflammation of the synovium with destruction of joint cartilage and bone, affecting small and large joints (fingers, toes, wrists, shoulders, knees, ankles). More common in women; onset usually 40–60 years.

  • The synovium is a thin lining of joints and tendon sheaths that secretes hyaluronic acid. In RA it is inflamed (excess TNF and IL-6) and hypertrophies into pannus: proliferative granulation tissue full of inflammatory cells that erodes cartilage and bone; synovial fluid increases.
  • Synovium contains CD4+ Th1 and Th17 cells, activated B cells, plasma cells and macrophages, with IL-1, IL-8, TNF, IL-6, IL-17, IFN-γ. Increased osteoclast activity destroys bone.
  • Rheumatoid factor (RF): IgM (or IgG) autoantibody against the Fc part of the patient’s own IgG; present in ~80%.
  • ACPA / anti-CCP: antibodies to citrullinated proteins; more specific than RF.
Watch out

Slide 27 labels RA “antibody-mediated, type III hypersensitivity”, while the Abbas table on the same slide lists it among T cell-mediated diseases (Th17 and Th1) with a contributing role for antibodies and immune complexes. Expect both: T cells drive the synovitis, RF/ACPA complexes add to it.

RA: pathogenesis

A complex disorder in which genes and environment break tolerance.

  1. Environmental insults, especially smoking and some infections, cause citrullination of self proteins (in lung, gums, gut).
  2. Citrullinated epitopes are neoantigens with no tolerance; HLA-DRB1 (“shared epitope”, HLA-DR4) alleles present them.
  3. T cells and ACPAs then attack joints, where the modified proteins are also present.

Other risk factors: female sex, age, PTPN22 variants, family history, hormonal factors, low alcohol intake, urban air pollutants. Smoking >25 cigarettes a day for >20 years gives a 15-fold risk in carriers of disease-associated HLA-DRB1. Known autoantigens include IgG Fc (RF), cyclic citrullinated peptides (anti-CCP), fibrinogen, enolase, vimentin and collagen II.

RA: joints and extra-articular disease

  • Symmetric, gradual onset; pain, swelling and morning stiffness >1 hour, improving with use; systemic symptoms (fever, fatigue, weight loss).
  • Classically MCP and PIP joints and wrists; DIP and first CMC spared. Also elbows, knees, hips, toes, cervical spine (subluxation).
  • Deformities: ulnar deviation (MCP swelling), swan neck (PIP hyperextended, DIP flexed), boutonnière (PIP flexed, DIP hyperextended).
  • X-ray: erosions, juxta-articular osteopenia, soft tissue swelling, subchondral cysts, joint space narrowing.
  • Baker’s (popliteal) cyst: synovium-lined sac behind the knee, continuous with the joint; rupture mimics DVT.
  • Subcutaneous (rheumatoid) nodules in 20–35%, almost always RF-positive; commonly at the elbow; central necrosis ringed by macrophages and lymphocytes.
  • Serositis: pleuritis and pleural effusion, pericarditis and pericardial effusion.
  • Eyes: episcleritis (red, painful, no discharge), scleritis (often bilateral, deep pain on eye movement), uveitis.

RA: diagnosis and treatment

2010 ACR/EULAR: score joints (0–5), serology RF/ACPA (0–3), synovitis duration ≥6 weeks (0–1), raised ESR or CRP (0–1); ≥6 of 10 = definite RA.

  • Early, tight control: education, monitoring of disease activity and yearly imaging; NSAIDs or COX-2 inhibitors for pain; glucocorticoids (also intra-articular).
  • Early DMARDs: methotrexate, sulfasalazine.
  • Biologics: cytokine blockade (anti-TNF), IL-6 receptor blockade, IL-1 antagonist, blocking B7:CD28 co-stimulation (CTLA-4-Ig), B cell depletion with anti-CD20.
  • Also: vaccinate (influenza, pneumococcus, zoster) before immunosuppression, protect bone, watch cardiovascular risk and drug toxicity.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • In SLE, complement levels are low (consumed), and inherited early complement deficiency predisposes to SLE.
  • Antiphospholipid antibodies prolong the PTT yet cause thrombosis, not bleeding.
  • Libman-Sacks vegetations are sterile and on both sides of the mitral valve.
  • Drug-induced lupus spares the kidney and CNS and has anti-histone antibodies.
  • RA spares DIP joints; morning stiffness lasts more than an hour and improves with use.
  • Rheumatoid nodules almost always mean RF-positive disease.
  • A ruptured Baker’s cyst can mimic a DVT.
