Gram-negative cocci: Neisseria

The two pathogenic Neisseria. N. meningitidis: capsule, carriage in the nasopharynx, meningitis and fulminant meningococcaemia with Waterhouse-Friderichsen syndrome, vaccines and prophylaxis. N. gonorrhoeae: pili and antigenic variation, urethritis and cervicitis, pelvic inflammatory disease, Fitz-Hugh-Curtis syndrome, neonatal conjunctivitis, and why there is no vaccine.

Labobo topic: Micro Lec 5: Gram-Negative Cocci (Neisseria)

In 60 seconds

  • Neisseria: gram-negative kidney-shaped diplococci, aerobic, non-motile, oxidase positive. Pathogens are found inside neutrophils; commensals stay outside cells.
  • Both have LOS (lipooligosaccharide: no long O antigen, still strongly endotoxic). Meningococcus has a capsule; gonococcus has none but carries plasmids.
  • N. meningitidis: droplets, nasopharyngeal carriage (up to 35% in close quarters); sudden meningitis or fulminant meningococcaemia with haemorrhagic rash, DIC and Waterhouse-Friderichsen syndrome. Late complement (C5–C9) deficiency predisposes. Ceftriaxone; conjugate vaccine (A, C, Y, W-135) and rifampin for contacts.
  • N. gonorrhoeae: sexual and perinatal spread; only piliated forms are virulent; urethritis in men, often silent cervicitis in women → PID, infertility, ectopic pregnancy, Fitz-Hugh-Curtis.
  • Gonorrhoea: ceftriaxone plus treatment for Chlamydia; Thayer-Martin medium; maltose not fermented (meningococcus ferments it). Reinfection is common because pili vary antigenically, so there is no vaccine.

The genus Neisseria

  • Gram-negative diplococci that look like paired kidney beans; aerobic, non-motile, non-spore forming.
  • Many species are harmless residents of the human respiratory tract and stay extracellular. The two pathogens, N. gonorrhoeae and N. meningitidis, are often seen attached to or inside polymorphonuclear leukocytes: a key diagnostic clue.
  • The two share about 70% of their DNA. Differences: meningococcus has a polysaccharide capsule (a major virulence factor), gonococcus does not; gonococci commonly carry plasmids, meningococci usually do not.

Surface antigens and growth

  • LOS (lipooligosaccharide): like LPS but without the long O-antigen chains; still strongly endotoxic, driving inflammation and tissue damage.
  • Pili (fimbriae): attach firmly to host cells and resist phagocytosis.
  • Outer membrane proteins: Por forms channels; Opa mediates tight adhesion to host receptors; Rmp stabilises the Por pores.
  • Growth: aerobic, usually with added CO₂; oxidase positive (reagent turns dark purple rapidly). N. gonorrhoeae is fastidious, growing best on chocolate agar or enriched media; colonies at 18–24 h, well developed by 48 h.

Meningococcus: pathogenesis

  • Polysaccharide capsule: antiphagocytic and antigenic; 5 common serogroups; used for serogrouping, CSF detection and vaccines. Group B is poorly immunogenic because its capsule is sialic acid resembling host molecules, and it is the commonest strain in developed countries.
  • IgA protease: allows colonisation of the oropharynx.
  • Endotoxin (LOS): fever and septic shock in meningococcaemia; outer membrane is overproduced and shed as blebs.
  • Pili and outer membrane proteins: colonisation and invasion.
  • Deficiency of late complement components (C5–C9) predisposes to recurrent neisserial bacteraemia.

Meningococcal disease

  • Meningitis, the commonest complication of meningococcal bacteraemia: sudden intense headache, vomiting, photophobia, confusion and neck stiffness, possibly coma within hours. The meninges are acutely inflamed with vessel thrombosis and a thick purulent exudate of neutrophils over the brain.
  • Fulminant meningococcaemia: high fever and a haemorrhagic rash, DIC and circulatory collapse with bilateral haemorrhagic necrosis of the adrenals and adrenal failure: Waterhouse-Friderichsen syndrome. Small vessels thrombose in many organs, with petechial haemorrhages; also interstitial myocarditis, arthritis and skin lesions.
  • DIC: widespread clotting in small vessels blocks the circulation and damages organs, while clotting factors are consumed, so severe bleeding follows.

Meningeal signs

  • Kernig’s sign: supine with the hip flexed to 90°; positive if passive extension of the knee causes pain.
  • Brudzinski’s sign: supine; passive flexion of the neck causes reflex flexion of hip and knee.

Epidemiology, treatment, prevention

  • Humans are the only natural host; spread by airborne droplets; the organism colonises the nasopharynx as transient flora and carriers are asymptomatic. From there it may enter the blood and reach the meninges or joints, or disseminate.
  • Carriage up to 35% in people living in close quarters; outbreaks in student dormitories.
  • S. pneumoniae and N. meningitidis cause more than 80% of bacterial meningitis in infants over 2 months. Group A causes epidemics; group B causes many cases in developed countries because it is not in the vaccine.
  • Diagnosis: Gram stain of CSF, PCR, latex agglutination.
  • Treatment: ampicillin and cefotaxime in neonates and infants; cefotaxime or ceftriaxone ± vancomycin in children and adults.
  • Prevention: conjugate vaccine against A, C, Y, W-135 (MCV4, a T cell-dependent response), recommended from age 11 with a booster at 16 and from 9 months in high-risk children; a separate 4CMenB vaccine covers group B in the EU. Close contacts get rifampin (or ciprofloxacin).

Gonococcus: epidemiology and pathogenesis

  • Fastidious: chocolate agar or enriched media with CO₂; small, smooth, non-pigmented colonies at 18–24 h, 2–4 mm by 48 h. Only piliated forms are virulent.
  • Infects humans only, by sexual contact and vertically during birth. Survival on surfaces is brief and non-sexual spread is negligible. Asymptomatic carriers are the main reservoir: about half of infected women have no symptoms. Highest rates in women aged 15–19 and men aged 20–24.
  • Not part of normal flora. It attaches to epithelium with pili and Opa proteins, targets mucous membranes of the genitourinary tract (also rectum, throat, eyes), and causes acute purulent inflammation with possible deeper invasion.
  • Immune evasion: IgA protease destroys secretory IgA; porin protein interferes with complement by inactivating C3b; antigenic variation of pili prevents lasting immunity, so reinfection is common and no vaccine exists.

Complications and the newborn

  • Pelvic inflammatory disease: infection of uterus, fallopian tubes and ovaries. Pelvic or lower abdominal pain, dyspareunia and cervical motion tenderness (the “chandelier sign”). Long-term: infertility and ectopic pregnancy.
  • Fitz-Hugh-Curtis syndrome: perihepatitis of Glisson’s capsule with sharp, pleuritic right upper quadrant pain and “violin-string” adhesions between liver and abdominal wall.
  • Disseminated gonococcal infection: bacteraemia spreading to organs, notably septic arthritis.
  • Gonococcal ophthalmia neonatorum: purulent conjunctivitis acquired in the birth canal, which can destroy the cornea if untreated.

Diagnosis, treatment, prevention

  • Smear: intracellular gram-negative diplococci inside PMNs in a urethral smear. Usually diagnosed by nucleic acid amplification (genetic probes).
  • Culture on Thayer-Martin medium: a Columbia agar base with haemoglobin and growth supplements plus vancomycin, colistin, nystatin and trimethoprim to suppress other flora, so it is selective for pathogenic Neisseria. Colonies are oxidase positive, do not ferment maltose and have no capsule.
  • Treatment: ceftriaxone, plus testing or empirical treatment for Chlamydia trachomatis (azithromycin, or doxycycline) because co-infection is frequent. Plasmid-mediated β-lactamase gives high-level penicillin resistance.
  • Prevention: condoms (no adult vaccine); for newborns, silver nitrate or erythromycin ointment in the eyes at birth.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • Recurrent Neisseria bacteraemia points to late complement (C5–C9) deficiency.
  • The meningococcal group B capsule is poorly immunogenic (sialic acid) and is not in the quadrivalent vaccine.
  • Waterhouse-Friderichsen is adrenal haemorrhage with shock, not meningitis itself.
  • Close contacts of a meningococcal case need rifampin, not just vaccination.
  • Gonococcal infection gives no lasting immunity because pili vary antigenically.
  • Treat gonorrhoea and chlamydia together; neonatal eye prophylaxis is routine.
