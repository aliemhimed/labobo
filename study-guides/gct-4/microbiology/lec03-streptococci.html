Streptococci and enterococci

Gram-positive cocci in chains: Lancefield grouping and the tests that tell them apart, group A streptococcus and its pyogenic, toxin-mediated and immunologic diseases (pharyngitis, scarlet fever, skin infections, toxic shock, rheumatic fever, glomerulonephritis), group B streptococcus in newborns, Streptococcus pneumoniae, the viridans group and the enterococci.

Labobo topic: Micro Lec 3: Streptococci

In 60 seconds

  • Streptococci: gram-positive cocci in chains or pairs, non-motile, catalase negative, facultative anaerobes; grouped by cell-wall carbohydrate (Lancefield A–O).
  • Group A (S. pyogenes): β-haemolytic, bacitracin sensitive, PYR +; M protein. Disease by pyogenic infection (pharyngitis, impetigo, cellulitis), toxins (scarlet fever, STSS) and immunology (rheumatic fever, PSGN). Treat with penicillin.
  • Group B (S. agalactiae): β-haemolytic, bacitracin resistant, hippurate +, CAMP +; vaginal flora (15–20%); #1 cause of neonatal sepsis and meningitis; intrapartum penicillin/ampicillin.
  • S. pneumoniae: α-haemolytic, lancet-shaped diplococci, optochin sensitive, bile soluble; capsule; commonest cause of typical pneumonia (rusty sputum), adult meningitis, otitis media.
  • Enterococcus (group D, E. faecalis/faecium): PYR +, grows in bile and 6.5% NaCl; UTI, biliary infection, subacute endocarditis; VRE.

Learning objectives

Jawetz, Melnick & Adelberg’s Medical Microbiology, 28th ed., chapter on the streptococci, enterococci and related genera. Syllabus weeks III–IV. By the end you can:

  • classify streptococci by haemolysis and Lancefield group and identify them with bacitracin, PYR, CAMP, hippurate, optochin, bile solubility and bile esculin;
  • explain the three disease mechanisms of S. pyogenes and its post-streptococcal sequelae;
  • describe group B streptococcus, S. pneumoniae, viridans streptococci and enterococci, with treatment and prevention.

Genus Streptococcus

  • Medically important: S. pyogenes (group A; “strep throat”, rheumatic fever), S. agalactiae (group B; neonatal sepsis), S. pneumoniae (pneumonia and meningitis at all ages), and the viridans group (S. mutans, S. sanguinis, S. gallolyticus).
  • Gram-positive round cells in chains or pairs; non-motile; catalase negative; facultatively anaerobic.
  • Lancefield grouping uses antibodies to the specific cell-wall carbohydrate, groups A to O: group A = S. pyogenes, group B = S. agalactiae.
  • Serotyping within species: S. pneumoniae by its polysaccharide capsule; S. pyogenes by its M protein (surface protein that evades immunity).

  • Bacitracin test: bacitracin (a cell-wall inhibitor) disc; a zone of inhibition = sensitive (GAS); growth up to the disc = resistant (GBS).
  • PYR test: colourless L-pyrrolidonyl-β-naphthylamide is split by pyrrolidonyl arylamidase into a red/pink product. Positive: GAS and enterococci; negative: S. pneumoniae, GBS.
  • CAMP test: GBS factor enhances the β-haemolysis of S. aureus, giving an arrowhead zone.

Group A streptococcus

Reservoir: human throat and skin. Transmission: direct contact, respiratory droplets. Diseases arise by three mechanisms:

MechanismWhat happensDiseases

ToxigenicExotoxins act away from the infectionScarlet fever, streptococcal toxic shock
ImmunologicCross-reacting antibodies or immune complexesRheumatic fever, post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis

GAS: pharyngitis, scarlet fever, skin, STSS

Pharyngitis (commonest GAS infection)

  • Sudden fever, red sore throat, white patches or tonsillar abscess, headache, chills, painful swallowing, tender neck nodes.
  • Attachment by lipoteichoic acid on surface pili; a hyaluronic acid capsule in some strains aids adhesion and evasion.
  • Infants: milder, subacute nasopharyngitis with watery discharge, little fever, spreads to middle ear and mastoid. Older children and adults: acute tonsillitis with purulent exudate and high fever.
  • About 20% are asymptomatic. Look-alikes: infectious mononucleosis, diphtheria, gonococcal pharyngitis, adenovirus.

Scarlet fever

  • A rash after pharyngitis, a skin reaction to erythrogenic (pyrogenic) toxin whose gene comes from a lysogenic phage.
  • Can lead to rheumatic fever, PSGN, otitis media, throat abscess, pneumonia, arthritis. Differential: rubella, roseola, Kawasaki disease, drug eruption.
  • Diagnosis: throat swab, blood tests. Treatment: penicillin (narrow-spectrum cephalosporin if allergic), oral for 10 days.

Skin and soft tissue

  • Impetigo (streptococcal pyoderma): superficial skin infection, crusted lesions; tropics and northern summers; clinically identical to, and often mixed with, S. aureus.
  • Erysipelas: upper dermis and superficial lymphatics; red, swollen, painful skin with a raised sharp edge, fever, blisters, swollen glands.
  • Cellulitis: acute spreading infection of skin and subcutaneous tissue.
  • Necrotising fasciitis (“flesh-eating”, streptococcal gangrene): rapidly destroys skin, fat and fascia; on the genitals = Fournier gangrene.

Streptococcal toxic shock syndrome

Toxin-mediated, rapid: myalgia, chills and pain at the infection site → vomiting, diarrhoea → hypotension and shock, bacteraemia, respiratory and multi-organ failure; mortality about 30%. Blood and urine cultures; IV antibiotics, fluids, pressors, sometimes IV immunoglobulin.

Jones criteria for rheumatic fever: 2 major, or 1 major + 2 minor, plus evidence of recent strep infection.

  • Major: polyarthritis, carditis, subcutaneous nodules, Sydenham chorea, erythema marginatum.
  • Minor: fever, raised ESR/CRP, arthralgia, ECG changes (prolonged PR).

In PSGN, the chest X-ray may show fluid overload, renal ultrasound usually shows normal-sized kidneys, and biopsy is kept for uncertain cases; immunosuppression only for severe disease.

Watch out

Slide 17 calls the subcutaneous nodules “Aschoff bodies”. Aschoff bodies are granulomas in the heart (myocardium) in rheumatic carditis; the painless nodules under the skin are simply subcutaneous nodules.

Group B streptococcus (S. agalactiae)

  • β-haemolytic, bacitracin resistant, hippurate +, CAMP +.
  • Lives in the vagina (about 15–20% of women) and GI tract; may colonise the upper airway.
  • Passed to the newborn during birth; risk rises with prolonged labour after membrane rupture.
  • Virulence: capsule, β-haemolysin, CAMP factor.
  • Newborns: #1 cause of neonatal septicaemia and meningitis. Adults: pneumonia, endocarditis, arthritis, cellulitis, osteomyelitis, postpartum endometritis.
  • Treatment: ampicillin + aminoglycoside (or a cephalosporin).
  • Prevention: screen pregnant women; if positive or at risk, intrapartum penicillin or ampicillin (clindamycin or erythromycin if allergic).

Streptococcus pneumoniae

  • α-haemolytic, optochin sensitive, lancet-shaped diplococci, bile soluble. Reservoir: human upper airway; droplets; often carried in the nasopharynx without disease.
  • Predisposing: preceding influenza or measles, COPD, heart failure, alcoholism; asplenia → septicaemia.
  • Virulence: polysaccharide capsule (major), IgA protease, teichoic acid, pneumolysin O (damages respiratory epithelium, inhibits the respiratory burst and classical complement fixation).
  • Pneumonia: sudden shaking chill, high fever, rusty blood-tinged sputum, lobar consolidation; commonest cause of typical pneumonia, especially in older adults.
  • Meningitis: commonest cause in adults; peptidoglycan and teichoic acid inflame the CNS. CSF: many neutrophils, low glucose, high protein.
  • Children: leading cause of otitis media and sinusitis.
  • Treatment: β-lactams for pneumonia; ceftriaxone or cefotaxime ± vancomycin for meningitis; amoxicillin for otitis media (erythromycin if allergic). Prevention: vaccination.

Viridans streptococci

The slide is a picture only; from Jawetz: α-haemolytic (or non-haemolytic) normal flora of the mouth, optochin resistant and bile insoluble (unlike pneumococcus).

  • S. mutans: makes dextran from sucrose and sticks to teeth → dental caries.
  • S. sanguinis and others: enter blood after dental work and settle on damaged valves → subacute bacterial endocarditis.
  • S. gallolyticus (formerly S. bovis): bacteraemia and endocarditis linked to colon cancer.

Enterococci

  • E. faecalis and E. faecium: group D gram-positive cocci in chains, catalase negative, PYR +, varied haemolysis; hydrolyse esculin in 40% bile and grow in 6.5% NaCl (bile esculin agar turns black).
  • Normal flora of colon, urethra and female genital tract; infection is endogenous, or person to person via healthcare workers in hospital (especially E. faecium), spreading vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE).
  • Patients: elderly, underlying disease, long stays, catheters, prior broad-spectrum antibiotics.
  • Diseases: UTI (commonest), biliary and polymicrobial intra-abdominal/pelvic infection, subacute endocarditis after GI or GU procedures on damaged valves.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • Rheumatic fever follows pharyngitis, not skin infection; PSGN can follow either.
  • The scarlet fever toxin gene arrives by lysogenic phage (transduction).
  • #1 cause of neonatal sepsis and meningitis: GBS; prevent with screening and intrapartum antibiotics.
  • Asplenic patients are at risk of overwhelming pneumococcal sepsis.
  • VRE is mostly E. faecium, spread by healthcare workers.
  • S. gallolyticus endocarditis: look for colon cancer.
