Gram-positive rods

The spore-forming rods Clostridium and Bacillus and the non-spore-forming Corynebacterium diphtheriae and Listeria monocytogenes: the toxin each produces, the disease it causes, how the laboratory identifies it, and how patients are treated and protected.

Labobo topic: Micro Lec 4: Gram-Positive Rods

In 60 seconds

  • C. perfringens: square-ended rod; α-toxin (lecithinase) → gas gangrene in injured muscle; identified by the Nagler reaction; penicillin G plus surgical debridement.
  • C. botulinum: toxin stops acetylcholine release → descending flaccid paralysis; foodborne, infant and wound forms; treat with trivalent antitoxin and respiratory support.
  • C. tetani: tetanospasmin blocks release of the inhibitory transmitters glycine and GABA → spastic paralysis (trismus, risus sardonicus, opisthotonos); immune globulin, diazepam; toxoid vaccine, boosters every 10 years.
  • C. difficile: follows antibiotics; toxins A and B damage the actin cytoskeleton → pseudomembranous colitis; stool toxin ELISA or PCR; oral vancomycin or metronidazole.
  • C. diphtheriae: “Chinese letter” rods with metachromatic granules, black colonies on tellurite; phage-encoded toxin shown by the Elek test; give antitoxin immediately. Listeria: tumbling motility, actin-based spread, grows at fridge temperature; danger in pregnancy and newborns.

Learning objectives

Jawetz, Melnick & Adelberg’s Medical Microbiology, 28th ed., chapters on the clostridia, aerobic spore-forming bacilli and non-spore-forming gram-positive rods. Syllabus week V. By the end you can:

  • match each clostridial toxin to its mechanism and disease;

Clostridium perfringens

  • Large, non-motile gram-positive rod with square ends, growing rapidly in anaerobic conditions with haemolytic colonies on blood agar. Spores live in soil; the vegetative form is normal flora of colon and vagina.
  • Infection follows entry into traumatised tissue, especially muscle: war wounds, severe accidents, septic abortion. Delay in surgical management is a key factor.

Toxins and gas gangrene

  • α-toxin, a phospholipase (lecithinase), hydrolyses lecithin and sphingomyelin in membranes, lysing red cells, white cells and muscle → haemolysis and extensive necrosis. The most important virulence factor.
  • θ-toxin: increases capillary permeability, is toxic to cardiac muscle and forms pores like streptolysin O.
  • Some strains make an enterotoxin that disrupts enterocytes and causes fluid loss.
  • Bacterial enzymes generate gas within tissue, the hallmark of the infection. Course: severe wound pain and a feeling of pressure → oedema, tenderness, pallor → discoloured skin and haemorrhagic bullae → shock, intravascular haemolysis, hypotension and acute renal failure.

Nagler reaction

On lecithin-rich egg yolk agar the α-toxin digests lecithin and makes an opaque zone. Half the plate is spread with antitoxin: opacity on the untreated half and none on the antitoxin half confirms α-toxin and so C. perfringens.

Treatment: penicillin G with debridement. Prevention: clean and debride wounds; penicillin prophylaxis; there is no vaccine.

Clostridium botulinum

  • A spore-forming anaerobic rod whose spores are highly resistant and widespread in soil and sediment; they are destroyed only by moist heat at 121°C.
  • Botulinum toxin (antigenic types A–G) is a metalloproteinase that binds presynaptic nerve terminals irreversibly and cleaves the proteins needed for vesicle fusion, so acetylcholine is not released → flaccid paralysis. (Tetanus is the opposite: spastic paralysis.)
  • The toxin is heat labile (destroyed at 100°C) but resists digestion, so it is absorbed from the gut.

Treatment: prompt trivalent antitoxin, which neutralises circulating toxin but cannot reverse paralysis already present; it is horse-derived, and about 15% of recipients develop serum sickness (fever, rash, joint pain). Intensive respiratory support is often needed. Prevention rests on correct commercial food processing and on discarding swollen cans; adequate cooking inactivates the heat-labile toxin.

Clostridium tetani

  • Slim spore-forming anaerobic rod; spores persist for years in soil and resist disinfectants and brief boiling. Terminal spores give a “tennis racket” appearance.
  • Spores enter through a wound, especially with necrotic tissue and poor blood supply (anaerobic conditions); also in people injecting drugs subcutaneously.
  • Tetanospasmin degrades the proteins needed for transmitter release, specifically blocking the inhibitory transmitters glycine and GABA from interneurons. Without inhibition, motor neurons fire continuously → spasms and spastic paralysis.
  • The toxin is heat labile and antigenic, neutralised by antitoxin and destroyed by intestinal proteases (so tetanus is not acquired by eating). Treated with formaldehyde it becomes a toxoid: antigenic but non-toxic.

Clinical features and management

  • Incubation four days to several weeks. Trismus (lockjaw), risus sardonicus, hyperreflexia and rigidity; opisthotonos (arching of the back); death usually from respiratory muscle spasm.
  • Diagnosis is clinical: there is no reliable microbiological or serological test, and the organism is rarely isolated.
  • Treatment: tetanus immune globulin to neutralise unbound toxin; antibiotics have a secondary role; secure airway and respiratory support; diazepam for rigidity and spasms.
  • Prevention: tetanus toxoid in the childhood DTaP schedule with boosters every 10 years; wound cleaning and debridement; for incompletely immunised patients a booster, and for heavily contaminated wounds immune globulin plus toxoid (with penicillin).

Clostridium difficile

  • Spore-forming anaerobic rod carried without symptoms by 2–15% of people and up to 30% of inpatients; spread faecal–oral in hospitals, often on staff hands.
  • Disease follows disruption of normal flora by antibiotics, which lets it overgrow and make toxins.
  • Toxin A (TcdA) and toxin B (TcdB) are glucosyltransferases that inactivate small GTP-binding proteins regulating the actin cytoskeleton; binary toxin (CDT) ADP-ribosylates actin. Toxin B especially depolymerises actin → cell rounding, apoptosis, colonic inflammation.
  • Antibiotic-associated diarrhoea and pseudomembranous colitis: yellow-white plaques on the colonic mucosa. Diarrhoea is watery, usually not bloody, with fever, abdominal pain and neutrophils in stool.
  • Diagnosis: stool toxin A/B ELISA, cytotoxicity assay on cultured cells, or PCR for toxin genes; sigmoidoscopy shows pseudomembranes.
  • Treatment: stop the offending antibiotic; oral metronidazole or oral vancomycin; fluids and electrolytes; faecal microbiota transplantation for recurrence; colectomy for toxic megacolon.

Bacillus anthracis and B. cereus

The slide introduces B. anthracis as an organism of public health concern whose spores persist in nature; the clinical detail below is from Jawetz.

  • B. anthracis: large gram-positive rod in chains, non-motile, spore-forming, with a protein (poly-D-glutamate) capsule. Toxin components: protective antigen, oedema factor (an adenylate cyclase) and lethal factor.
  • Human disease is usually cutaneous after contact with infected animals or their products: a painless ulcer with a black eschar and marked oedema. Inhalational and gastrointestinal forms are rare and severe. Treatment: ciprofloxacin or doxycycline; a vaccine exists for people at occupational risk.
  • B. cereus: food poisoning in two patterns, an emetic form 1–6 h after reheated rice (preformed heat-stable toxin) and a diarrhoeal form 8–16 h later; self-limiting, treated supportively.

Corynebacterium diphtheriae

  • Gram-positive pleomorphic, curved rod arranged in “Chinese letters” or palisades from snapping division, with metachromatic (volutin) granules on special stains.
  • Culture: Loeffler’s serum medium (rapid growth, prominent granules) and Tinsdale/tellurite agar, where it reduces tellurite and forms black colonies; also blood agar.
  • Only strains carrying a bacteriophage with the toxin gene cause disease. Toxigenicity is confirmed by the Elek test, an immunodiffusion in which toxin and antitoxin form precipitin lines; PCR can detect the toxin gene.
  • Diagnosis is initially clinical; take Dacron swabs from nose, throat or lesions before antibiotics.
  • Treatment: antitoxin immediately, because toxin binds cells rapidly and irreversibly and cannot then be neutralised; the organism is susceptible to penicillins, cephalosporins, erythromycin and tetracycline.
  • Transmission: humans are the only natural host; airborne droplets from toxigenic and non-toxigenic carriers; skin lesions can also be infected.
  • Prevention: immunisation with diphtheria toxoid, 3–4 doses from the first year with boosters every 10 years. Antibodies act against the toxin only, so immunised people can still be infected but have mild disease; serious disease and death occur in the unimmunised.

Listeria monocytogenes

  • Gram-positive rod from soil; a facultative intracellular organism with tumbling motility. It polymerises host actin into “actin rockets” and moves cell to cell, avoiding extracellular defences.
  • Epidemiology: widespread in soil, groundwater, decaying vegetation and the intestines of food animals. Outbreaks from soft cheese and dairy contaminated after pasteurisation; the 1985 California outbreak had 86 cases and 29 deaths, mostly mother–infant pairs. It grows at refrigerator temperature, so small numbers reach an infectious dose during storage.
  • Transmitted transplacentally to the fetus and to newborns in the birth canal, like group B streptococcus.
  • Diagnosis: culture of blood, CSF or focal lesions; gram-positive rods resembling diphtheroids with small grey colonies and a narrow zone of β-haemolysis; confirmed by motility (corynebacteria are non-motile) and sugar fermentation.
  • Treatment: invasive disease (meningitis, sepsis) with trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, or ampicillin with gentamicin (or with TMP-SMX); resistance is rare. Listeria gastroenteritis usually needs no treatment.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • Botulism paralysis is descending and symmetric, starting with cranial nerves.
  • Antitoxin neutralises only unbound toxin, in both botulism and diphtheria, so it must be given early.
  • Tetanus is diagnosed clinically, not by culture.
  • Only phage-infected C. diphtheriae strains cause disease; the Elek test proves toxigenicity.
  • Listeria is a reason pregnant women avoid soft cheeses; it multiplies in the fridge.
  • Stopping the offending antibiotic is part of treating C. difficile.

Key terms
