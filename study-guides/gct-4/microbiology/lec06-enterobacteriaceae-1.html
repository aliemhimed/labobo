Enterobacteriaceae I: enterics part 1

The gram-negative rods of the gut: what unites the family, their O, H and K antigens, and the lactose test that splits them. Then Escherichia coli and its five diarrhoeal pathotypes, Shigella, Salmonella (enterocolitis, typhoid fever, bacteraemia), and the extra-intestinal pathogens Klebsiella and Proteus.

Labobo topic: Micro Lec 6: Enterobacteriaceae I

In 60 seconds

  • Enterobacteriaceae: gram-negative rods, facultative anaerobes, ferment glucose, oxidase negative, reduce nitrate to nitrite.
  • On MacConkey: lactose fermenters pink (E. coli, Klebsiella, Enterobacter), non-fermenters colourless (Shigella, Salmonella, Proteus). Salmonella makes H₂S, Shigella does not.
  • E. coli: commonest cause of UTI and gram-negative sepsis, a leading cause of neonatal meningitis (K1 capsule), and of travellers’ diarrhoea. Pathotypes: ETEC (LT/ST, watery), EHEC/STEC O157:H7 (Shiga toxin, bloody, HUS), EPEC (attaching and effacing), EIEC, EAEC.
  • Shigella: non-motile, non-lactose, no H₂S; Type III secretion system; low infectious dose; dysentery. Salmonella: motile, H₂S; enterocolitis, typhoid fever, bacteraemia with focal lesions.
  • Klebsiella: non-motile, big capsule, hospital pneumonia and UTI, carbapenemase strains. Proteus: swarming, urease → alkaline urine and struvite stones.

Learning objectives

Jawetz, Melnick & Adelberg’s Medical Microbiology, 28th ed., chapter on enteric gram-negative rods. Syllabus week VI. By the end you can:

  • list the shared properties and antigens of the family and interpret MacConkey and H₂S results;
  • distinguish the five diarrhoeagenic E. coli pathotypes and explain HUS;
  • compare Shigella and Salmonella and describe typhoid fever;
  • describe Klebsiella and Proteus infections and the role of urease.

The family and where it lives

GroupGenera
Pathogens inside and outside the gutEscherichia, Salmonella
Pathogens mainly inside the gutShigella, Vibrio, Campylobacter, Helicobacter
Pathogens outside the gutKlebsiella, Proteus
  • Shared properties: facultative anaerobes; ferment glucose (other sugars vary); lack cytochrome oxidase, so oxidase negative; reduce nitrates to nitrites.
  • Habitat: lower gastrointestinal tract of humans and animals; many also survive in water and the environment. In humans they are a major part of the facultative flora of the colon, also the female genital tract and transiently the skin. Commonest is E. coli, then Klebsiella, Proteus and Enterobacter.
  • Salmonella and Shigella are not normal flora, though people may carry them. Shigella and S. Typhi are strictly human, with no animal reservoir.
  • Morphology: short rods with rounded ends, 0.5–1.5 µm by 2–4 µm; many have peritrichous flagella; Proteus swarms across agar; some have a capsule.

Antigens and laboratory diagnosis

  • All are gram-negative, so all carry endotoxin (LPS); several also make exotoxins.
  • O (somatic) antigen: the outer polysaccharide of LPS, the basis of serotyping; about 1500 types in Salmonella and 150 in E. coli.
  • H antigen: flagellar protein, present only in motile organisms (Escherichia, Salmonella; not Shigella or Klebsiella).
  • K (capsular) antigen: polysaccharide layer, prominent in heavily encapsulated organisms such as Klebsiella.
  • Media: blood agar, and the selective and differential MacConkey or eosin methylene blue agar. Lactose fermenters give coloured (pink) colonies; non-fermenters are colourless.

Escherichia coli

  • Normal intestinal flora; most strains are harmless and help keep the gut healthy, but some are pathogenic. Ferments lactose, which separates it from Shigella and Salmonella.
  • Clinically it is the commonest cause of urinary tract infection and of gram-negative rod sepsis, one of the two leading causes of neonatal meningitis (with group B streptococcus; about 80% of meningitis strains carry the K1 capsule), and the usual agent of travellers’ diarrhoea. Newborns are susceptible because they lack IgM.
  • Serotyping uses O, H and K antigens: more than 150 O, 50 H and 90 K types give over 1000 antigenic types, written for example O111:K76:H7.
  • Virulence: adhesion to mucosa by pili (colonisation factors), invasion of epithelium, and exotoxins: pore-forming cytotoxins, cytotoxic necrotising factor, heat-labile toxin (LT), heat-stable toxin (ST) and Shiga-like toxin.

Urinary tract infection

  • E. coli causes about 90% of first infections in young women; uropathogenic strains have pili whose adhesins bind receptors on urinary epithelium.
  • Acute uncomplicated cystitis is common in women because the anus is close to the vaginal introitus and the urethra is short. Most cases come from faecal flora, E. coli in 75–90%; risk is highest after recent sexual activity or a previous UTI.
  • Symptoms: dysuria, frequency and urgency, haematuria, suprapubic pain. History alone usually suffices; examination and urine culture are needed if pregnancy is possible, if pyelonephritis is likely (fever, chills, flank pain), or if vaginal infection is suspected (itch, discharge).
  • Empirical treatment: trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (or nitrofurantoin).

Haemolytic uraemic syndrome

  1. EHEC releases Shiga toxin.
  2. The toxin damages endothelial cells of small vessels.
  3. Platelets activate and form microthrombi, narrowing the vessels.
  4. Red cells are torn as they pass through → schistocytes.
  5. The kidney suffers most → acute renal failure.

Classic triad: haemolytic anaemia, thrombocytopenia, acute kidney injury.

Shigella

  • Gram-negative, facultative anaerobic, non-spore-forming, non-motile rods; four species: S. dysenteriae, S. flexneri, S. boydii, S. sonnei.
  • Being non-motile they have no H antigen; serovars are defined by O antigens. Closely related to E. coli; most produce no gas from glucose and do not ferment lactose.
  • Key lab distinction: no lactose, no H₂S (E. coli ferments lactose; Salmonella does not ferment lactose but makes H₂S).
  • Virulence: an enterotoxin causing early watery diarrhoea, and a Type III secretion system, a molecular syringe that injects effector proteins into host cells to invade epithelium, escape immune killing, kill macrophages and trigger inflammation and tissue damage. S. dysenteriae also makes Shiga toxin.
  • Treatment: many cases are self-limited. Effective drugs include ciprofloxacin, ampicillin, doxycycline, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole; azithromycin is often used in children. Plasmid-borne multidrug resistance is widespread.

Salmonella

  • Gram-negative rod that does not ferment lactose but produces H₂S. Nearly all human pathogens belong to Salmonella enterica, with more than 2500 serotypes defined by O and H antigens.

Typhoid fever

  • A life-threatening bloodstream infection, still common in developing countries (about 21.5 million cases a year), caused by S. Typhi from food or water contaminated by human carriers who shed the organism in stool (rarely urine) and are often asymptomatic.
  • Sources: water (faecal contamination causes explosive epidemics), milk and dairy, shellfish from contaminated water, dried or frozen eggs, meat products, animal dyes, household pets such as turtles.
  • Pathogenesis: the bacteria invade the small intestine and briefly enter the blood, are carried by white cells to liver, spleen and bone marrow, multiply there, then re-enter the blood to cause systemic disease.
  • Clinically: prolonged high fever (39–40°C), headache, abdominal pain, diarrhoea, weakness, myalgia and relative bradycardia.
  • Treatment of invasive Salmonella infection: ampicillin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole or a third-generation cephalosporin, guided by susceptibility testing because plasmid-borne multidrug resistance is a problem.
  • Prevention: sanitation to keep animals and rodents from contaminating food and water, thorough cooking of poultry, meat and eggs, and keeping carriers out of food handling.

Klebsiella

  • Non-motile gram-negative rods with a prominent polysaccharide capsule; surface antigens are the O (LPS) and K (capsular) antigens, both contributing to virulence. Human pathogens: K. pneumoniae, K. oxytoca, K. granulomatis.
  • Among the top 5 causes of hospital-acquired infection. Resistant to many penicillins and cephalosporins; ST258 is a multidrug-resistant carbapenemase producer resistant to all β-lactams, usually through plasmids carrying several resistance genes.
  • Colonises skin, pharynx and gut, and also wounds and urine. Syndromes: UTI, pneumonia, bacteraemia, thrombophlebitis, cholecystitis, diarrhoea, wound infection, osteomyelitis, meningitis, upper respiratory infection.

Proteus

  • Gram-negative swarming bacilli, part of normal intestinal flora. Pathogens: P. mirabilis, P. vulgaris, P. penneri.
  • Urease is the key to its urinary disease: it hydrolyses urea to ammonia, raising the pH to make alkaline urine, which favours struvite stones (magnesium ammonium phosphate).
  • Stones obstruct flow, damage the urothelium and shelter bacteria, causing recurrent infection; alkaline urine also helps the organism grow and worsens renal damage, so treatment includes keeping the urine acidic.
  • Susceptibility varies: P. mirabilis is often inhibited by penicillins; the others respond best to aminoglycosides and cephalosporins.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • Antibiotics are avoided in EHEC because killing the bacteria releases more toxin and raises the risk of HUS.
  • Shiga and Shiga-like toxins inactivate the 60S ribosomal subunit.
  • Shigella needs a very low infectious dose; ETEC needs a high one.
  • Typhoid fever has relative bradycardia despite high fever.
  • A urinary stone with alkaline urine suggests a urease producer (Proteus).
  • Only motile organisms have an H antigen, so Shigella and Klebsiella have none.

Key terms

Enterobacteriaceae: enteric gram-negative rod family · Oxidase negative: lacks cytochrome oxidase · MacConkey agar: selective and differential lactose medium · O antigen: LPS polysaccharide · H antigen: flagellar antigen · K antigen: capsular antigen · Uropathogenic E. coli: strains with urinary adhesins · ETEC: travellers’ diarrhoea strains · LT / ST: heat-labile and heat-stable toxins · EHEC / STEC: Shiga toxin-producing E. coli · O157:H7: classic EHEC serotype · HUS: haemolytic uraemic syndrome · Schistocyte: fragmented red cell · Attaching and effacing lesion: EPEC brush-border damage · Type III secretion system: injection apparatus · Enteric fever: typhoid · Relative bradycardia: slow pulse with high fever · Carbapenemase: enzyme destroying carbapenems · Urease: splits urea to ammonia · Struvite: magnesium ammonium phosphate stone.
