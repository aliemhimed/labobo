Labobo topic: Micro Lec 7: Enterobacteriaceae II and Hospital Gram-Negatives

In 60 seconds

  • V. cholerae: curved, oxidase positive, single polar flagellum; only serogroups O1 and O139; high infectious dose (10⁸); cholera toxin → rice-water stool, dehydration, death in hours; treat with oral rehydration; TCBS agar.
  • C. jejuni: microaerophilic, grows at 42°C, “gull wing” rods; from poultry and animal faeces; bloody diarrhoea; linked to Guillain-Barré syndrome by molecular mimicry; erythromycin or ciprofloxacin.
  • H. pylori: spiral, motile, strongly urease positive (Campylobacter is urease negative); gastritis, peptic ulcer (90% of duodenal ulcers), MALT lymphoma, gastric adenocarcinoma; triple therapy.
  • Y. enterocolitica: invasive plus ST-like toxin; grows in the fridge; mesenteric adenitis mimicking appendicitis. B. fragilis: anaerobe, capsule, no lipid A; abscesses after bowel breach; metronidazole and drainage.
  • P. aeruginosa: aerobic, oxidase positive, pyocyanin blue-green, grape smell, grows at 42°C; burns, cystic fibrosis biofilm, contact-lens keratitis, malignant otitis externa; exotoxin A blocks protein synthesis like diphtheria toxin.

Vibrio cholerae

  • Facultative anaerobic, gram-negative, non-spore-forming curved rod; oxidase positive, reduces nitrate, motile by a single polar flagellum.
  • Identified by biochemistry, LPS O antigen and production of cholera toxin. Of more than 150 O serotypes, only O1 and O139 cause cholera.
  • Transmission: faecal contamination of water and food, mainly from human sources; animal reservoirs are marine shellfish such as shrimp and oysters.
  • Pathogenesis: colonisation of the small intestine (strong motility, easy attachment) then secretion of cholera toxin. A relatively high infectious dose (10⁸) is needed. The toxin is taken into epithelial cells and alters electrolyte channels, so fluid rich in chloride, bicarbonate, sodium and potassium pours into the lumen.

Cholera: clinical picture and treatment

  • Symptoms: rice-water stool, leg cramps, mild fever, vomiting, sunken eyes and cheeks, dry mucous membranes, reduced urine output.
  • Everything follows from fluid and electrolyte loss: extreme dehydration, hypotension, cardiac and renal failure; acidosis from bicarbonate loss and hypokalaemia from potassium loss. Untreated mortality about 40%; roughly 1 in 10 infected people develop severe disease and can die within hours.
  • Diagnosis: clinical suspicion in the right setting plus isolation from stool. It grows on blood and MacConkey agar, better on the selective thiosulfate-citrate-bile salts-sucrose (TCBS) agar. Colonies on MacConkey are colourless (lactose is fermented slowly), and it is oxidase positive, which separates it from Enterobacteriaceae.
  • Treatment: most patients recover with prompt oral rehydration solution; severe dehydration needs rapid intravenous fluids. Mass antibiotic administration is not recommended: it does not stop spread and drives resistance.
  • Prevention: clean water and food supply above all. Three WHO-prequalified oral vaccines (Dukoral, Shanchol, Euvichol), each needing 2 doses, for people living in or travelling to areas with active transmission. The older killed vaccine is only about 50% effective for 3–6 months and does not interrupt transmission; neither killed nor live vaccine is recommended routinely for travellers. Tetracycline helps close contacts but cannot stop an epidemic; detecting carriers early limits outbreaks.

Campylobacter jejuni

  • Causes diarrhoeal and systemic disease and is among the most widespread infections in the world; C. jejuni is the medically important species and the leading cause of bacterial foodborne gastroenteritis.
  • Microaerophilic: grows best in 5% oxygen. It grows at 36–37°C, but incubation at 42°C suppresses other faecal flora and simplifies isolation; Skirrow’s medium adds vancomycin, polymyxin B and trimethoprim.
  • Transmission: faecal–oral from domestic animals (cattle, chickens, dogs) through contaminated food and water. Infectious dose only 500–800 organisms.
  • Pathogenesis: LPS with endotoxic activity, cytopathic extracellular toxins and enterotoxins. It adheres to host epithelial proteins, invades, translocates and multiplies in the mucosa; toxins necrose the villi, tight junctions open, inflammation and electrolyte release follow, giving strong bloody diarrhoea.
  • Clinical: after 2–5 days, enterocolitis starting as watery, foul-smelling diarrhoea, then bloody stools with fever and severe abdominal pain.
  • Diagnosis: diarrhoeal stool (blood cultures occasionally in the elderly or immunocompromised); Gram stain shows “gull wing” curved rods; culture on selective blood agar. Treatment: erythromycin or ciprofloxacin.

Guillain-Barré syndrome

Gastrointestinal infection with C. jejuni is associated with Guillain-Barré syndrome, the commonest cause of acute neuromuscular paralysis. It is autoimmune: antibodies raised against C. jejuni cross-react with neuronal antigens (molecular mimicry), damaging peripheral nerves.

Helicobacter pylori

  • A spiral gram-negative rod resembling campylobacters but differing biochemically: strongly urease positive (campylobacters are urease negative), also oxidase and catalase positive and motile.
  • Grows in 3–6 days at 37°C in a microaerophilic atmosphere on Skirrow’s medium, chocolate agar or other selective media.
  • Transmission is still unclear; person to person and within families seems the main route, judging from intrafamilial clustering.
  • Pathogenesis: it lives deep in the mucous layer near the epithelium, where the pH is physiological, and secretes a protease that alters gastric mucus so acid diffuses less well. Limited invasion of the epithelial surface, toxins, LPS and the ammonia from urease damage mucosal cells.
  • Disease: long-term colonisation raises the risk of chronic gastritis, peptic ulcer disease, MALT lymphoma and gastric adenocarcinoma. Infection persists for years or a lifetime; about 90% of duodenal ulcer patients and 50–80% of gastric ulcer patients are infected.
  • Diagnosis: gastric biopsy for histology or culture; Gram stain of biopsy; culture as for campylobacters but urease positive; serum antibodies; rapid urease tests for presumptive identification.
  • Treatment: antibiotics to eradicate the organism plus a drug to reduce gastric acid. Triple therapy with metronidazole and bismuth plus either amoxicillin or tetracycline for 14 days eradicates it in 70–95%.

Yersinia enterocolitica

  • Motile gram-negative rod causing acute gastroenteritis. Animals are the major source; faecal–oral spread, often from unpasteurised milk or contaminated water.
  • Two mechanisms: it is invasive, attaching to the intestinal wall and spreading to regional lymph nodes and sometimes the blood; and it makes an enterotoxin like the heat-stable toxin of E. coli.
  • Fever, diarrhoea and abdominal pain; mesenteric lymph node involvement can mimic appendicitis. It grows at refrigerator temperature, so refrigerated foods can transmit it.
  • Diagnosis by stool or blood culture. Uncomplicated gastroenteritis is self-limited and antibiotics do not change its course; sepsis is treated.

Bacteroidaceae

  • About 99% of normal intestinal flora: mainly obligate anaerobic gram-negative rods, also in mouth and vagina. Harmless in place, dangerous when they escape.
  • Bacteroides fragilis: has no lipid A, so no typical endotoxin activity, but has a capsule that promotes abscess formation. Low virulence until the intestinal barrier is broken: abdominal surgery, appendicitis, perforation, bowel ischaemia. Prophylaxis before abdominal surgery covers anaerobes with clindamycin, metronidazole or chloramphenicol; an established abscess needs surgical drainage as well as antibiotics.
  • Bacteroides melaninogenicus (mouth, vagina, intestine): necrotising anaerobic pneumonia after aspiration of oral secretions, for example during seizures or heavy alcohol intoxication; also a cause of periodontal disease.

Pseudomonas aeruginosa

  • Aerobic, motile gram-negative rod, slimmer and paler than Enterobacteriaceae; minimal growth requirements; oxidative metabolism with high cytochrome oxidase, so oxidase positive.
  • Pigments: green fluorescent pyoverdin and blue pyocyanin, giving colonies and infected dressings a blue-green colour; fluorescein fluoresces under ultraviolet light.
  • Habitat: the environment, in water, soil and vegetation. A leading cause of opportunistic infection of the eye (contact lenses), wounds, urinary tract and burns, and it colonises the airways of people with cystic fibrosis by forming a biofilm, becoming chronic. Risk is highest in the immunocompromised.
  • Pathogenesis: exotoxin A, an A–B toxin blocking protein synthesis by the same mechanism as diphtheria toxin; a type III secretion system injecting exoenzyme S; elastase secreted outside. Elastin is abundant in the lung and blood vessel walls, which it attacks, and haemorrhagic destruction of vessel walls is the histological hallmark.
  • Manifestations: pneumonia, osteomyelitis, burn-wound infection, UTI and pyelonephritis, endocarditis, malignant external otitis, corneal infection in contact lens wearers.
  • Identification: oxidase-positive colonies, pyocyanin and growth at 42°C.
  • Treatment is complicated by resistance: penicillin and aminoglycoside resistance is common; ceftazidime and cefepime are often active. Aerosolised tobramycin improves lung function and reduces hospitalisation in cystic fibrosis.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • The mainstay of cholera treatment is rehydration, not antibiotics.
  • Cholera needs a high infectious dose; Campylobacter and Shigella need very low ones.
  • C. jejuni is the classic antecedent infection of Guillain-Barré syndrome.
  • H. pylori eradication always combines antibiotics with acid suppression.
  • B. fragilis has a capsule but no lipid A; abscesses need drainage.
  • Blue-green pus with a sweet smell in a burn or a contact-lens ulcer: think Pseudomonas.
