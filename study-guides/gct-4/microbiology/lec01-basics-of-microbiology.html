Basics of microbiology

How bacteria are classified and built: taxonomy, the Gram stain, the gram-positive and gram-negative cell wall, peptidoglycan, LPS, capsules, pili and flagella, virulence factors, endotoxin and exotoxins, and how bacteria use oxygen, carbon and energy.

Labobo topic: Micro Lec 1: Basics of Microbiology

In 60 seconds

  • Bacteria are prokaryotes: no nucleus, no membrane-bound organelles. The cell wall protects against osmotic lysis and is the main target of immunity and antibiotics.
  • Gram-positive (purple): thick peptidoglycan, lipoteichoic acid. Gram-negative (pink): thin peptidoglycan, periplasm, outer membrane with LPS.
  • Peptidoglycan = NAG + NAM chains; peptides attach to NAM only and cross-link the sheets. Penicillins and cephalosporins block its synthesis.
  • Virulence: capsule (anti-phagocytic; Quellung test), pili (adhesion, conjugation), protein A (binds IgG Fc), IgA protease, M protein (binds factor H).
  • Endotoxin = lipid A of gram-negative LPS, released on death, heat stable, weakly immunogenic, causes fever and shock via TNF and IL-1. Exotoxins are secreted proteins.

Learning objectives

Jawetz, Melnick & Adelberg’s Medical Microbiology, 28th ed., section I (cell structure, classification, metabolism) and the chapter on pathogenesis (toxins). Syllabus week I. By the end you can:

  • describe bacterial taxonomy and compare prokaryotes with eukaryotes;
  • explain the Gram stain from the structure of the two cell walls;
  • list the surface structures and virulence factors and what each does;
  • compare endotoxin with exotoxins and classify bacteria by oxygen, carbon and energy use.

Taxonomy and prokaryotes

Bacterial taxonomy is the science of classification (grouping), nomenclature (naming) and identification (recognising species). Hierarchy, broad to specific: domain → phylum → class → order → family → genus → species.

ProkaryotesEukaryotes
NucleusNone; DNA free in cytoplasmTrue nucleus
OrganellesNo membrane-bound organelles (no mitochondria)Membrane-bound organelles
ExamplesBacteria (unicellular)Plants, animals, protozoa, fungi

The cell wall protects bacteria, supports them, and prevents osmotic swelling and rupture. It is a major target for the immune system and antibiotics, and the basis of classification.

The Gram stain and cell walls

The Gram stain divides bacteria by cell wall: gram-positive retain the stain (purple); gram-negative lose it and take the counterstain (pink/red).

Gram-positiveGram-negative
ColourPurplePink/red
PeptidoglycanThick, many layersThin
Special moleculesLipoteichoic acid (adhesion)Periplasm (enzymes); outer membrane with LPS
Cell membranePresentPresent
EndotoxinNoYes (lipid A)

Shapes: cocci (spheres; in chains, pairs or clusters), bacilli (rods), spirochaetes (spirals). Gram reaction plus shape is the first step in identification.

Peptidoglycan

  • A strong polymer in all bacterial walls: repeating sheets of sugars and peptides, cross-linked into multiple layers.
  • Sugars: N-acetylglucosamine (NAG) and N-acetylmuramic acid (NAM), alternating.
  • Peptides (3–5 amino acids) are attached to NAM, never NAG (high-yield); they link sugar chains, and peptide bridges cross-link the chains.
  • Humans have no peptidoglycan, so drugs that block it kill bacteria selectively: penicillins and cephalosporins inhibit peptidoglycan synthesis.

Cell membrane and LPS

  • The cell membrane (phospholipid bilayer, hydrophilic outside, hydrophobic inside) lies just inside the wall in both groups.
  • Bacteria have no mitochondria, so electron transport and oxidative phosphorylation happen in the cell membrane, which also holds enzymes and carrier proteins for transport.
  • Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) forms the outer leaflet of the gram-negative outer membrane: lipid A (toxic), a core polysaccharide and the strain-variable O antigen.

Capsule, pili, flagella

Capsule

  • A sticky gelatinous layer outside the wall, mostly water with polysaccharide. It helps attachment and blocks phagocytosis: a major virulence factor.
  • Exception: Bacillus anthracis has a protein (poly-D-glutamate) capsule.
  • Quellung reaction (largely historical): type-specific antiserum makes the capsule swell and become visible. Positive in S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae, N. meningitidis, E. coli, Salmonella, Klebsiella, group B streptococcus.
Watch out

Slide 11 calls the anthrax capsule “a protein called D-glutamate”. D-glutamate is an amino acid; the capsule is a polypeptide of D-glutamic acid (poly-D-glutamate). The point to remember is the same: it is protein, not polysaccharide.

Pili (fimbriae) and flagella

  • Pili/fimbriae: hair-like protein appendages for adhesion and colonisation. E. coli pili attach to the urinary tract (UTI, pyelonephritis); N. gonorrhoeae pili show antigenic variation to evade immunity.
  • Sex pili attach to another bacterium to transfer DNA in conjugation.
  • Flagellum: long whip-like protein for motility through liquids.

Endospores and biofilm

Named in the syllabus for week I but not on the slides; from Jawetz:

  • Endospores: dormant, highly resistant forms made by some gram-positive rods (Bacillus, Clostridium) when nutrients run out. A thick keratin-like coat and dipicolinic acid with calcium make them resistant to heat, drying and many disinfectants; they germinate when conditions improve. Only sterilisation (for example autoclaving) reliably kills them.
  • Biofilm: a community of bacteria embedded in a self-made polysaccharide matrix on a surface (catheters, prosthetic valves, teeth). It protects bacteria from antibiotics and immune cells and makes device infections hard to clear.

Lipooligosaccharide (LOS) is like LPS but lacks the O antigen; found in nonenteric gram-negatives, chiefly N. meningitidis. Endotoxin diseases: meningococcaemia, gram-negative sepsis.

Oxygen, carbon and energy

GroupOxygenExample
Obligate aerobesNeed O₂; aerobic respiration; have catalase and superoxide dismutaseMycobacterium tuberculosis
Obligate anaerobesO₂ is toxic; lack detoxifying enzymes; fermentation or anaerobic respirationClostridium
Facultative anaerobesWith or without O₂; prefer aerobic (more ATP), switch to fermentationE. coli
MicroaerophilesNeed low O₂; high O₂ harms themHelicobacter pylori
  • Carbon source: autotrophs fix CO₂ (Calvin cycle); heterotrophs use organic compounds such as glucose (most bacteria, all animals).
  • Energy source: phototrophs use light; chemotrophs oxidise chemicals. Medically important bacteria are chemoheterotrophs.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • Both gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria have a cell membrane; only gram-negatives have an outer membrane.
  • Endotoxin is heat stable and weakly immunogenic, so there is no endotoxin vaccine.
  • The toxic part of LPS is lipid A, not the O antigen.
  • B. anthracis is the classic protein capsule.
  • Oxidative phosphorylation in bacteria happens in the cell membrane.

Key terms

Taxonomy: classification, nomenclature, identification · Prokaryote: cell without nucleus · Gram stain: purple (positive) vs pink (negative) · Peptidoglycan: NAG–NAM cell wall polymer · Lipoteichoic acid: gram-positive adhesion molecule · Periplasm: space between gram-negative membranes · LPS: lipopolysaccharide · Lipid A: toxic core of endotoxin · O antigen: variable LPS sugars · Capsule: anti-phagocytic outer layer · Quellung reaction: capsule swelling with antiserum · Pili: adhesion appendages · Sex pilus: conjugation bridge · Endospore: resistant dormant form · Biofilm: matrix-encased bacterial community · Protein A: IgG Fc binder · IgA protease: cleaves mucosal IgA · Facultative anaerobe: grows with or without O₂ · Heterotroph: uses organic carbon.
