Bacterial genetics and staphylococci

How bacteria swap genes (transformation, transduction, conjugation, transposons), the bench tests that sort gram-positive cocci (catalase, coagulase, haemolysis), and Staphylococcus aureus: its toxins, the diseases they cause (abscess, pneumonia, osteomyelitis, food poisoning, toxic shock, scalded skin) and how it is diagnosed and prevented.

Labobo topic: Micro Lec 2: Bacterial Genetics and Staphylococci

In 60 seconds

  • Gene transfer: transformation (naked DNA, S. pneumoniae), transduction by phage (generalised = any gene; specialised = genes next to the prophage site, λ phage), conjugation (sex pilus, F factor, plasmid), transposons (jumping DNA).
  • Catalase: staphylococci +, streptococci −. Coagulase: only S. aureus +. Haemolysis on blood agar: β complete, α green, γ none.
  • Staphylococci: gram-positive cocci in clusters. S. aureus: golden β-haemolytic colonies, coagulase +, ferments mannitol; nose carriage in ~25–30%.
  • Toxins: protein A (binds IgG Fc), heat-stable enterotoxins (vomiting 2–6 h), TSST-1 superantigen (toxic shock), exfoliatins (desmoglein-1, scalded skin), α-toxin and PVL (pores).
  • Diseases: abscess, wound infection, necrotising pneumonia, osteomyelitis (commonest cause). Prevention: hand washing; mupirocin for nasal carriers.

Learning objectives

Jawetz, Melnick & Adelberg’s Medical Microbiology, 28th ed., chapters on microbial genetics and the staphylococci. Syllabus weeks III–IV. By the end you can:

  • compare the four ways bacteria acquire new genes;
  • use catalase, coagulase, haemolysis and mannitol to identify staphylococci;
  • link each S. aureus toxin to its disease and describe diagnosis and prevention.

Bacterial genetics

Bacteria store their genes on a single circular chromosome in the nucleoid (no nucleus), often with small extra DNA circles, plasmids. They pass genes to offspring and also exchange genes with other bacteria, so they quickly gain traits such as antibiotic resistance or more virulence.

Catalase, coagulase, haemolysis

Catalase test

Catalase breaks H₂O₂ → water + O₂. A colony mixed with a drop of 3% H₂O₂ on a slide: rapid bubbling = positive (Staphylococcus, Bacillus, Pseudomonas); no bubbles = negative (Streptococcus, Enterococcus).

Coagulase test

Done on gram-positive, catalase-positive cocci. Coagulase binds prothrombin; together they polymerise fibrin and clot plasma. Only S. aureus is positive. Fibrin around the organism may protect it from phagocytes; coagulase production is taken as a sign of invasive potential.

Haemolysis on blood agar (5% sheep blood)

TypeAppearanceExamples
β (complete)Clear zoneS. pyogenes, S. aureus, Listeria
α (partial)Green/brown zone (haemoglobin → methaemoglobin)S. pneumoniae, viridans streptococci
γ (none)No changeEnterococcus faecalis, S. epidermidis

S. aureus haemolysins: α-toxin inserts into membranes to form pores; β-toxin (a sphingomyelinase) damages many cells including red cells; δ-toxin disrupts membranes, perhaps in diarrhoea; γ-haemolysin is a leukocidin.

Genus Staphylococcus

  • Gram-positive cocci in clusters, catalase positive. Medical species: S. aureus, S. epidermidis, S. saprophyticus.
  • Grow readily aerobically or microaerophilically, fastest at 37°C, best pigment at room temperature (20–25°C). S. aureus: grey to golden yellow colonies; S. epidermidis: grey to white. No pigment anaerobically or in broth.
  • S. aureus: small yellow β-haemolytic colonies, coagulase positive (all others negative), ferments mannitol on mannitol salt agar.
  • Reservoir: humans. S. epidermidis is normal skin, respiratory and GI flora. S. aureus colonises the anterior nose of about 25–30% (chronic carriers get more skin infections) and the skin of hospital staff and patients; found on clothing, bedding and fomites.

Diseases of S. aureus

  • Localised skin infection: pimple, folliculitis, abscess (also mastitis, impetigo). Intense painful inflammation with central pus that heals once drained. The fibrin wall limits spread and should not be squeezed or broken.
  • Surgical and wound infection: postoperative wounds, chronic osteomyelitis after open fracture, meningitis after skull fracture.
  • Pneumonia: rapid, productive, necrotising and often fatal; nosocomial, ventilator-associated, post-influenza, IV drug use, cystic fibrosis, chronic granulomatous disease. Salmon-coloured sputum.
  • Osteomyelitis (S. aureus is the most common cause) and septic arthritis, especially in children: bone pain, fever, lytic lesions; haematogenous or from a wound.
  • Food poisoning (intoxication): preformed heat-stable enterotoxin; 2–6 hours later nausea, cramps, vomiting, then diarrhoea.

Toxic shock and scalded skin syndromes

Toxic shock syndrome (TSST-1)

  1. TSST-1 skips antigen processing and binds MHC class II on APCs and the Vβ region of the TCR together.
  2. This activates a huge, non-specific fraction of T cells → cytokine storm (TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6).
  3. Leaky vessels → hypotension, shock, multi-organ failure. Many people lack neutralising antibody.

Clinically: sudden high fever, hypotension, diffuse sunburn-like rash (erythroderma), diarrhoea and vomiting, then kidney, liver and CNS involvement; desquamation of palms and soles 1–2 weeks later. Risks: prolonged use of high-absorbency tampons, surgical wounds, retained packing.

Staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome (exfoliatins)

  • Newborns and infants (often 3–7 days old). Toxin spreads in blood to the skin.
  • Fever and irritability, then red tender skin starting around the mouth, spreading and peeling like a burn.
  • Toxin cleaves desmoglein-1 in the stratum granulosum → intraepidermal split; Nikolsky sign positive; heals without scarring; good prognosis.

Coagulase-negative staphylococci

Named in syllabus week IV; from Jawetz, since the slides list them only:

  • S. epidermidis: normal skin flora; makes biofilm on catheters, prosthetic joints and valves, shunts; a common contaminant of blood cultures; novobiocin sensitive.
  • S. saprophyticus: urinary tract infection in young sexually active women (second to E. coli); novobiocin resistant.
  • MRSA: methicillin resistance comes from the mecA gene encoding an altered penicillin-binding protein (PBP2a); β-lactamase is the cause of plain penicillin resistance.

Laboratory diagnosis and prevention

  • Specimens: pus (better than swabs), sputum or bronchoscopic lavage, faeces, vomit or suspected food, blood (sepsis, osteomyelitis, endocarditis), midstream urine, anterior nasal and perineal swabs for carriers.
  • Smear: gram-positive cocci in clusters. Culture: typical colonies on blood agar in 18 h at 37°C; S. aureus ferments mannitol.
  • Catalase (staphylococci +, streptococci −), coagulase (S. aureus +). API Staph strip: 20 microtubes of substrates, read by colour changes against an index.
  • Molecular typing (pulsed-field gel electrophoresis, multilocus sequence typing) tracks epidemic clones.
  • Prevention: thorough hand washing before procedures is the most important measure; intranasal mupirocin reduces carriage.
Watch out

Slide 26 says the catalase test detects “cytochrome oxidase enzymes”. It detects catalase (H₂O₂ → H₂O + O₂); cytochrome oxidase is a separate test (the oxidase test). The nose carriage rate is given as 25% on one slide and about 30% on another.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • A staphylococcus (versus a streptococcus) is always catalase positive; that is the one thing you know without more tests.
  • Staph food poisoning comes fast (2–6 h) because the toxin is preformed; reheating does not help (heat stable).
  • Superantigens bind outside the peptide groove: MHC II + TCR Vβ.
  • SSSS splits the epidermis (granular layer), so no scarring.
  • Commonest cause of osteomyelitis: S. aureus.

Key terms

Plasmid: extra-chromosomal DNA circle · Transformation: uptake of naked DNA · Transduction: phage-carried DNA transfer · Prophage: integrated phage DNA · Conjugation: pilus-mediated DNA transfer · F factor: fertility plasmid · Transposon: jumping gene · Catalase: splits H₂O₂ · Coagulase: clots plasma · β-haemolysis: complete red cell lysis · Protein A: IgG Fc binder · Enterotoxin: toxin acting on the gut · Superantigen: non-specific T cell activator · TSST-1: toxic shock toxin · Exfoliatin: desmoglein-1 protease · Nikolsky sign: skin peels with rubbing · PVL: Panton-Valentine leukocidin · MRSA: methicillin-resistant S. aureus · Mupirocin: nasal decolonisation antibiotic.
