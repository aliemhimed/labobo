Vesicular Transport and Exocytosis

How proteins travel between compartments in coated vesicles (COPII, COPI, clathrin), how each organelle keeps its own proteins (retention and KDEL retrieval), and how vesicles find, dock with and fuse into their target (motors, tethers, Rabs, SNAREs, NSF), ending with exocytosis.

Labobo topic: MB Lec 10: Vesicular Transport and Exocytosis

In 60 seconds

  • Vesicles leave ribosome-free ER exit sites, fuse into VTCs in the ERGIC, then go to the Golgi.
  • Coats: outer cage + inner adaptors; they bend the membrane and select cargo.
  • COPII: ER → ERGIC/Golgi (forward). COPI: backward (Golgi → ER, trans → cis). Clathrin: TGN → endosomes/lysosomes, and endocytosis.
  • COPII assembly: GEF loads Sar1-GTP → its helix bends the membrane → Sec23/Sec24 join (Sec24 binds cargo tails). GTP hydrolysis → coat falls off.
  • Residents stay by retention and retrieval: soluble ER proteins carry C-terminal KDEL, caught by KDEL receptors and returned in COPI vesicles.
  • Movement (microtubules, kinesin/dynein) → tethering (golgins, Rabs; 50–200 nm) → docking (v-SNARE + t-SNARE, > 35 SNAREs) → fusion; NSF (ATP) pulls SNAREs apart.
  • Botulinum and tetanus toxins cut neuronal SNAREs → paralysis. Neurotransmitter release: Ca²⁺ + synaptotagmin.

ER to Golgi: the ERGIC

  • Vesicles carrying cargo bud from the ER and head to the Golgi.
  • They form at ER exit sites: specialised regions of RER cisternae without ribosomes.
  • Soon after budding, they fuse with each other, forming larger vesicles and tubules between the ER and Golgi: the ERGIC (ER–Golgi intermediate compartment). The carriers formed there are vesicular-tubular carriers (VTCs).

Protein coats

  • Where budding happens, soluble proteins gather on the cytosolic surface of the donor membrane to form a coat; the coated bud pinches off as a coated vesicle.
  • Two jobs of the coat:
    1. Mechanical: curves the membrane into a bud.
    2. Selection: picks what the vesicle carries (secretory, lysosomal and membrane cargo, plus the targeting and docking machinery).
  • Two layers: an outer cage/scaffold, and an inner layer of adaptors that bind the bilayer and the cargo.
  • Adaptors choose cargo by binding the cytosolic tails of integral membrane proteins.

Memory hook

COPII goes to the Golgi (“two to”); COPI goes back in to the ER.

COPII assembly and disassembly

COPII coats select and concentrate cargo. ER membrane proteins with “ER export” signals in their cytosolic tails bind COPII proteins.

What COPII vesicles carry

  1. Enzymes that act later in the pathway, e.g. Golgi glycosyltransferases.
  2. Membrane proteins for docking and fusion with the target.
  3. Receptors that bind soluble cargo (secretory proteins).
  1. A GEF (guanine-exchange factor) on the ER swaps GDP for GTP on the small G protein Sar1.
  2. Sar1-GTP pushes a finger-like helix into the cytosolic leaflet → the membrane curves.
  3. Sar1-GTP recruits the Sec23–Sec24 heterodimer, which curves the membrane further; cargo tails bind Sec24.
  4. The full coat forms and the vesicle pinches off.
  5. Before fusing, the coat must come off: GTP hydrolysis makes Sar1-GDP, which leaves the membrane, followed by the other COPII subunits.

COPI, retention and retrieval

COPI vesicles return proteins backward: Golgi enzymes trans → cis, and ER enzymes from the ERGIC/Golgi back to the ER.

If vesicles bud all the time, how does each compartment keep its own proteins? Two mechanisms:

MechanismHow
RetentionResidents are excluded from vesicles by their physical properties, e.g. soluble proteins in large complexes, or membrane proteins with short transmembrane domains
Retrieval“Escaped” proteins are brought back

The KDEL retrieval signal

  • ER-resident proteins carry short retrieval signals at their C-terminus.
  • Soluble ER lumen proteins (e.g. PDI, chaperones) end in Lys-Asp-Glu-Leu = KDEL (single-letter code: K lysine, D aspartate, E glutamate, L leucine).
  • KDEL receptors, integral membrane proteins shuttling between the cis Golgi and ER, capture them and send them back in COPI vesicles.
  • Delete the KDEL and the protein is not returned: it moves on through the Golgi.

Movement and tethering

Fusion is selective: ER vesicles fuse with the ERGIC or cis Golgi, not with a trans cisterna. Vesicle membrane proteins govern where it goes and what it can fuse with (still not fully understood).

  1. Movement: often over long distances, along microtubules with kinesin and dynein motors, like a railway.
  2. Tethering: first contact, by two groups of tethering proteins: long fibrous rods that bridge 50–200 nm, and large multiprotein complexes that hold membranes closer. Golgins (fibrous, around the Golgi) act like tentacles that catch vesicles. Rabs, small G proteins cycling between GTP (active) and GDP (inactive), also tether; GTP-bound Rabs attach to membranes by a lipid anchor.
  3. Docking, then fusion (below).

Docking and fusion: SNAREs

  • Docking brings the two membranes very close through interactions of the cytosolic parts of integral proteins: the SNAREs, a family of more than 35 proteins.
  • Each has a SNARE motif of 60–70 amino acids that pairs with other SNARE motifs.
  • v-SNAREs are on the vesicle; t-SNAREs are on the target membrane. Together they form a four-stranded helical bundle wherever membranes are to fuse.
  • SNARE pairing can pull bilayers together hard enough to fuse them; in cells it is required but not sufficient.
  • In regulated secretion, the SNARE bundle is held in an arrested state by accessory proteins; docked vesicles release their contents almost instantly when Ca²⁺ rises.
  • After fusion, all the SNAREs sit in one membrane. NSF, a doughnut-shaped cytosolic protein, uses ATP to twist the bundle apart for reuse.
In the clinic: botulism and tetanus

The SNAREs of synaptic vesicles and the presynaptic membrane are targets of two of the most potent bacterial toxins. Both toxins are proteases whose only known substrates are SNAREs. Cutting neuronal SNAREs blocks neurotransmitter release → paralysis.

Exocytosis

  • Exocytosis = fusion of a secretory vesicle or granule with the plasma membrane and discharge of its contents.
  • Goes on more or less continuously in most cells (delivering membrane and extracellular materials).
  • Best studied in regulated secretion, especially neurotransmitter release: a nerve impulse → Ca²⁺ influx → fusion, controlled by the Ca²⁺-binding protein synaptotagmin on the synaptic vesicle.
  • In other cells, Ca²⁺ is released from internal stores.
  • Contact forms a small protein-lined fusion pore.
  • After fusion: the vesicle’s luminal surface becomes the outer surface of the plasma membrane; its cytosolic surface becomes the inner surface.

Exam traps

Exam trap
  • KDEL is at the C-terminus, not N.
  • KDEL proteins return in COPI, not COPII, vesicles.
  • Coats must come off before fusion (Sar1 GTP hydrolysis).
  • NSF needs ATP; Sar1 and Rab use GTP.
  • Botulinum and tetanus toxins are proteases of SNAREs.

Key terms

ERGIC
ER–Golgi intermediate compartment.
VTC
Vesicular-tubular carrier.
Coated vesicle
Vesicle with a protein coat that shapes it and selects cargo.
COPII / COPI
Forward / backward ER–Golgi vesicle coats.
Clathrin
Coat for TGN and endocytic vesicles.
Sar1
Small G protein starting COPII assembly.
KDEL receptor
Retrieves escaped ER proteins.
Tethering
First capture of a vesicle by its target.
Rab
Small G protein involved in tethering.
SNARE
Protein mediating docking and fusion (v- and t-).
NSF
ATPase that separates SNAREs.
Exocytosis
Vesicle fusion with the plasma membrane releasing contents.
