Types of Cells, Viruses, Viroids and Model Organisms

The two prokaryotic domains (Archaea, Bacteria), the kinds of eukaryotic cells and how they specialise, what viruses are and how they replicate (lytic and lysogenic), viroids, and the six model organisms of research.

Labobo topic: MB Lec 2: Cell Types, Viruses & Model Organisms

In 60 seconds

  • Prokaryotes: Archaea (extremophiles: methanogens, halophiles, acidophiles, thermophiles) and Bacteria.
  • Mycoplasma: smallest bacterium (0.2 μm), no cell wall, ~500 genes. Cyanobacteria: most complex, chlorophyll a, release O₂; ancestors of chloroplasts.
  • A fertilised human egg gives about 250 differentiated cell types.
  • Ivanovsky (1892): tobacco mosaic disease passes through bacteria-proof filters → viruses. TMV ≈ 300 nm × 18 nm.
  • Viruses are obligate intracellular parasites: DNA or RNA, single or double, 3–4 to several hundred genes, inside a capsid (often an icosahedron), sometimes an envelope.
  • HIV gp120 binds CD4. Lytic cycle bursts the cell; lysogenic integrates as a provirus.
  • Models: E. coli, yeast, Arabidopsis (120 Mbp), C. elegans (~1000 cells), Drosophila, mouse.

Types of prokaryotic cells

Two domains: Archaea (archaebacteria, “extremophiles”, most living in very harsh places) and Bacteria (including cyanobacteria).

GroupLives / does
MethanogensConvert CO₂ + H₂ → methane (CH₄)
HalophilesExtremely salty places: Dead Sea, deep-sea brine pools (as salty as 5 M MgCl₂)
AcidophilesAcid-loving; drainage fluid of mine shafts
ThermophilesVery high temperatures; “strain 121” is a hyperthermophile

Many archaea also live in ordinary soil and ocean conditions.

Bacteria
GroupFeatures

Types of eukaryotic cells

  • Unicellular: protists. One cell does everything: senses the environment, traps food, escapes predators, expels excess water. Example: Vorticella, a complex ciliated protist.
  • Multicellular: fungi (some unicellular, e.g. yeasts), plants, animals.

Cell differentiation

  • In multicellular organisms, different jobs are done by specialised cells, formed by differentiation.
  • A fertilised human egg develops into about 250 distinct differentiated cell types (gland, muscle, bone…).
  • Differentiated cells look different, contain unique materials and have distinct functions:
    • skeletal muscle cells: precisely aligned filaments of contractile proteins;
    • red blood cells: disc-shaped sacs filled with one protein, hemoglobin;
    • cartilage cells, etc.
  • Despite their differences, all are built from similar organelles.

Discovery of viruses

  1. Tobacco mosaic disease (plants) and hoof-and-mouth disease (cattle) suggested an infectious agent other than bacteria.
  2. Dmitri Ivanovsky forced sap from a diseased tobacco plant through filters with pores too small for the smallest known bacterium.
  3. The filtrate was still infective. In 1892 he concluded the pathogen was smaller and simpler than bacteria: a virus.

Tobacco mosaic virus (TMV): identical protein subunits wrap a single helical RNA molecule in a rod about 300 nm long and 18 nm wide.

Structure of viruses

  • Cause many human diseases: AIDS, polio, influenza, common cold, Ebola, measles, and some cancers.
  • All viruses are obligate intracellular parasites; hosts can be plant, animal or bacterial cells.
  • Outside a cell, a virus is a virion: a macromolecular package that cannot reproduce or metabolise on its own. Whether viruses are alive is still debated.
  • Genetic material: DNA or RNA, single- or double-stranded; from 3–4 genes to several hundred.
  • Surrounded by a protein coat, the capsid.
VirusStructure
Adenovirus (respiratory infections)Icosahedral capsid (20 flat faces; a common polyhedral shape)
HIV (AIDS)Capsid inside a lipid envelope taken from the host’s plasma membrane as it buds out
T bacteriophageAmong the most complex viruses (and the most abundant biological entities on Earth): polyhedral head with DNA, cylindrical stalk that injects DNA, tail fibres; looks like a landing module

Binding to the host

Each virus has a surface protein that binds a specific component of its host cell. HIV’s gp120 (glycoprotein, 120,000 daltons) binds CD4 on certain white blood cells, letting the virus enter.

Lytic and lysogenic cycles

LyticLysogenic
What happensVirus takes over the cell, stops its normal synthesis, makes viral nucleic acids and proteins, assembles virionsViral DNA integrates into the host chromosome as a provirus and waits
Host cellLyses (bursts), releasing new virionsSurvives (for now)

What a provirus can do

  • Bacteria: behave normally until a stimulus such as UV light activates the provirus → lysis and release of progeny.
  • Some animal cells (HIV): keep producing virions that bud from the surface without lysing the cell, which acts as a virus factory for a while.
  • Some animal cells: lose control of growth and division and become malignant (tumour viruses).

Viruses as tools; viroids

  • Research tools for studying DNA replication and gene expression.
  • Viral vectors deliver genes into human cells: the basis of gene therapy (in vivo or in vitro). Vectors include adenoviruses, herpes simplex viruses, retroviruses, and bacteriophages (hybrid vectors). Main uses: research, vaccines, gene therapy.
  • Insect- and bacteria-killing viruses may help fight pests and bacterial infections. Phage therapy has been used for decades in eastern Europe and Russia; the West relied on antibiotics.

Viroids

Listed in the topics; from Karp: viroids are even simpler infectious agents, small circular RNA molecules with no protein coat and no protein-coding genes. They cause diseases in plants.

Watch out

Watch out: Rhizobium is not a cyanobacterium

Slide 5 says nitrogen-fixing bacteria such as Rhizobium “belong to cyanobacteria”. Rhizobium is a separate group of soil bacteria living in legume root nodules. Some cyanobacteria also fix nitrogen, but nitrogen fixers are not all cyanobacteria.

Exam traps

Exam trap
  • Mycoplasma has no cell wall.
  • Cyanobacteria release oxygen; chloroplasts came from them.
  • HIV buds out without lysing its host cell.
  • The lysogenic provirus is integrated DNA.
  • The nude mouse lacks a thymus.
  • Yeast is the least complex model eukaryote.

Key terms

Archaea
Prokaryotic domain, many extremophiles.
Extremophile
Organism living in extreme conditions.
Differentiation
Process making specialised cells.
Virion
Complete virus particle outside a cell.
Capsid
Protein coat of a virus.
Envelope
Lipid membrane around some viruses, from the host.
Bacteriophage
Virus infecting bacteria.
Lytic cycle
Replication ending in cell lysis.
Lysogenic cycle / provirus
Viral DNA integrated in the host genome.
Viroid
Small naked circular RNA infecting plants.
Viral vector
Virus used to deliver genes.
Model organism
Species studied as a stand-in for biology in general.
