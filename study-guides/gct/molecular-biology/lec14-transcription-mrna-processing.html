Transcription and mRNA Processing

The central dogma, how RNA polymerase reads DNA, transcription in bacteria (sigma factor, promoters, two kinds of termination) and in eukaryotes, the structure of mRNA, capping, polyadenylation, splicing (spliceosome and self-splicing), alternative splicing, and FISH.

Labobo topic: MB Lec 14: Transcription and mRNA Processing

In 60 seconds

  • DNA → RNA → protein; proteins are needed to make both nucleic acids.
  • RNA polymerase: multi-subunit, polymerase + helicase + ATPase; uses rNTPs; reads the template 3′→5′ and makes RNA 5′→3′; 20–50 nt/s.
  • Bacteria: one polymerase (5-subunit core). Core + σ = holoenzyme, which finds the promoter; σ leaves after ~10 nucleotides. σ70 = housekeeping.
  • Termination: Rho-dependent (Rho moves 5′→3′ along the RNA, helicase) or Rho-independent (G-C hairpin + run of U’s).
  • Promoters: 100–1000 bp, upstream of +1. Eukaryotes need transcription factors; transcription and translation are separated in space and time.
  • Processing of pre-mRNA (made by Pol II): 5′ cap (7-methylguanosine, 5′–5′ triphosphate; capping enzyme binds the phosphorylated CTD), poly(A) tail (signal AAUAAA, cut 10–35 nt downstream, ~250 A’s, no template), splicing.
  • Spliceosome: snRNAs U1, U2, U4, U5, U6 (107–210 nt); two transesterifications; lariat. Alternative splicing: 60% of genes; fibronectin > 20 isoforms.

The central dogma

  • Hereditary information flows DNA → RNA → protein: a DNA gene encodes an RNA message that is translated into protein.
  • It does not show that proteins are needed to make DNA and RNA, or that many proteins regulate gene expression.
  • Transcription: the coded part of a gene is copied into single-stranded RNA by RNA polymerase (DNA is the template). “Transcribed” = rewritten in the same nucleotide language.
  • Translation: ribosomes link amino acids in the order dictated by mRNA, using the universal genetic code. “Translated” = into the amino acid language.
  • In eukaryotes the precursor RNA is processed into smaller mRNA, which leaves the nucleus for the cytoplasmic ribosomes.
  • Gene expression = making a functional product from a gene’s information.
  • Ribosomes contain rRNA (structure and catalysis of peptide bonds) and protein; tRNAs carry amino acids and decode mRNA.

RNA polymerase

  • DNA-dependent RNA polymerase: a multi-subunit (quaternary) enzyme with several activities: polymerase, helicase, ATPase.
  • Uses ribonucleoside triphosphates (rNTPs) (ATP is one) to extend the RNA.
  • It cannot recognise promoters alone: it needs σ factor (bacteria) or transcription factors (eukaryotes).
  • Moves along the template toward its 5′ end (3′→5′), unwinding DNA (helicase) and making RNA 5′→3′. Behind it the double helix re-forms.
  • Speed: 20–50 nucleotides per second; one gene may be read by a hundred or more polymerases at once; how often is tightly regulated.
  • Products: mRNA, tRNA, rRNA, catalytic RNA/snRNA, and miRNA (regulates gene expression by RNA interference).

Promoters and numbering

  • A promoter is a regulatory (non-coding) DNA region where RNA polymerase binds to start transcription.
  • Located near the start site, on the same strand, upstream; about 100–1000 bp long.
  • The first transcribed nucleotide is +1; the one before is −1 (no 0).
  • Downstream = direction the polymerase travels (+ numbers). Upstream = the opposite (− numbers), toward the 3′ end of the template strand.

Transcription in bacteria

  • E. coli has a single RNA polymerase: a core enzyme of 5 subunits.
  • Adding σ (sigma) factor → the holoenzyme, which slides along DNA until it finds a promoter (with a TATA-like sequence).
  • The holoenzyme separates the strands: the polymerase–σ–DNA complex with open strands is the open complex.
  • After ~10 nucleotides, the enzyme changes shape into a processive elongation complex; σ dissociates.
  • Holoenzyme = initiation; core enzyme = elongation.
  • Bacteria have several σ factors for different promoters; σ70 is the housekeeping factor for most genes.
  • With no nucleus, ribosomes start translating the mRNA even while its 3′ end is still being made: transcription and translation are coupled.

Transcription in eukaryotes

  • Several RNA polymerases (bacteria have one). From the textbook: Pol I → large rRNAs (28S, 18S, 5.8S); Pol II → all pre-mRNAs (and most snRNAs, miRNAs); Pol III → tRNAs, 5S rRNA.
  • Many transcription factors replace σ. General transcription factors act in initiation, elongation and termination.
  • mRNAs, rRNAs and tRNAs all come from longer precursors (primary transcripts) that must be processed.
  • Genes are split: exons (coding) and introns (non-coding). The pre-RNA copies both; introns must be cut out and exons joined.
  • Transcription and translation are separated in space (nucleus vs cytoplasm) and time (processing first, then export).

Structure of mRNA

  • Properties: a continuous coding sequence for one polypeptide; found in the cytoplasm; attached to ribosomes when translated; usually a sizeable non-coding part (e.g. human β-globin mRNA ~25% non-coding).
  • Layout: 5′ methylguanosine cap → 5′ UTR → coding region → 3′ UTR → poly(A) tail.
  • The cap and poly(A) tail are found on eukaryotic mRNAs only, not on bacterial mRNAs, tRNAs or rRNAs.

Processing a pre-mRNA (transcribed by Pol II over the whole transcription unit, from promoter to terminator) needs three steps: 5′ cap, 3′ poly(A) tail, splicing.

5′ cap

  • As soon as the 5′ end leaves the polymerase, enzymes add 7-methylguanosine.
  • It is joined by an unusual 5′–5′ triphosphate link (not a 5′–3′ phosphodiester bond).
  • Pol II has a phosphorylated carboxyl-terminal domain (CTD) that binds the dimeric capping enzyme. Pols I and III have no CTD, so only Pol II transcripts are capped.
Functions of the cap
Function
Protects mRNA from exonuclease degradation
Helps export to the cytoplasm
Binds the factor needed to start translation

3′ poly(A) tail

  1. Nearly all pre-mRNAs contain the signal AAUAAA.
  2. An endonuclease cuts 10–35 nucleotides downstream, leaving a free 3′-OH.
  3. Poly(A) polymerase adds A’s one at a time without a template (~100–250; typically starts ~250, then shortens).
  • The tail plus its bound protein protects mRNA from exonucleases.
  • Up to 40% of prokaryotic mRNAs are also polyadenylated, but with short tails (15–60 A’s). Lower eukaryotes (yeast, invertebrates) have shorter tails; each species has its own length.

Splicing

The last processing step: introns are cut out and exons joined.

Example: β-globin

The β-globin gene has 3 exons and 2 introns, plus non-coding regions from the start site to the poly(A) site. Processing: cap at the start site → cleave and add A’s at the poly(A) site → splice out the two introns.

The spliceosome

  • Five U-rich snRNAs: U1, U2, U4, U5, U6 (107–210 nucleotides), each bound to proteins as snRNPs.
  • They assemble on the pre-mRNA into the spliceosome, by base pairing (U1 and U6 to the pre-RNA, and snRNAs to each other).
  • Splice sites have consensus sequences (from comparing many pre-mRNAs): GU at the intron’s 5′ end, AG at its 3′ end, and a branch-point A.
  1. First transesterification: the branch-point A bonds to the intron’s 5′ end, forming a lariat.
  2. Second transesterification: the two exons are joined by a normal phosphodiester bond, releasing the intron (with snRNPs).
  3. The intron–snRNP complex comes apart; snRNPs are reused; the intron is quickly degraded.

Self-splicing

  • Some RNAs slowly splice out introns without any protein: the intron is a ribozyme.
  • Group I introns: in rRNA transcripts of bacteria, protozoa and some animal cells.
  • Group II introns: the widest group, in all three domains; in mitochondrial and chloroplast mRNAs, nuclear mRNAs of plants and fungi, some rRNAs and tRNAs. They fold into six domains; the 2′-OH of an adenosine in domain VI attacks the 5′ splice site, forming a 2′–5′ bond and a lariat.
  • This discovery changed ideas about what RNA can do.
  • Group II self-splicing resembles spliceosome splicing, suggesting the snRNAs play the catalytic role of the group II intron’s RNA structure.

Alternative splicing

  • A primary transcript can be processed in two or more ways: a sequence that is an intron in one path is an exon in another, so one gene codes several polypeptides.
  • About 60% of human genes are alternatively spliced.
  • Humans may have over 2 million kinds of protein but only 20,000–25,000 genes (genome sequenced 2003); alternative splicing helps explain the gap.

In situ hybridisation (FISH)

  • RNA-Seq lists which genes are expressed but not where. In situ hybridisation (“in place”) shows a specific DNA or RNA in its normal location.
  • Uses single-stranded DNA or RNA probes labelled with fluorescent dyes or radioisotopes that bind complementary sequences in tissue or on chromosomes.
  • Examples: locating satellite DNA; detecting HPV DNA in infected cell nuclei (pink probe, green cytoplasm).
Applications
Chromosome abnormalities: aneuploidy, deletions, duplications, translocations (e.g. in the fetus)
Gene mapping to chromosome locations
Gene expression: where mRNAs are in cells and tissues
Clinical diagnosis: genetic disorders, some cancers, birth defects

Exam traps

Exam trap
  • RNA is made 5′→3′ while the template is read 3′→5′.
  • Only the holoenzyme finds promoters; the core enzyme elongates.
  • Rho moves 5′→3′ along the RNA.
  • Only Pol II transcripts are capped (CTD).
  • The cap link is 5′–5′.
  • Poly(A) is added without a template.
  • Bacteria translate mRNA while it is being transcribed.

Key terms

Transcription / translation
DNA → RNA / RNA → protein.
Promoter
DNA site where RNA polymerase binds to start.
Holoenzyme
Bacterial core polymerase + σ factor.
Open complex
Polymerase–σ–DNA with strands separated.
Rho factor
Helicase that ends some bacterial transcripts.
Transcription factors
Eukaryotic proteins needed for polymerase to work.
Exon / intron
Kept coding segment / removed non-coding segment.
5′ cap
7-Methylguanosine on mRNA’s 5′ end.
Poly(A) tail
Adenosine string at the 3′ end.
Spliceosome
snRNP complex that removes introns.
Lariat
Looped intron formed during splicing.
Alternative splicing
Different exon combinations from one gene.
FISH
Fluorescence in situ hybridisation.
