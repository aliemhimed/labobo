ER Quality Control, the Unfolded Protein Response, and Import into Mitochondria and Peroxisomes

How the ER checks every new glycoprotein (calnexin cycle), destroys failures (ERAD, cystic fibrosis), copes with overload (UPR), and how proteins made on free ribosomes are imported after synthesis into peroxisomes and mitochondria (TOM and TIM).

Labobo topic: MB Lec 9: ER Quality Control and Protein Sorting

In 60 seconds

  • Two of three glucoses are trimmed → calnexin binds the glycoprotein → glucosidase II removes the last glucose → release.
  • Still misfolded? UGGT (senses exposed hydrophobic patches) adds a glucose back → another round with calnexin.
  • Repeated failure → dislocation to the cytosol → sugars removed → proteasome: ERAD. Over 60 diseases, including cystic fibrosis.
  • UPR: BiP normally holds sensors off; when misfolded proteins pile up, BiP leaves → PERK dimerises → phosphorylates eIF2α → less translation; sensor fragments turn on stress genes; if it fails → apoptosis.
  • Nucleus, mitochondria, chloroplasts and peroxisomes import proteins post-translationally (made on free ribosomes).
  • Peroxisomes: PTS/mPTS signals; form from fusion of two ER-derived vesicles (each half an importomer); import folded proteins.
  • Mitochondria: ~99% of proteins imported; matrix proteins have an N-terminal presequence (positive + hydrophobic faces); TOM → TIM23 (matrix) or TIM22 (inner membrane); driven by the membrane potential (stopped by DNP).

Quality control: the calnexin cycle

Soon after the oligosaccharide is attached, it is gradually trimmed, and this sets up a check of whether the protein is fit to move on.

  1. Step 1: two of the three terminal glucose residues are removed.
  2. Step 2: the glycoprotein, now with one glucose, binds the ER chaperone calnexin.
  3. Step 3: glucosidase II removes the last glucose → the protein is released.
  4. Step 4: if it is still unfolded or misfolded, UGGT, a conformation-sensing enzyme, adds one glucose back to an exposed mannose. UGGT spots misfolded proteins by their exposed hydrophobic residues.
  5. Step 5: the re-tagged protein binds the chaperones again for another chance to fold.
  6. After repeated failure, the protein is sent to the cytosol and destroyed in the proteasome.
Memory hook

One glucose = “please hold me” (calnexin). No glucose = “I’m done”. UGGT is the inspector who sticks the glucose back on if the protein isn’t ready.

ERAD and cystic fibrosis

  • Misfolded proteins are not destroyed inside the ER. They are moved to the cytosol by dislocation (mechanism still debated).
  • In the cytosol their sugar chains are removed and they are destroyed by proteasomes.
  • This is ER-associated degradation (ERAD): it keeps faulty proteins from reaching the rest of the cell, but can also cause disease.
  • Over 60 human diseases are linked to ERAD.
In the clinic: cystic fibrosis

In most CF patients the epithelial plasma membrane lacks the protein encoded by the CF gene. The mutant protein would often work if given time to fold, but ER quality control destroys it, so it never reaches the cell surface. From the textbook: the protein is CFTR, a chloride channel; the commonest mutation, ΔF508, is handled this way; the disease is autosomal recessive and causes thick mucus in lungs and pancreas.

The unfolded protein response (UPR)

  • Sometimes misfolded proteins are made faster than they can be removed; their build-up is potentially lethal. The cell responds with the UPR.
  • ER sensors monitor unfolded proteins. Normally they are kept inactive by the chaperone BiP.
  • When misfolded proteins accumulate, BiP is pulled away to help fold them, leaving the sensors free and active.
  1. Released from BiP, the sensor PERK dimerises and becomes an active protein kinase.
  2. PERK phosphorylates eIF2α, a factor needed to start protein synthesis; phosphorylated eIF2 is inactive.
  3. Fewer new proteins enter the ER, giving time to clear those already there.
  4. Meanwhile, the cytosolic part of another sensor travels to the nucleus and switches on stress genes.

The UPR is a survival response, but if it can’t fix the stress, it triggers apoptosis and the cell dies.

Post-translational import

  • Trafficking depends on sorting signals (e.g. signal peptide, mannose 6-phosphate of lysosomal enzymes) and receptors that recognise them.
  • The nucleus, mitochondria, chloroplasts and peroxisomes import proteins through their outer membranes, using address sequences read by receptors.
  • The RER imports co-translationally; these organelles import post-translationally, after complete synthesis on free ribosomes.

Import into peroxisomes

  • Peroxisomes have only two places for a protein: the boundary membrane and the matrix.
  • Proteins carry a peroxisomal targeting signal: PTS for matrix proteins, mPTS for membrane proteins.
  • PTS receptors bind them in the cytosol and shuttle them to the peroxisome surface.
  • Formation: peroxisomes form when two different ER-derived vesicles fuse, each carrying half of the peroxisomal translocon (importomer); fusion completes it.
  • Unlike mitochondria and chloroplasts, peroxisomes import matrix proteins fully folded, even multi-subunit ones (how is unknown).

Import into mitochondria

Four destinations: outer membrane, inner membrane, intermembrane space, matrix.

  • Mitochondria make only 13 of their own polypeptides (mammals); about 99% are nuclear-encoded, made on free ribosomes and imported post-translationally.
  • Most matrix proteins have a removable N-terminal presequence: positively charged residues on one face of a helix and hydrophobic residues on the other.
  • Most inner membrane proteins have internal targeting sequences that stay in the protein.
  1. The protein must arrive unfolded (extended); chaperones such as Hsp70 and Hsp90 prepare it and bring it to the outer membrane.
  2. TOM complex (translocase of the outer membrane): receptors plus a channel across the outer membrane.
  3. Inner membrane: TIM22 inserts inner-membrane proteins with internal signals into the bilayer; TIM23 takes proteins with an N-terminal presequence (all matrix proteins) right into the matrix.
  4. Entry into the matrix is powered by the electric potential across the inner membrane pulling on the positive signal; dissipating it with DNP stops import.
  5. In the matrix, mtHsp70 helps the chain in; the presequence is cut off and the protein folds to its native shape.

Exam traps

Exam trap
  • Misfolded ER proteins are degraded in the cytosol, not the ER.
  • In CF the mutant protein might work but is destroyed before reaching the surface.
  • PERK phosphorylates eIF2α, which inactivates it.
  • The UPR can end in apoptosis.
  • Mitochondrial import needs the membrane potential; DNP blocks it.
  • Peroxisomes arise from ER-derived vesicles.
