The Chemical Basis of Life: Macromolecules

The four kinds of organic molecules in a cell, functional groups, and the four macromolecules (carbohydrates, lipids, nucleic acids, proteins), with a close look at DNA architecture, base pairing, DNA vs RNA, ribozymes and ATP.

Labobo topic: MB Lec 3: Chemical Basis & Macromolecules

In 60 seconds

  • Cell organic molecules: macromolecules, their building blocks, metabolic intermediates, and miscellaneous molecules (vitamins, steroids, ATP, cAMP).
  • Proteins, nucleic acids and polysaccharides are polymers (made by polymerisation); lipids are not.
  • Sugars: (CH₂O)ₙ; glycosidic bond = C1 of one sugar to an –OH of another (C–O–C).
  • Glycogen (most branched), starch (helical), cellulose (unbranched, extended): all glucose. Glycogen/starch store energy; cellulose/chitin are structural.
  • Fat = glycerol + 3 fatty acids (ester bonds); phospholipid = 2 fatty acids + phosphate + polar group; steroids = 4 rings (cholesterol).
  • DNA: sugar–phosphate backbone outside, bases inside; phosphodiester bonds; 5′ phosphate end, 3′ OH end; antiparallel.
  • A=T (2 H bonds), G≡C (3 H bonds); G-C-rich DNA is more stable. RNA: single, short, unstable, has U and ribose. Ribozymes catalyse.

Understanding cell function requires knowing the structure and properties of these molecules.

Functional groups

  • Most functional groups contain electronegative atoms (N, P, O, S).
  • They make organic molecules more polar, more water-soluble and more reactive.
  • Several can ionise and become positively or negatively charged (e.g. –COO⁻, –NH₃⁺, phosphate).

Macromolecules and building blocks

  • Proteins, nucleic acids and carbohydrates are polymers: many low-molecular-weight monomers joined by polymerisation.
  • Lipids are macromolecule-like but are not polymers of repeating units.

Carbohydrates

  • Simple sugars (monosaccharides) and everything built from them.
  • Main roles: stores of chemical energy and durable building materials (e.g. plant cell walls).
  • General formula (CH₂O)ₙ. By carbons: triose (3), tetrose (4), pentose (5), hexose (6), heptose (7).
  • Monosaccharides: glucose, fructose, galactose (hexoses), ribose, deoxyribose (pentoses).

Disaccharides and the glycosidic bond

Sugars join by covalent glycosidic bonds: C1 of one sugar reacts with a hydroxyl of another, forming a C–O–C link.

Lipids

Important lipids: fats, steroids, phospholipids.

Fats (triacylglycerols)

  • A glycerol linked by ester bonds to three fatty acids: a triacylglycerol (triglyceride, neutral fat).
  • A fatty acid has a hydrophobic hydrocarbon chain and a hydrophilic carboxyl group (–COO⁻ at body pH). Molecules with both kinds of region are amphipathic.
  • Cell fatty acids are typically 14–20 carbons. Stearic acid is an 18-carbon saturated fatty acid common in animal fat.
  • Saturated: no double bonds. Unsaturated: double bonds, naturally in the cis configuration (which kinks the chain).

Phospholipids

  • Like a fat but with only two fatty acids (a diacylglycerol); the third glycerol –OH carries a phosphate bonded to a small polar group such as choline.
  • Example: phosphatidylcholine: negative phosphate + positive choline = hydrophilic head; fatty acid tails = hydrophobic.
  • Two ends with opposite properties make them ideal for cell membranes.

Steroids

  • Built on a four-ring hydrocarbon skeleton.
  • Cholesterol: part of animal cell membranes; precursor of testosterone, progesterone, estrogen, vitamin D and bile acids.
  • Largely absent from plant cells (so vegetable oils are “cholesterol-free”), though plants have related sterols.

Nucleic acids: nucleotides

  • Nucleic acids are long chains of nucleotides.
  • Nucleotide = sugar + nitrogenous base + phosphate. Sugar + base alone = nucleoside.
  • Bases: purines (two rings: A, G) and pyrimidines (one ring: C, T, U).
  • DNA: A, G, C, T; RNA: A, G, C, U.
  • DNA also has minor bases (methylated and oxidised forms).

DNA architecture

  • DNA is a long, unbranched, double-stranded polymer wound into a helix: the Watson–Crick model, which explained storage, replication and repair.
  • DNA sugar = deoxyribose, a five-carbon single-ring pentose.
  • Adjacent sugars are joined through one phosphate by a phosphodiester bond (really two covalent bonds, one on each side of the phosphate).
  • Phosphodiester bonds are the only links along a strand. Sugars + phosphates form the backbone, key to stability.
  • The backbone is outside the helix; the bases are inside.

Chemical polarity

  • Each phosphate joins the 5′ carbon of one sugar to the 3′ carbon of the next. This gives each strand direction, an asymmetric backbone, and makes the two strands antiparallel.
  • The 5′ end has a free phosphate; the 3′ end has a free –OH.
  • Polarity lets information be read in a consistent order, like reading English left to right: 5′ → 3′.

Base pairing and complementarity

  • Hydrogen bonds link bases on opposite strands (H with O or N). Each is weaker than a covalent phosphodiester bond, but thousands together give great stability.
  • A pairs only with T (2 H bonds); G only with C (3 H bonds). So G-C-rich DNA is more stable.
  • Pairs are always purine + pyrimidine, keeping the helix the same width all along.
  • Complementarity: one strand’s sequence predicts the other’s exactly.

Example: 5′-ATGC-3′ pairs with 3′-TACG-5′.

  • RNA is single-stranded but often folds back on itself, forming double-stranded segments and complex 3-D shapes (e.g. ribosomal RNA).

Watch out

Watch out: amphipathic fats?

Slide 15 describes the hydrophilic carboxyl group while talking about fats. That describes a free fatty acid, which is amphipathic. In a triacylglycerol all three carboxyl groups are tied up in ester bonds, so a fat is almost entirely hydrophobic (“neutral fat”). Phospholipids are the classic amphipathic membrane lipids.

Exam traps

Exam trap
  • Lipids are not true polymers.
  • Glycogen is more branched than starch.
  • Natural unsaturated fatty acids are cis.
  • Cholesterol makes vitamin D and bile acids, not insulin.
  • A–T has two H bonds, G–C three.
  • Bases are inside the helix; the backbone is outside.

Key terms

Monomer / polymer
Building block / chain of building blocks.
Polymerisation
Joining monomers into a polymer.
Functional group
Reactive group of atoms (e.g. –OH, –COOH).
Glycosidic bond
Sugar–sugar link (C–O–C).
Triacylglycerol
Glycerol + three fatty acids.
Amphipathic
Having hydrophilic and hydrophobic parts.
Steroid
Lipid with a four-ring skeleton.
Nucleotide
Sugar + base + phosphate.
Phosphodiester bond
Phosphate link between 3′ and 5′ carbons of sugars.
Antiparallel
Strands running in opposite 5′→3′ directions.
Complementarity
A pairs with T, G with C.
Ribozyme
Catalytic RNA.
