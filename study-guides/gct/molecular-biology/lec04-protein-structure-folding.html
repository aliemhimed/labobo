Amino acids and peptide bonds, the four levels of protein structure (with myoglobin, domains and hemoglobin as examples), Anfinsen’s self-assembly experiment, chaperones and chaperonins, and diseases of protein structure.

Labobo topic: MB Lec 4: Protein Structure & Misfolding

In 60 seconds

  • 20 amino acids; α-carbon carries –COO⁻ and –NH₃⁺ at neutral pH; peptide bonds form by condensation (water released).
  • N-terminus = free α-amino; C-terminus = free α-carboxyl. Side chains: polar charged, polar uncharged, nonpolar, unique (Cys disulfides).
  • Possible sequences = 20ⁿ. Sickle cell: one change, Glu → Val in β-globin.
  • Secondary (α-helix, β-sheet) = backbone H bonds; tertiary = side-chain non-covalent bonds. Silk (β-sheet) is ~5× stronger than steel by weight.
  • Myoglobin: 153 aa, ~75% α-helix, no β-sheet, no disulfides. Phospholipase C has 4 domains. TGF-β2 = homodimer; hemoglobin = heterotetramer (α₂β₂).
  • Anfinsen (1956): ribonuclease A (124 aa, 4 S–S) refolds after urea + mercaptoethanol are removed → sequence decides shape.
  • Hsp70 binds chains as they leave the ribosome; Hsp60 chaperonins fold inside chambers (TRiC folds up to 15% of proteins).

Amino acids and peptide bonds

  • Proteins are polymers of amino acids; each protein has a unique sequence that gives it its properties.
  • 20 amino acids build proteins in every organism, from viruses to humans.
  • Every amino acid has a carboxyl and an amino group separated by one carbon, the α-carbon.
  • In neutral water the α-carboxyl loses a proton (–COO⁻) and the α-amino gains one (–NH₃⁺).
  • In protein synthesis each amino acid joins two neighbours, forming a long, unbranched polypeptide chain.
  • A peptide bond links the carboxyl of one amino acid to the amino group of the next, releasing water: a condensation reaction.
  • The N-terminus has a free α-amino group; the C-terminus a free α-carboxyl.

Primary structure and sickle cell

  • Primary structure = the linear amino acid sequence.
  • With 20 building blocks, the number of possible chains of length n is 20ⁿ. Most chains have well over 100 residues, so the variety is practically unlimited.
  • The sequence is encoded in the genome and determines the 3-D shape and so the function. Changes from mutation are often not tolerated.
In the clinic: sickle cell anemia

A single change in β-globin: nonpolar valine replaces charged glutamic acid. Red cells change from discs to sickles, block small vessels and lyse → pain crises, organ ischemia, hypoxia and life-threatening complications.

Fibrous vs globular

  • Fibrous: elongated; mostly structural outside cells (collagen, elastin, keratin, silk); resist pulling and shearing.
  • Globular: compact; most enzymes and transport proteins.

Myoglobin

  • Stores oxygen in muscle; O₂ binds the iron of a heme group.
  • About 75% of its 153 amino acids are α-helix; no β-sheet.
  • Heme sits in a pocket of hydrophobic side chains, which lets O₂ bind without oxidising the iron.
  • No disulfide bonds: held entirely by non-covalent interactions.
  • Unlike myoglobin, most globular proteins have both α-helices and β-sheets. Each protein’s unique tertiary structure matches its sequence and function.

Domains

  • Most eukaryotic proteins have two or more domains: spatially distinct modules that fold independently.
  • Phospholipase C has 4 domains (one catalytic); each is found in other proteins too.
  • Domains often work semi-independently: binding a coenzyme vs a substrate, DNA vs another protein, or moving separately.
  • Examples: a PH domain lets a protein bind membranes containing a specific phospholipid; steroid hormone receptors have a ligand-binding domain and a DNA-binding domain.

Quaternary structure

  • Proteins with more than one chain; each chain is a subunit.
  • Subunits may be linked by disulfide bonds but most often by non-covalent bonds.
  • Homodimer: two identical subunits, e.g. TGF-β2. Heterodimer: two different subunits.
  • Hemoglobin: a heterotetramer of 2 α + 2 β globins held non-covalently (structure solved by Max Perutz, Cambridge, 1959). O₂ binding to one chain changes the shape and affinity of the others, so the assembled molecule behaves differently from separate chains.

Denaturation and Anfinsen’s experiment

  • Denaturation = unfolding of a protein, by detergents, organic solvents, radiation, heat, urea, guanidine chloride.
  1. 1956, Christian Anfinsen (NIH) studied ribonuclease A: one chain of 124 amino acids with 4 disulfide bonds.
  2. Mercaptoethanol (a reducing agent) breaks disulfides into –SH pairs, but the protein must first be partly unfolded by urea.
  3. With urea + mercaptoethanol, the enzyme lost all activity (fully unfolded).
  4. When both were removed, the enzyme regained its activity (renaturation) and was identical to the native protein.
  5. Conclusion: the amino acid sequence contains all the information for the 3-D shape: proteins can self-assemble.

Folding pathways

  • After myoglobin’s structure was solved (late 1950s), the question became: how does such a complex shape arise?
  • Once α-helices and β-sheets form, folding is driven by hydrophobic interactions that bury nonpolar residues in the core.
  • Simulations: proteins first explore many shapes, then funnel into fewer and fewer.
  • Most fold by a middle route where secondary structure and compaction happen together.
  • Because sequence decides folding, mutations can cause abnormal folding; many inherited disorders work this way, and misfolding can be fatal.

Chaperones and chaperonins

Molecular chaperones help unfolded or misfolded proteins reach their proper shape. They bind short hydrophobic stretches that are exposed in non-native proteins but buried in folded ones. They also help import proteins into organelles and prevent or reverse aggregation.

  1. Chains are made one amino acid at a time from the N-terminus.
  2. Hsp70 chaperones bind the growing chain as it leaves the exit channel of the large ribosomal subunit, stopping it binding other proteins and aggregating.
  3. After synthesis, many proteins are released and fold spontaneously; others are bound and released repeatedly until folded.
  4. Many larger proteins pass to chaperonins (Hsp60 family): cylinders with chambers where proteins fold undisturbed.

Watch out

Watch out: two different dystrophies

Slide 50 is titled “Myotonic dystrophy (Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy)” but describes only Duchenne. These are different diseases: Duchenne is an X-linked dystrophin defect; myotonic dystrophy is an autosomal dominant CTG repeat disease.

Watch out: “heterodimer”

Slide 34 is titled “Heterodimer” but hemoglobin is a heterotetramer (α₂β₂), as the text says.

Exam traps

Exam trap
  • Myoglobin has no β-sheet and no disulfide bonds.
  • Secondary structure uses backbone H bonds; tertiary uses side chains.
  • Anfinsen showed self-assembly; chaperones help but don’t carry shape information.
  • Hsp70 acts during translation; chaperonins (Hsp60) after.
  • TGF-β2 is a homodimer.

Key terms

α-carbon
Carbon carrying the amino and carboxyl groups.
Peptide bond
Amide link between amino acids.
N- / C-terminus
Ends with free amino / carboxyl group.
Disulfide bridge
Covalent S–S bond between cysteines.
Domain
Independently folding module of a protein.
Homodimer / heterodimer
Two identical / different subunits.
Denaturation / renaturation
Unfolding / refolding of a protein.
Self-assembly
Folding using only the sequence’s information.
Molecular chaperone
Protein that helps others fold.
Chaperonin
Hsp60 cylinder with folding chambers.
TRiC
Eukaryotic cytosolic chaperonin.
