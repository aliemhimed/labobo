Mitochondria and Cellular Respiration

Why oxygen changed life, the structure of the mitochondrion (two membranes, cristae, matrix, its own DNA), the three stages of respiration, how the electron transport chain and ATP synthase make ATP, uncouplers and brown fat, and three mitochondrial diseases.

Labobo topic: MB Lec 5: Mitochondria & Cellular Respiration

In 60 seconds

  • O₂ is toxic but, once used, lets cells oxidise food fully to CO₂ and H₂O and extract far more energy.
  • Mitochondria fill 15–20% of a liver cell, with > 1000 proteins. Heart/skeletal muscle ~5000 per cell; liver 1000–2000; mature RBCs none.
  • Outer membrane: ~50% lipid, porins (2–3 nm), freely permeable. Inner: protein:lipid > 3:1, > 100 polypeptides, no cholesterol, rich in cardiolipin, impermeable.
  • Matrix: proteins up to 500 mg/ml, ribosomes, circular mtDNA with 37 genes (13 polypeptides, 2 rRNAs, 22 tRNAs).
  • Respiration: glycolysis (cytoplasm, 10 enzymes) → pyruvate → acetyl-CoA → TCA (8 steps) → oxidative phosphorylation.
  • Proton pumps: Complexes I, III, IV; mobile carriers ubiquinone (in bilayer) and cytochrome c (intermembrane space). Mitchell (1961): chemiosmosis.
  • NADH ≈ 3 ATP, FADH₂ ≈ 2 ATP, ~36 ATP per glucose. DNP uncouples; brown fat makes heat. Diseases: MERRF, KSS, LHON.

Oxygen and evolution

  • Molecular oxygen is toxic: it takes extra electrons and reacts with biological molecules. Its appearance in the atmosphere was a powerful force of natural selection.
  • Species evolved that were protected from O₂ and then used it.
  • Without O₂, organisms extract only a little energy and excrete energy-rich products (lactic acid, ethanol).
  • With O₂, they oxidise these completely to CO₂ + H₂O, extracting much more energy. These first aerobes gave rise to all oxygen-using life.

Structure of mitochondria

  • In eukaryotes, oxygen is used in the mitochondrion: a bean-shaped, double-membrane organelle, the cell’s “energy transformer”.
  • It occupies 15–20% of a mammalian liver cell’s volume and has more than 1000 different proteins.
  • Main job: turn nutrient energy (e.g. glucose) into ATP. Cells use ATP constantly, so mitochondria always work, and need O₂ (which is why we breathe constantly).

The inner membrane’s two domains

  • Inner boundary membrane: just inside the outer membrane (forming a double envelope); rich in protein-import machinery.
  • Cristae: folded sheets reaching into the interior; huge surface area holding the machinery of aerobic respiration and ATP synthesis.
  • The two are joined by narrow tubes: cristae junctions.

Mitochondrial DNA

  • Mitochondria have their own circular DNA and machinery to make their own RNAs and proteins.
  • Human mtDNA has 37 genes: 13 polypeptides (all inner-membrane respiratory proteins), 2 rRNAs and 22 tRNAs.
  • The rest of the mitochondrial proteins are coded in the nucleus and imported.

Cellular respiration: the stages

Cellular respiration converts energy in nutrients (sugars, amino acids, fatty acids) into ATP; the usual electron acceptor is O₂, so it is aerobic. ATP then powers biosynthesis, movement and transport. Overall: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂ → 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O + energy (ATP).

  1. Glycolysis (cytoplasm, 10 enzymes): glucose (6C) → 2 pyruvate (3C); 2 ATP (net) and NADH.
  2. Pyruvate oxidation (matrix): each pyruvate → acetyl-CoA (2C), releasing CO₂ and making NADH.
  3. TCA (Krebs) cycle (matrix): acetyl-CoA joins a 4C molecule; the cycle regenerates it, making ATP (GTP), NADH, FADH₂ and CO₂.
  4. Oxidative phosphorylation (inner membrane): NADH and FADH₂ give electrons to the ETC; protons are pumped out; they flow back through ATP synthase, making ATP; O₂ accepts the electrons to form water.

Glycolysis, NAD and pyruvate

  • NAD (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide): two nucleotides joined through their phosphates; carries electrons and H⁺ in redox reactions.
  • NAD⁺ is the oxidising form (accepts electrons); NADH is the reducing form (donates them).
  • Pyruvate (anion of pyruvic acid), the end product of glycolysis, stands at the junction of anaerobic and aerobic pathways:
    • no O₂: fermentation → ethanol (yeast) or lactate (bacteria, and sometimes our muscles);
    • with O₂: into the mitochondria → acetyl-CoA → TCA cycle.

TCA cycle, acetyl-CoA and ATP

  • 8 steps, 8 enzymes. Most of the energy is captured by NAD⁺ and FAD.
  • Each turn: 3 NADH, 1 FADH₂, 1 ATP (GTP), and CO₂ as waste. Double this per glucose (2 pyruvates).
  • Hans Krebs won the Nobel Prize in 1953 for discovering the cycle.
  • Acetyl-CoA takes part in protein, carbohydrate and lipid metabolism; its main job is delivering acetyl groups to the cycle for oxidation.
  • ATP = adenine + ribose + three phosphates (α, β, γ); used up to ADP or AMP and regenerated repeatedly. Made mainly by glycolysis, the TCA cycle and oxidative phosphorylation.

Electron transport chain

ComponentRole
Complex I (NADH dehydrogenase)Takes electrons from NADH; pumps H⁺; passes electrons to CoQ
Complex II (succinate dehydrogenase)Takes electrons from FADH₂ (TCA cycle); passes them to CoQ; no pumping
Ubiquinone (CoQ)Mobile pool dissolved in the lipid bilayer; carries electrons to Complex III
Complex III (cytochrome bc₁)Pumps many H⁺; passes electrons to cytochrome c
Cytochrome cSmall soluble protein in the intermembrane space; carries electrons to Complex IV
Complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase)Gives electrons to O₂, which binds H⁺ → water; the first ETC part shown to be a redox-driven proton pump
  • The three proton pumps are Complexes I, III and IV: they move H⁺ from the matrix to the intermembrane space.
  • Ubiquinone and cytochrome c are not part of any complex; they shuttle electrons between the large, fairly immobile complexes.
  • Oxygen, one of the most electronegative elements, accepts the electrons and drives the whole process.

Chemiosmosis and ATP synthase

  1. Substrates (e.g. isocitrate, succinate) are oxidised; electrons go to NAD⁺ or FAD → NADH, FADH₂.
  2. Electrons pass along the chain; energy pumps protons out → a proton electrochemical gradient (the proton-motive force).
  3. Protons flow back down the gradient through ATP synthase (Complex V), which uses the energy to make ATP from ADP + Pi.

Proposed in 1961 by Peter Mitchell (University of Edinburgh): the chemiosmotic mechanism.

  • ATP synthase has two actions: a proton channel, and ATP synthesis.
  • Making ATP from ADP + Pi is energetically unfavourable; ATP synthase drives it by coupling it to the H⁺ gradient, changing shape to push ADP and phosphate together.
  • ATP is then exported from the mitochondrion to fuel the cell.

Uncouplers and brown fat

  • If ATP synthesis needs the gradient, then breaking the inner membrane or dissipating the gradient should stop it. Both are true.
  • 2,4-Dinitrophenol (DNP) carries H⁺ across the inner membrane, bypassing ATP synthase. Electron transport continues, but the proton-motive force is lost and no ATP is made: it uncouples the two.
  • Brown fat cells do this naturally: most energy from fat oxidation becomes heat. They act as biological heating pads, reviving hibernating animals and protecting newborn babies (e.g. the back of the neck) from cold.

Maternal inheritance

Mitochondria come from the egg, so mtDNA diseases pass from an affected mother to all her children; affected fathers don’t pass them on.

Watch out: lecture numbering

Watch out: 36 ATP

The lecture uses the classic 3 and 2 ATP per NADH and FADH₂ (≈ 36 per glucose). Newer values (2.5 and 1.5) give about 30–32. Use the lecture’s numbers unless a question says otherwise.

Exam traps

Exam trap
  • The inner membrane has cardiolipin and no cholesterol; the outer has porins.
  • Complex II does not pump protons.
  • Ubiquinone is in the bilayer; cytochrome c in the intermembrane space.
  • Uncouplers keep electron transport going but stop ATP synthesis.
  • mtDNA codes 13 proteins; the rest are nuclear.
  • LHON involves Complex I.

Key terms

Cristae
Folds of the inner membrane holding respiratory machinery.
Cristae junctions
Tubes joining cristae to the inner boundary membrane.
Porin
Channel protein of the outer membrane.
Cardiolipin
Diphosphatidylglycerol of the inner membrane.
Matrix
Gel-like interior of the mitochondrion.
Aerobic
Requiring oxygen.
Fermentation
Anaerobic conversion of pyruvate to lactate or ethanol.
Proton-motive force
Energy stored in the H⁺ gradient.
Chemiosmosis
Using the H⁺ gradient to make ATP.
Uncoupler
Agent that dissipates the gradient without ATP synthesis.
Brown fat
Fat that turns oxidation energy into heat.
