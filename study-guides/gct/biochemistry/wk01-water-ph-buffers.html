Water, Acids, Bases and Buffers

Where the body’s water is, how water and salts move between compartments, what pH means, and how buffers, lungs and kidneys keep blood pH between 7.35 and 7.45.

Labobo topic: Biochem Wk 1: Water, pH & Buffers

In 60 seconds

  • About two-thirds of body water is inside cells (ICF) and one-third outside (ECF).
  • Water is a dipole (δ– oxygen, δ+ hydrogens), so it forms hydrogen bonds and dissolves polar molecules and salts.
  • ECF is rich in Na⁺ and Cl⁻; ICF is rich in K⁺ and phosphate. The Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase keeps it that way.
  • Water moves from low osmolality to high osmolality.
  • pH = –log₁₀[H⁺]. Normal blood pH is 7.4 (range 7.35–7.45).
  • The main blood buffer is bicarbonate (pKa 6.1); lungs remove CO₂ fast, kidneys handle H⁺ and HCO₃⁻ slowly.
  • Four disorders: respiratory and metabolic acidosis, respiratory and metabolic alkalosis. Acidosis pushes K⁺ out of cells (hyperkalemia).

Learning objectives

From the syllabus for Week 1 (Marks’ Basic Medical Biochemistry, 6th ed., Chapter 4, pages 83–98). By the end you can:

Body water compartments

Biochemistry starts with a simple question: what is the normal body made of? The biggest answer is water. Almost two-thirds of an adult’s body weight is water.

That water sits in separate spaces called compartments, divided by cell membranes and capillary walls.

Where total body water is
CompartmentShare of total body waterWhat it includes
Intracellular fluid (ICF)About ⅔Water inside all cells
Extracellular fluid (ECF)About ⅓Plasma (inside vessels) and interstitial fluid (between cells)
Transcellular waterA small part of ECFFluid in special epithelium-lined cavities: cerebrospinal fluid (brain and spinal cord), ocular fluid (eyes), synovial fluid (joints)

Water as the solvent of life

Structure: a dipole

A water molecule (H₂O) is one oxygen atom joined to two hydrogen atoms by covalent bonds. Oxygen pulls the shared electrons towards itself.

  • The oxygen carries a partial negative charge (δ–).
  • Each hydrogen carries a partial positive charge (δ+).
  • So the molecule has a negative end and a positive end: an electrical dipole.

Two kinds of bond

BondWhereStrength
Covalent O–H bondWithin one water molecule (intramolecular)Strong
Hydrogen bondBetween two water molecules (intermolecular): the δ+ H of one molecule is attracted to the δ– O of anotherWeak, but very many of them

Why water dissolves so much

Because water is dipolar, it forms hydrogen bonds and electrostatic interactions with other charged or polar substances. That is why polar organic molecules (such as glucose) and inorganic salts (such as NaCl) dissolve readily in water. Nonpolar molecules (fats) cannot do this and stay apart from water.

Water and temperature control

The syllabus also lists “water and thermal regulation”. From the textbook chapter: the many hydrogen bonds mean water absorbs a lot of heat before its temperature rises (high heat capacity), and it takes a lot of heat to evaporate it. So body water buffers temperature changes, and evaporating sweat carries heat away from the skin.

Electrolytes and ion transport

Electrolytes is the general name for bicarbonate and the inorganic anions and cations dissolved in body fluids. Both ECF and ICF contain them, but they are unevenly distributed.

CompartmentMain cationMain anion
ECF (plasma and interstitial fluid)Na⁺Cl⁻
ICF (inside cells)K⁺Phosphates such as HPO₄²⁻

How the difference is kept

  • Water diffuses freely across most cell membranes.
  • Ions and neutral molecules cannot move freely; their movement is restricted to channels and transporters.
  • The Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase (the sodium pump) uses ATP to keep Na⁺ high outside and K⁺ high inside.
In the clinic: digoxin

Digoxin, a heart drug, inhibits the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase. Na⁺ builds up inside heart muscle cells. With less Na⁺ gradient, the cell pushes out less calcium, so more Ca²⁺ stays inside and the heart contracts more strongly. This stronger contraction is the drug’s positive inotropic effect.

The anion gap

Blood is electrically neutral: total positive charge equals total negative charge. But a routine blood test measures only some ions. The anion gap (AG) is the difference between the measured cations and the measured anions. It stands for the anions we don’t measure (proteins such as albumin, phosphate, sulfate, organic acids).

Plasma ions in the lecture
IonTypical plasma concentration
All cations togetherAbout 150 mmol/L
Sodium (Na⁺)About 140 mmol/L
Potassium (K⁺)About 4 mmol/L
Chloride (Cl⁻)About 100 mmol/L
Bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻)About 25 mmol/L

The lecture gives two versions of the formula:

  • With potassium: AG = (Na⁺ + K⁺) – (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻). With the numbers above: (140 + 4) – (100 + 25) = 19 mmol/L.
  • Without potassium (used in the metabolic acidosis slide): AG = Na⁺ – (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻). Normal range 8–12 mEq/L.

For a monovalent ion such as Na⁺ or Cl⁻, 1 mmol/L = 1 mEq/L, so the units are interchangeable here.

Osmolality and water movement

  • Osmolality is the total concentration of all dissolved particles (ions, organic metabolites such as glucose and urea, and proteins) in a fluid. Unit: milliosmoles per kilogram of water (mOsm/kg).
  • Water distributes between compartments according to osmolality.
  • Water moves from the side with lower osmolality (fewer solutes) to the side with higher osmolality (more solutes), until both sides are equal.
  • Osmotic pressure is the minimum pressure needed to stop pure solvent flowing into a solution through a semipermeable membrane. The more solute, the higher the osmotic pressure.

When ECF osmolality changes

ECF osmolalityWater movesEffect on cells
Rises (e.g. high plasma Na⁺)Out of cells into ECFCells shrink: cellular dehydration
Falls (e.g. low plasma Na⁺)From ECF into cellsCells swell: cell edema
Memory hook

“Water follows solute.” Water always goes towards the side that has more dissolved particles.

Regulating water and salt: RAAS

The renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) controls salt and water balance. When blood pressure or blood volume falls, the kidney releases renin; this leads to angiotensin II, which makes the adrenal cortex release aldosterone. Aldosterone makes the kidney keep Na⁺ (and with it water) and lose K⁺ and H⁺.

TypeCauseHow common
Primary hyperaldosteronismAbnormal activity of the adrenal gland itself, e.g. a single adrenal tumour (adenoma) = Conn’s syndromeRare
Secondary hyperaldosteronismToo much renin drives the adrenal to make aldosteroneMore common

pH, acids and bases

What pH means

pH = –log₁₀[H⁺], with [H⁺] in moles per litre. pH is usually a positive number between 0 and 14. Because of the minus sign, more H⁺ means a lower pH (more acidic). Because it is a log scale, one pH unit is a tenfold change in [H⁺].

Body fluids differ in pH

Blood pH is 7.4 and is kept within a narrow range of 7.35–7.45. Outside this range, medical problems follow: below 7.35 is acidemia, above 7.45 is alkalemia. Other body fluids have their own pH (the slide shows a chart): for example, stomach juice is strongly acidic and pancreatic juice is alkaline.

Acids and bases

TermMeaningExamples from the slides
AcidReleases H⁺ when dissolved in waterHydrochloric acid (HCl), sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄), acetic acid (CH₃COOH)
Strong acidCompletely dissociates in water, releasing all its H⁺; high KaHCl, H₂SO₄
Weak acidOnly partly dissociates; only a small fraction of molecules release H⁺; low KaAcetic acid, carbonic acid
BaseReleases OH⁻ (or takes up H⁺) in waterSodium hydroxide (NaOH), potassium hydroxide (KOH), ammonia (NH₃)

A weak acid HA (the conjugate acid) dissociates into H⁺ and A⁻ (the conjugate base): HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻. Ka is the dissociation constant: the bigger Ka, the stronger the acid.

Buffers

A buffer is a solution of a weak acid and its conjugate base (or a weak base and its conjugate acid). It keeps the pH change small when a little strong acid or strong base is added.

  • Add acid → the buffer’s base part takes up the extra H⁺.
  • Add base → the buffer’s acid part releases H⁺, which neutralises the OH⁻.

The bicarbonate buffer system

The most important buffer in blood and ECF:

CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻

SituationWhat happens
pH too low (too much H⁺)H⁺ binds HCO₃⁻ to form H₂CO₃ (which can leave as CO₂ through the lungs)
pH too high (too little H⁺)H₂CO₃ dissociates into H⁺ and HCO₃⁻, releasing H⁺

The first step, CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃, is sped up by the enzyme carbonic anhydrase, found especially in red blood cells and kidney tubule cells.

The hemoglobin buffer in red blood cells

  1. In the tissues, cells produce CO₂.
  2. CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻.
  3. Hemoglobin binds the H⁺ (forming HHb), so the blood does not become too acidic.
  4. At the same time, hemoglobin releases O₂ to the tissues.

So hemoglobin has two jobs at once: it carries oxygen and it buffers H⁺. In the lungs the process reverses: Hb takes up O₂, releases H⁺, and the H⁺ combines with HCO₃⁻ to make CO₂, which is breathed out.

Intracellular pH

Inside cells, the main buffers are phosphate (HPO₄²⁻/H₂PO₄⁻) and proteins, because these are what cells contain most. Intracellular pH is kept slightly lower than blood pH.

Henderson–Hasselbalch and pKa

The Henderson–Hasselbalch equation links pH to the ratio of base to acid in a buffer:

pH = pKa + log ( [A⁻] / [HA] )

  • [A⁻] = concentration of the conjugate base.
  • [HA] = concentration of the weak acid.
  • pKa = –log Ka. It measures how strong an acid is: lower pKa = stronger acid.
  • pKa is the pH at which the acid is exactly half dissociated: 50% HA and 50% A⁻. Then [A⁻]/[HA] = 1, log 1 = 0, so pH = pKa.

For blood

For the bicarbonate buffer, pKa = 6.1, and the acid part is represented by dissolved CO₂ (0.03 × PCO₂ in mmHg). This lets you calculate pH from HCO₃⁻ and PCO₂:

pH = 6.1 + log ( [HCO₃⁻] / (0.03 × PCO₂) )

Worked example with normal values (HCO₃⁻ 24 mmol/L, PCO₂ 40 mmHg): 0.03 × 40 = 1.2; 24 / 1.2 = 20; log 20 ≈ 1.3; pH = 6.1 + 1.3 = 7.4.

Key idea

Blood pH depends on the ratio of HCO₃⁻ (controlled by the kidneys) to CO₂ (controlled by the lungs). Normal ratio ≈ 20 : 1. Anything that changes this ratio changes pH.

Lungs and kidneys

Respiratory buffering (the slide is a diagram)

  • CO₂ is an acid-forming gas: more CO₂ means more H₂CO₃, more H⁺, lower pH.
  • Breathing faster and deeper (hyperventilation) blows off CO₂ → pH rises.
  • Breathing slower (hypoventilation) keeps CO₂ → pH falls.
  • The respiratory centre responds within minutes.

Renal mechanisms

The kidneys have two main tools: they reabsorb HCO₃⁻ from the urine back into the blood, and they secrete H⁺ into the urine. They act more slowly (hours to days) but can truly remove acid from the body.

StateKidney response
AcidosisSecrete more H⁺ into urine (carried as ammonium NH₄⁺ and phosphate), reabsorb and make more HCO₃⁻ → urine becomes more acidic
AlkalosisSecrete less H⁺, reabsorb less HCO₃⁻ and excrete it → urine becomes more alkaline

Acid–base disorders

There are four primary disorders. The name tells you two things: the direction of pH (acidosis or alkalosis), and what changed first (respiratory = PCO₂, metabolic = HCO₃⁻). The body then compensates with the other system to bring the ratio back towards 20 : 1.

Most of these slides are diagrams; the details below follow the textbook chapter the syllabus names.

The four disorders at a glance
DisorderpHPrimary changeCompensationTypical causes
Respiratory acidosis↓↑ PCO₂ (CO₂ is not eliminated)Kidneys keep more HCO₃⁻ (↑ HCO₃⁻), over daysHypoventilation: chronic lung disease (COPD), airway obstruction, drugs that depress breathing (opioids, sedatives), weak breathing muscles
Respiratory alkalosis↑↓ PCO₂Kidneys excrete HCO₃⁻ (↓ HCO₃⁻)Hyperventilation: anxiety or panic, high altitude, fever, early aspirin (salicylate) overdose
Metabolic acidosis↓↓ HCO₃⁻Hyperventilation lowers PCO₂ (fast)Ketoacidosis, lactic acidosis, kidney failure, poisons, diarrhea
Metabolic alkalosis↑↑ HCO₃⁻Hypoventilation raises PCO₂Vomiting (loss of stomach HCl), diuretics, hyperaldosteronism, too much antacid or bicarbonate

Metabolic acidosis in detail

Metabolic acidosis is a primary fall in plasma bicarbonate, which lowers extracellular pH. Its causes fall into three groups:

  1. More acid produced: e.g. ketone bodies in diabetic ketoacidosis, lactate in shock.
  2. Less acid eliminated: e.g. kidney failure.
  3. Bicarbonate lost: e.g. severe diarrhea.

The anion gap splits metabolic acidosis into two kinds:

TypeWhyExamples
High anion gap (above 8–12 mEq/L)Unmeasured organic acid anions are added; HCO₃⁻ falls with no rise in Cl⁻Lactate, ketones, oxalic acid, formic acid, glycolic acid (the last three from poisons such as ethylene glycol or methanol)
Normal anion gap (hyperchloremic)HCO₃⁻ is lost and replaced by Cl⁻, so the gap stays normalDiarrhea, some kidney tubule disorders

Compensation: hyperventilation removes more CO₂ and lowers PCO₂, which pulls pH back up.

In the clinic

A patient with uncontrolled type 1 diabetes breathes deeply and fast. Ketone bodies (acids) have used up bicarbonate (high anion gap metabolic acidosis), and the deep breathing is the lungs’ compensation, blowing off CO₂.

In the clinic

Repeated vomiting removes stomach HCl. The body loses H⁺ and Cl⁻ and is left with extra HCO₃⁻: metabolic alkalosis.

Potassium shifts in pH disorders

K⁺ is mainly inside cells. When blood pH changes, H⁺ and K⁺ swap places across the cell membrane to keep the charges balanced (electroneutrality). The shift is temporary.

StateH⁺ movesK⁺ movesResult
Acidosis (↑ H⁺ in blood)Into cellsOut of cellsHyperkalemia
Alkalosis (↓ H⁺ in blood)Out of cellsInto cellsHypokalemia
Memory hook

K⁺ and H⁺ move in opposite directions, so blood K⁺ follows blood H⁺: acidosis → high K⁺, alkalosis → low K⁺.

Watch out

Watch out: bonds in water

Slide 5 says “Covalent bonds intermolecular”. That is the wrong way round. Covalent bonds are intramolecular (the O–H bonds inside one molecule). Hydrogen bonds are intermolecular (between molecules). Use this in the exam.

Watch out: “aceto acetic acid”

Slide 15 lists “Aceto acetic acid (CH₃COOH)”. CH₃COOH is acetic acid, a weak acid. Acetoacetic acid is a different molecule (CH₃COCH₂COOH), one of the ketone bodies.

Watch out: two anion gap formulas

Slide 9 includes K⁺ in the formula; slide 26 uses Na⁺ only and gives the normal range 8–12 mEq/L. The 8–12 range belongs to the formula without K⁺. If a question gives K⁺ and asks for the gap “with potassium”, add it; otherwise use Na⁺ – (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻).

Exam traps

Exam trap
  • “Water moves towards lower osmolality” is false: it moves towards higher osmolality.
  • A higher [H⁺] means a lower pH, not higher.
  • At pH = pKa the acid is half dissociated, not fully.
  • Digoxin inhibits the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase; it does not stimulate it.
  • In metabolic acidosis the lungs compensate by hyperventilating, not hypoventilating.
  • Acidosis causes hyperkalemia, alkalosis hypokalemia.
  • Conn’s syndrome is primary hyperaldosteronism.

Check yourself

  1. About what fraction of total body water is in the intracellular fluid?

    1. One-tenth
    2. One-third
    3. Two-thirds
    4. Nine-tenths
    Show answer

    C. About two-thirds is ICF and one-third ECF.

  2. Which pair are the major electrolytes inside cells?

    1. Na⁺ and Cl⁻
    2. K⁺ and phosphate
    3. Ca²⁺ and HCO₃⁻
    4. Na⁺ and HCO₃⁻
    Show answer

    B. K⁺ and phosphates (e.g. HPO₄²⁻) dominate the ICF; Na⁺ and Cl⁻ dominate the ECF.

  3. Digoxin produces a positive inotropic effect because it:

    1. Activates the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase
    2. Blocks K⁺ channels
    3. Inhibits the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, so less Ca²⁺ is pushed out of the cell
    4. Increases Ca²⁺ excretion by the kidney
    Show answer

    C. Intracellular Na⁺ rises, Ca²⁺ extrusion falls, and more Ca²⁺ strengthens contraction.

  4. If ECF osmolality rises, what happens to cells?

    1. Water enters them and they swell
    2. Water leaves them and they shrink
    3. Nothing, because water cannot cross membranes
    4. They take up Na⁺ to balance it
    Show answer

    B. Water moves towards higher osmolality, out of the cells: cellular dehydration.

  5. Na⁺ 140, Cl⁻ 104 and HCO₃⁻ 24 mEq/L. What is the anion gap, and is it normal?

    1. 12, normal
    2. 20, high
    3. 36, high
    4. 4, low
    Show answer

    A. 140 – (104 + 24) = 12 mEq/L, within 8–12.

  6. An acid has a pKa of 6.1. At pH 6.1, the acid is:

    1. Fully dissociated
    2. Not dissociated at all
    3. 50% dissociated
    4. 90% dissociated
    Show answer

    C. pKa is the pH at which an acid is half dissociated: 50% HA, 50% A⁻.

  7. What does the bicarbonate buffer do when blood pH falls?

    1. H₂CO₃ releases more H⁺
    2. HCO₃⁻ binds H⁺ to form H₂CO₃
    3. Hemoglobin releases H⁺
    4. The kidneys excrete HCO₃⁻
    Show answer

    B. At low pH, HCO₃⁻ takes up the extra H⁺; the H₂CO₃ formed can leave as CO₂.

  8. In the tissues, hemoglobin acts as a buffer by:

    1. Binding H⁺ while releasing O₂
    2. Releasing H⁺ while binding O₂
    3. Binding Cl⁻
    4. Making HCO₃⁻ directly
    Show answer

    A. Hb binds H⁺ (HHb) produced from CO₂ and at the same time gives O₂ to the tissues.

  9. Which finding fits metabolic acidosis with respiratory compensation?

    1. ↓ HCO₃⁻ and ↓ PCO₂
    2. ↑ HCO₃⁻ and ↑ PCO₂
    3. ↑ PCO₂ and ↑ HCO₃⁻
    4. ↓ PCO₂ and normal HCO₃⁻
    Show answer

    A. HCO₃⁻ falls first; hyperventilation then lowers PCO₂.

  10. Which is a cause of a high anion gap metabolic acidosis?

    1. Severe diarrhea
    2. Repeated vomiting
    3. Diabetic ketoacidosis
    4. Hyperventilation from anxiety
    Show answer

    C. Ketone body anions are unmeasured anions. Diarrhea gives a normal-gap acidosis; vomiting gives alkalosis; anxiety gives respiratory alkalosis.

  11. A patient is in acidosis. Which change in plasma potassium do you expect?

    1. Hypokalemia, as K⁺ enters cells
    2. Hyperkalemia, as K⁺ leaves cells
    3. No change
    4. Hypokalemia, as K⁺ is lost in sweat
    Show answer

    B. H⁺ moves into cells and K⁺ moves out to keep electroneutrality.

  12. Conn’s syndrome is:

    1. Secondary hyperaldosteronism from high renin
    2. Primary hyperaldosteronism from an adrenal adenoma
    3. A lack of aldosterone
    4. A kidney tumour making renin
    Show answer

    B. A single adrenal adenoma causes primary hyperaldosteronism, which is rare.

Key terms

ICF / ECF
Intracellular fluid (inside cells) / extracellular fluid (plasma and interstitial fluid).
Transcellular water
Fluid in epithelium-lined cavities: CSF, eye fluid, synovial fluid.
Dipole
A molecule with a partly negative end and a partly positive end, like water.
Hydrogen bond
A weak attraction between a δ+ hydrogen and a δ– atom such as oxygen on another molecule.
Electrolytes
Bicarbonate and the inorganic anions and cations of body fluids.
Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase
Membrane pump using ATP to keep Na⁺ outside and K⁺ inside cells.
Anion gap
Measured cations minus measured anions; reflects unmeasured anions.
Osmolality
Total concentration of dissolved particles, in mOsm per kg of water.
Osmotic pressure
The minimum pressure that stops pure solvent flowing into a solution across a semipermeable membrane.
Aldosterone
Adrenal hormone that makes the kidney keep Na⁺ and lose K⁺ and H⁺.
pH
The negative log₁₀ of the hydrogen ion concentration in mol/L.
Ka / pKa
Acid dissociation constant / its negative log; pKa is the pH at which an acid is half dissociated.
Conjugate base
What is left of an acid after it gives up H⁺ (A⁻ from HA).
Buffer
A weak acid plus its conjugate base that keeps pH nearly steady when acid or base is added.
Carbonic anhydrase
Enzyme that speeds up CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃.
Acidosis / alkalosis
A process that lowers / raises blood pH.
Compensation
The other system (lungs or kidneys) changing to bring pH back towards normal.
Hyperkalemia / hypokalemia
High / low plasma potassium.
