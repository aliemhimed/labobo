Enzymes: Kinetics, Inhibition, Regulation and Clinical Use

What enzymes are, the six classes, how they lower activation energy, the Michaelis–Menten curve, how inhibitors change Km and Vmax, how cells switch enzymes on and off, and the enzymes doctors measure in blood (LDH, CK).

Labobo topics: Biochem Wk 10 and Wk 11

In 60 seconds

  • Enzymes are biological catalysts: they speed reactions and are not consumed. Catalytic RNAs are ribozymes.
  • Six classes: Oxidoreductases, Transferases, Hydrolases, Lyases, Isomerases, Ligases.
  • Enzymes lower the activation energy by stabilising the transition state; they don’t change ΔG of the reaction.
  • Km = [S] at ½ Vmax. [S] ≪ Km → first order; [S] ≫ Km → zero order.
  • Competitive: Vmax same, Km ↑. Noncompetitive: Vmax ↓, Km same. Irreversible: covalent, permanent.
  • Regulation: allosteric effectors, covalent modification (phosphorylation of Ser/Thr/Tyr), and changing enzyme amount (induction/repression).
  • LDH1 = H4 (heart, RBC); LDH5 = M4 (liver, muscle). CK-MB rises 4–8 h after MI, peaks at 24 h, normal in 2–3 days.

What an enzyme is

  • Enzymes are biological catalysts: they accelerate reactions but are not consumed or changed by them.
  • They take part in every process essential for life: DNA replication and transcription, protein synthesis, metabolism, signal transduction.
  • Most enzymes are proteins. Some RNAs can also catalyse reactions on phosphodiester or peptide bonds; these are ribozymes, far less common than protein enzymes.

Properties: active site, specificity, cofactors

The active site

  • A special pocket or cleft formed by the folding of the protein.
  • Its amino acid side chains take part in substrate binding and catalysis.
  • The substrate binds non-covalently, forming the enzyme–substrate (ES) complex.

Two models of binding

ModelIdea
Lock and key (Fischer)The binding site is rigid; only a substrate of exactly the right shape fits
Induced fit (Koshland)The enzyme is flexible; substrate binding changes its shape so a stable complex forms

Other properties

  • Efficiency: how fast an enzyme turns substrate into product, and how often a collision with substrate succeeds.
  • Specificity: each enzyme binds one or a very few substrates and catalyses only one type of reaction.
  • Location: most enzymes work inside cells, often in specific organelles, which keeps pathways organised and separate.

Activation energy and the transition state

  • Most reactions have an energy barrier between reactants and products: the activation energy (free energy of activation, ΔG‡).
  • It is the energy difference between the reactant and a high-energy intermediate, the transition state (T*): A ⇄ T* ⇄ B. Every molecule must pass through T* to become product.
  • The higher the activation energy, the slower the reaction.
  • An enzyme provides another pathway with a lower activation energy (“lowers the hill”), so the reaction goes faster. It does not change the energy of the reactants or products, or the equilibrium.

Substrate, temperature and pH

Enzyme speed depends on substrate concentration, pH, temperature and inhibitors. Everyday example: sliced apples turn brown by enzyme-driven oxidation; lemon juice (with vitamin C) prevents this by changing the pH around the enzyme.

FactorEffect
Substrate concentrationVelocity (v = substrate converted per unit time) rises with [S] until the enzyme is saturated: then Vmax is reached and more substrate does nothing (at constant enzyme)
TemperatureVelocity rises with temperature to a peak; higher still, the enzyme denatures and velocity falls (a bell-shaped curve)
pH(1) Enzyme and substrate need certain groups ionised or un-ionised to interact; (2) extreme pH denatures the enzyme. Each enzyme has an optimum pH

Michaelis–Menten kinetics

The Michaelis–Menten equation describes how velocity depends on substrate concentration. It assumes the enzyme first binds substrate (ES), then makes product. Over time [S] falls and [P] rises.

v = Vmax [S] / (Km + [S])

SymbolMeaning
vReaction rate (product formed per unit time); proportional to enzyme concentration: halve the enzyme → v and Vmax halve
VmaxMaximum rate, when all active sites are full
[S]Substrate concentration
KmMichaelis constant: [S] at which v = ½ Vmax. Low Km = high affinity for substrate

The plot of v against [S] is a hyperbola: a steep rise, then a plateau at Vmax.

Reaction order

SituationOrderRate depends on
[S] ≪ KmFirst orderv is roughly proportional to [S]
[S] ≫ Km (at Vmax)Zero orderOnly the enzyme amount; adding substrate doesn’t help. To make more product, add more enzyme

From the textbook: the Lineweaver–Burk (double-reciprocal) plot, 1/v against 1/[S], is a straight line that crosses the y-axis at 1/Vmax and the x-axis at –1/Km. It makes inhibitor effects easy to see.

Inhibition

Inhibitors reduce enzyme activity, typically by stopping the active site forming ES. Some cause temporary, others permanent loss of activity.

Regulation

Cells must coordinate many pathways, so enzyme speed is regulated. Most enzymes respond to substrate concentration; beyond that there are three main ways.

1. Allosteric enzymes

  • Regulated by effectors that bind non-covalently at a site other than the active site.
  • Negative effectors inhibit; positive effectors activate. They can change substrate affinity (Km), maximal activity (Vmax) or both.
  • Homotropic: the substrate itself is the effector, usually positive: binding at one site helps the other sites (cooperativity, giving a sigmoidal curve).
  • Heterotropic: the effector is not the substrate. Example: feedback inhibition, where a pathway’s end product inhibits an early enzyme so unneeded product doesn’t build up.

2. Covalent modification

  • Most often adding or removing phosphate on serine, threonine or tyrosine (by kinases and phosphatases).
  • One of the main ways cells regulate processes.
  • Depending on the enzyme, the phosphorylated form may be more or less active.

3. Changing the amount of enzyme

  • Cells alter the rate of enzyme degradation or, more typically, synthesis.
  • Induction (more synthesis) or repression (less) changes the total number of active sites. This is slower (hours to days).
  • Used for enzymes needed only at one stage of development or under certain conditions.

Enzymes in diagnosis: LDH and CK

Enzyme activity in serum or plasma helps diagnose disease. Blood enzymes form two groups:

  • Plasma-functional enzymes: made to work in plasma (e.g. clotting factors).
  • Non-plasma-functional enzymes: normally inside cells; they appear in blood when cells are damaged. These are the diagnostic ones.

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH)

Found in heart, liver, muscle, kidney, lung and blood cells; released when cells are damaged. A tetramer of two kinds of subunit, coded by different genes: H (mainly heart) and M (mainly skeletal muscle and liver). Five isoenzymes:

Creatine kinase (CK)

Three isoenzymes, each a dimer of B (brain) and M (muscle) subunits, each moving differently on electrophoresis:

IsoenzymeSubunitsTissue

In the clinic: myocardial infarction

After a heart attack, CK-MB appears in blood within 4–8 hours, peaks at 24 hours and returns to normal within 2–3 days. Because it clears fast, a second rise suggests a new infarct.

Watch out

Watch out: “steady state”

Slide 17 says steady state is when the enzyme works at maximum activity. In enzyme kinetics, steady state means the concentration of the ES complex stays constant (made as fast as it breaks down). Working at maximum activity is saturation (Vmax).

Watch out: activation energy vs ΔG

Slide 15 calls the activation energy “ΔG”. The barrier is the free energy of activation (ΔG‡). ΔG without the ‡ is the overall energy change between reactants and products, which an enzyme does not change.

Watch out: “solubility curve”

Slide 21 calls the v–[S] plot a “hyperbolic solubility curve”. It is a hyperbolic saturation curve; solubility has nothing to do with it.

Exam traps

Exam trap
  • Enzymes don’t change the equilibrium or ΔG; they only make it faster to get there.
  • A low Km means high affinity.
  • Halving the enzyme halves Vmax, not Km.
  • At Vmax the reaction is zero order in substrate.
  • Noncompetitive inhibition can’t be overcome by more substrate.
  • Phosphorylation can activate or inhibit, depending on the enzyme.
  • CK-MB, not CK-MM, is the heart marker.

Key terms

Enzyme
Biological catalyst, not consumed in the reaction.
Ribozyme
Catalytic RNA.
Active site
Pocket where substrate binds and catalysis happens.
ES complex
Enzyme bound non-covalently to its substrate.
Induced fit
Enzyme changes shape when substrate binds.
Holoenzyme / apoenzyme
Active complete enzyme / inactive protein part.
Cofactor / coenzyme
Metal ion / organic helper molecule.
Prosthetic group
Coenzyme permanently bound to the enzyme (e.g. FAD).
Transition state
High-energy intermediate between reactant and product.
Activation energy
Energy barrier to reach the transition state.
Vmax
Maximum velocity when all active sites are full.
Km
[S] at half Vmax.
Zero-order kinetics
Rate independent of substrate concentration.
Allosteric effector
Molecule binding outside the active site to change activity.
Feedback inhibition
End product inhibiting an early enzyme of its pathway.
Induction / repression
Increase / decrease of enzyme synthesis.
Isoenzymes
Different forms of one enzyme in different tissues (LDH, CK).
