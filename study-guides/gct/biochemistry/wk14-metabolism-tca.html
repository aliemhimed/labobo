Overview of Metabolism, Pyruvate Dehydrogenase and the TCA Cycle

How pathways are organised, how pyruvate becomes acetyl-CoA, the eight steps of the citric acid cycle, how much energy it gives, how it is controlled, and what happens when PDH or fumarase is missing.

Labobo topic: Biochem Wk 14: Metabolism and TCA Cycle

In 60 seconds

  • Catabolic pathways break down; anabolic pathways build and use energy.
  • Cellular respiration: glycolysis (cytoplasm) → pyruvate oxidation and TCA cycle (mitochondrial matrix) → electron transport chain.
  • PDH complex (E1, E2, E3) needs 5 cofactors: TPP (B1), lipoic acid, CoA (B5), FAD (B2), NAD⁺ (B3).
  • PDH is switched off by PDH kinase (high energy) and on by PDH phosphatase (low energy, Ca²⁺).
  • TCA: citrate → isocitrate → α-ketoglutarate → succinyl-CoA → succinate → fumarate → malate → oxaloacetate.
  • Per acetyl-CoA: 3 NADH, 1 FADH₂, 1 GTP, 2 CO₂ = 12 ATP (lecture value).
  • Succinate dehydrogenase is the only TCA enzyme in the inner membrane: it is also Complex II. Give thiamine before glucose in alcoholics.

Cellular respiration

Cellular respiration turns glucose into usable energy, ATP, for cell function, growth and maintenance. Three stages:

StageWhat happensWhere
1. GlycolysisGlucose (6 C) → 2 pyruvate (3 C each), making ATP and NADHCytoplasm of all cells
Link: pyruvate oxidationPyruvate → acetyl-CoA by the PDH complexMitochondrial matrix
2. TCA cycleAcetyl-CoA is oxidised to CO₂, making NADH, FADH₂, GTPMitochondrial matrix
3. Electron transport chainNADH and FADH₂ give electrons → ATP generationInner mitochondrial membrane

Pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDH)

Pyruvate oxidation links glycolysis to the TCA cycle and is essential for aerobic respiration. It happens in the mitochondrial matrix, catalysed by a large multi-enzyme complex:

Pyruvate + CoA + NAD⁺ → acetyl-CoA + CO₂ + NADH

EnzymeCofactorWhat it does
E1: pyruvate dehydrogenaseTPP (thiamine)Decarboxylates pyruvate → hydroxyethyl-TPP; CO₂ released
E2: dihydrolipoyl transacetylaseLipoamide, CoAMoves the hydroxyethyl group to lipoamide, oxidising it to an acetyl group (acetyl-lipoamide); passes the acetyl to CoA → acetyl-CoA
E3: dihydrolipoyl dehydrogenaseFAD, NAD⁺Re-oxidises lipoamide: FAD → FADH₂, then electrons go to NAD⁺ → NADH

TPP, lipoic acid and FAD are tightly bound prosthetic groups. Lipoic acid is a sulfur-containing cofactor of oxidative decarboxylations, covalently bound to lysine residues.

PDH deficiency, thiamine and arsenic

Pyruvate dehydrogenase deficiency

  • Inherited; most often an E1 defect (from the textbook: the E1α gene is X-linked).
  • Pyruvate is not converted to acetyl-CoA → less carbon enters the TCA cycle → less ATP; pyruvate is turned into lactate instead (lactic acidosis), and the brain suffers most.
  • No proven cure. Helpful: restrict carbohydrate, give ketogenic nutrients (fat, lysine and leucine, which give acetyl-CoA without PDH), and thiamine.
In the clinic: thiamine before glucose

Alcoholism commonly causes thiamine deficiency (poor intake, poor absorption, poor liver storage and activation, more urinary loss). Give thiamine before glucose: glucose given to a thiamine-deficient patient floods a PDH that can’t work → pyruvate builds up → severe lactic acidosis (and can precipitate Wernicke encephalopathy).

In the clinic: arsenic poisoning

Arsenite binds lipoic acid, blocking every lipoic-acid enzyme: PDH, α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase and branched-chain α-keto acid dehydrogenase. Pyruvate and lactate build up; the brain is mainly affected. Clues: garlic-like breath, vomiting and diarrhea.

Acetyl-CoA and anaplerosis

Sources of acetyl-CoAFates of acetyl-CoA
Pyruvate (from glucose) via PDHTCA cycle → CO₂ + energy
Fatty acid β-oxidationFatty acid synthesis
Ketogenic amino acids (leucine, lysine…)Cholesterol synthesis
Ketone bodies (used by tissues)Ketone body synthesis (liver)
  • The TCA cycle happens in the mitochondrial matrix: acetyl-CoA is oxidised to CO₂, making NADH, FADH₂ and GTP (ATP).
  • Acetyl-CoA’s two carbons leave as two CO₂, so there is no net gain of intermediates. That is why acetyl-CoA cannot make oxaloacetate and cannot be used for gluconeogenesis directly.
  • But acetyl-CoA activates pyruvate carboxylase (pyruvate → oxaloacetate), which indirectly promotes gluconeogenesis.
  • Topping up cycle intermediates like this is called anaplerosis.

The eight steps of the TCA cycle

StepReactionEnzymeMade
1Acetyl-CoA (2C) + oxaloacetate (4C) → citrate (6C), CoA releasedCitrate synthase—
2Citrate → isocitrate (remove then add water; why some count 9 steps)Aconitase—
3Isocitrate → α-ketoglutarate (5C): first oxidative decarboxylationIsocitrate dehydrogenaseNADH, CO₂
4α-Ketoglutarate → succinyl-CoA (4C): second oxidative decarboxylationα-Ketoglutarate dehydrogenaseNADH, CO₂
5Succinyl-CoA → succinate: substrate-level phosphorylationSuccinyl-CoA synthetase (succinate thiokinase)GTP (→ ATP by nucleoside diphosphate kinase)
6Succinate → fumarateSuccinate dehydrogenase (= Complex II)FADH₂
7Fumarate + H₂O → malate (hydration)Fumarase—
8Malate → oxaloacetate (regenerated)Malate dehydrogenaseNADH
  • Citrate signals high energy: it inhibits glycolysis (PFK-1) and stimulates fatty acid synthesis.
  • Fumarate is also made by the urea cycle, purine synthesis (AMP from adenylosuccinate) and breakdown of phenylalanine and tyrosine.
  • Succinate dehydrogenase is the only TCA enzyme in the inner mitochondrial membrane, so it is also Complex II of the ETC.

TCA regulation

The slide is a diagram; from Lippincott, the cycle is controlled at three enzymes, mainly by the cell’s energy state:

EnzymeActivated byInhibited by
Citrate synthase—Citrate (its product)
Isocitrate dehydrogenase (main control point)ADP, Ca²⁺ATP, NADH
α-Ketoglutarate dehydrogenaseCa²⁺Succinyl-CoA, NADH

Simple rule: plenty of ATP and NADH slows the cycle; ADP and Ca²⁺ (exercise) speed it up.

Fumarase deficiency

Listed in the syllabus but not on the slides; from the textbook:

  • A rare, autosomal recessive defect of fumarase (fumarate → malate).
  • Fumarate builds up and is lost in the urine (fumaric aciduria).
  • Severe neurological problems from infancy: developmental delay, seizures, poor muscle tone.

Watch out

Watch out: 12 ATP or 10?

The lecture uses the classic values (3 ATP per NADH, 2 per FADH₂ → 12 ATP per acetyl-CoA). Newer textbooks use 2.5 and 1.5, giving 10 ATP. For this course’s exam, use 12 unless the question says otherwise.

Watch out: E2 oxidises the group

Slide 8 says E2 transfers the hydroxyethyl group and oxidises it to acetyl. Correct: the hydroxyethyl group is oxidised as it is moved onto lipoamide (lipoamide is reduced). E3 then re-oxidises lipoamide.

Exam traps

Exam trap
  • Glycolysis is in the cytoplasm; PDH and TCA in the matrix.
  • Acetyl-CoA cannot make net glucose; it only activates pyruvate carboxylase.
  • Lipoic acid is bound to lysine; arsenic attacks it.
  • The only GTP-making step is succinyl-CoA → succinate.
  • FADH₂ comes from succinate dehydrogenase; all other oxidations make NADH.
  • In PDH deficiency, lysine and leucine are given because they are ketogenic.

Key terms

Metabolic pathway
A series of enzyme steps from one molecule to a product.
Catabolism / anabolism
Breaking down / building up molecules.
PDH complex
Three-enzyme complex turning pyruvate into acetyl-CoA.
TPP
Thiamine pyrophosphate, cofactor of E1.
Lipoic acid
Sulfur cofactor of E2, bound to lysine.
Anaplerosis
Refilling TCA intermediates, e.g. by pyruvate carboxylase.
Oxidative decarboxylation
Removal of CO₂ coupled to oxidation (making NADH).
Substrate-level phosphorylation
Making ATP/GTP directly in a reaction, not via the ETC.
Complex II
Succinate dehydrogenase in the ETC.
PDH deficiency
Inherited block of pyruvate → acetyl-CoA, causing lactic acidosis.
Fumarase deficiency
Inherited block of fumarate → malate, causing fumaric aciduria.
