Microscopy

The five kinds of light microscope (bright-field, fluorescence, phase-contrast, confocal, polarizing), resolving power, the two electron microscopes (TEM and SEM), and how to interpret sections and their artifacts.

Labobo topic: Histo Lec 2: Microscopy

In 60 seconds

  • Light microscopes: bright-field, fluorescence, phase-contrast, confocal, polarizing. All use light interacting with tissue.
  • Resolving power = smallest distance at which two objects are still seen as separate. Light microscope: 0.2 μm, useful magnification 1000–1500×. A ribosome or microfilament is too small to see.
  • Fluorescence: light in, longer wavelength out (e.g. acridine orange for DNA and RNA).
  • Phase-contrast: turns invisible phase shifts (refractive index differences) into brightness differences: living, unstained cells.
  • Confocal: laser point + pinhole removes out-of-focus light → sharp optical slices, 3-D.
  • Polarizing: polarized light shows ordered, birefringent structures (crystals, collagen, muscle).
  • Electron microscopes: electron wavelength is much shorter → ~1000× better resolution. TEM = through ultra-thin sections (inside); SEM = scans the surface (3-D look).
  • Artifacts (e.g. shrinkage spaces, wrinkles, stain precipitate) are not in living tissue. A section is a 2-D slice of a 3-D structure.

Learning objectives

  • name the light microscope types and the principle of each;
  • define resolving power and give the limit of the light microscope;
  • explain why phase-contrast is used for living cells;
  • compare TEM and SEM;
  • recognise common artifacts and the limits of a 2-D section.

Bright-field microscopy and resolving power

  • The most common light microscope: light is transmitted through the specimen, seen against a bright background. Works because of contrast between the specimen and its surroundings, so it suits stained or pigmented samples.
  • The key to a crisp image is resolving power (resolution): the smallest distance between two structures at which they are still seen as two separate objects.
  • Maximum resolving power of the light microscope ≈ 0.2 μm, giving clear images magnified 1000–1500 times. Magnifying more just makes a bigger blur (“empty magnification”).
  • Anything smaller than 0.2 μm (a single ribosome, a microfilament) cannot be distinguished.
  • Virtual microscopy: a stained slide is scanned into high-resolution digital images, studied on a computer without a microscope or the glass slide.
Key idea: resolution vs magnification

Magnification makes things bigger; resolution makes them clearer (separate). Better resolution = a smaller resolvable distance.

Fluorescence microscopy

  • The specimen is lit with a specific wavelength (often ultraviolet or blue); fluorescent molecules absorb it and emit light of a longer wavelength, which is what we see (bright on a dark background).
  • Fluorescent dyes or tags (e.g. fluorescent antibodies) make specific structures or molecules glow.
  • Example: acridine orange binds both DNA and RNA; they fluoresce slightly different colours, so they can be located separately.
  • Used in cell biology, pathology and biochemistry.

Phase-contrast microscopy

The problem

  • Cells are mostly water and very transparent. In bright-field, light passes through with little absorption: poor contrast, a faint image.
  • But light slows down slightly as it passes structures with different refractive indices (nucleus, organelles, cytoplasm). This causes a phase shift in the light waves.
  • Our eyes cannot see phase shifts, only differences in intensity (brightness).

The solution

  • The phase-contrast system converts phase shifts into brightness differences, so structures look lighter or darker than each other.
  • No staining is needed, so it is ideal for living cells, tissue culture and microorganisms, and for watching dynamic processes.

Confocal microscopy

  • A laser lights one small point of the sample at a time.
  • The emitted fluorescence or reflected light passes through a pinhole that blocks out-of-focus light from above and below the focal plane.
  • Result: better contrast and resolution; a series of thin “optical sections” can be stacked into a 3-D image.
  • Used for cellular structures, tissues and real-time processes.

Polarizing microscopy

  • Filters make polarized light (waves vibrating in one plane) that passes through the sample.
  • Structures with highly ordered, repeating molecules rotate polarized light: birefringence. They shine brightly on a dark background.
  • Reveals orientation, birefringence and texture: crystals, collagen bundles, skeletal muscle striations, also used in geology and materials science.

Electron microscopy: TEM and SEM

  • Both use a beam of electrons interacting with tissue.
  • The wavelength of electrons is much shorter than light’s → about 1000-fold better resolution than the light microscope.

Interpreting structures in sections

  • Artifacts are structural changes caused by preparation that do not exist in living tissue:
    • shrinkage from fixatives, ethanol or the heat of paraffin embedding → artificial spaces between cells;
    • wrinkles (folds) in sections;
    • stain precipitates that can look like cell structures.
  • No single stain shows all components, so several preparations must be studied.
  • TEM gives great detail but only of tiny samples.
  • A section is two-dimensional: one cut through a 3-D structure. A tube may look like a ring, an oval or a strip depending on the angle of the cut, so think in 3-D.

Exam traps

Exam trap
  • Fluorescence emits a longer wavelength than it absorbs.
  • Phase-contrast needs no stain: living cells.
  • The pinhole is the confocal microscope.
  • SEM = surface, 3-D; TEM = inside, thin sections.
  • Freeze fracture/etching is a TEM technique for membranes.
  • Spaces between cells after paraffin processing are usually shrinkage artifacts.

Key terms

Resolving power
Smallest separation at which two points look separate.
Bright-field
Transmitted light through a stained section.
Fluorescence
Emission of longer-wavelength light after absorption.
Phase-contrast
Turns phase shifts into contrast; no stain.
Confocal
Laser + pinhole optical sectioning.
Birefringence
Rotation of polarized light by ordered structures.
TEM / SEM
Transmission (internal) / scanning (surface) electron microscope.
Cryofracture
Splitting frozen cells for TEM, exposing membrane interiors.
Artifact
Change caused by preparation, absent in life.
Virtual microscopy
Studying scanned digital slides on a computer.
