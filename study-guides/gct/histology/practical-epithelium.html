Epithelium: Identifying Slides

A slide-recognition guide for the epithelium practical: how to identify each type of epithelium under the microscope, what to look for, where each is found, and how to tell look-alikes apart.

Labobo topic: Histo: Epithelium

In 60 seconds

  • Step 1: find the basement membrane and the free surface. Step 2: count layers. Step 3: look at the surface cells and their nuclei.
  • Simple squamous: one row of flat cells, nuclei bulge like eggs → transfer (vessels, alveoli, mesothelium).
  • Simple cuboidal: square cells, round central nuclei → absorption, secretion, excretion (kidney tubules, thyroid).
  • Simple columnar: tall cells, oval basal nuclei in one row → absorption, secretion (intestine, stomach, gallbladder).
  • Pseudostratified: nuclei at several levels, all cells on the basement membrane, cilia + goblet cells (trachea).
  • Stratified squamous: many layers, flat at the top; keratinised (skin; pink anucleate keratin layer) or non-keratinised (oesophagus, vagina, cornea; nuclei to the surface).
  • Stratified cuboidal: two layers of cubes (large ducts). Transitional: dome umbrella cells; flattens when stretched (bladder).

Learning objectives

  • use a step-by-step method to classify any epithelium on a slide;
  • name the function and typical locations of each type;
  • tell apart common look-alikes.

How to identify an epithelium

  1. Find the free (apical) surface facing a lumen or the outside, and the basement membrane on the connective tissue side.
  2. Count layers of nuclei between them. One row → simple. Several → stratified (or pseudostratified if every cell seems to touch the base).
  3. Look at the surface cells and nuclei: flat nuclei → squamous; round → cuboidal; oval and vertical → columnar.
  4. Check the surface for cilia, microvilli (brush border), keratin or umbrella cells.
  5. Use the organ to confirm.

Simple squamous epithelium

  • One layer of flattened cells; thin nuclei that bulge into the lumen.
  • Thinness allows transfer of gases, fluids and nutrients.
  • Where: endothelium of blood vessels, mesothelium (pleura, peritoneum), lung alveoli, Bowman’s capsule.

Simple cuboidal epithelium

  • One layer of cube-shaped cells (as tall as wide); round, central nuclei.
  • Associated with absorption, secretion or excretion.
  • Where: kidney tubules, thyroid follicles, small gland ducts, ovary surface.

Simple columnar epithelium

  • One layer of cells taller than wide; oval nuclei usually in a row near the base.
  • Associated with absorption or secretion. May have a brush border (microvilli) and goblet cells (intestine), or cilia (oviduct).
  • Where: stomach, small and large intestine, gallbladder, oviduct, collecting ducts.

Pseudostratified columnar epithelium

  • Looks stratified because nuclei are at different levels.
  • But every cell touches the basement membrane; not all cells reach the lumen (short basal cells don’t).
  • Usually ciliated with goblet cells: “respiratory epithelium”.
  • Where: trachea, bronchi, nasal cavity; non-ciliated with stereocilia in the epididymis.

Stratified squamous epithelium

  • Multiple layers; cells become flatter as they move from the basal layer (cuboidal/columnar) toward the surface.
  • Protects against abrasion.

Stratified cuboidal epithelium

  • Multiple (often two) layers with an outer layer of cuboidal cells.
  • Limited distribution: lining of larger ducts (sweat, salivary glands).

Transitional epithelium (urothelium)

  • Adapted for stretching; found only in the urinary tract.
  • Multiple layers with a surface layer of large, dome-shaped umbrella cells (sometimes two nuclei).
  • Relaxed (empty bladder): many layers, domed surface cells. Stretched (full bladder): fewer layers, surface cells flatten.
