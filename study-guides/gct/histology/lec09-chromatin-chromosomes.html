Chromatin and Chromosomes

How 2 metres of DNA fit in the nucleus (nucleosomes, the 30-nm fibre, loops), euchromatin versus heterochromatin (constitutive and facultative, Barr bodies), chromosome territories, ploidy and karyotypes.

Labobo topic: Histo Lec 9: Chromatin and Chromosomes

In 60 seconds

  • Chromatin = DNA + proteins that organise and regulate it. Each cell: ~2 m of DNA, 3.2 billion bp; 46 chromosomes (23 pairs).
  • After replication, each chromosome = two sister chromatids held by cohesin.
  • Nucleosome: core of 8 histones (2 × H2A, H2B, H3, H4) with ~150 bp wrapped; linker DNA 50–80 bp; H1 binds outside. “Beads on a string” → 30-nm fibre → loops on a scaffold.
  • Euchromatin: loose, pale, active. Heterochromatin: dense, dark, inactive; constitutive (repetitive DNA: centromeres, telomeres) or facultative (can be reactivated; e.g. the Barr body).
  • Active cells (neurons) have more euchromatin; inactive cells (lymphocytes) more heterochromatin.
  • Chromosomes occupy territories: gene-poor at the periphery, gene-rich in the centre. Diploid 2n = 46, haploid n = 23. Karyotype from metaphase cells arrested with colchicine.

Learning objectives

  • describe the levels of DNA packaging;
  • compare euchromatin and the two kinds of heterochromatin;
  • relate chromatin appearance to cell activity;
  • describe chromosome territories, ploidy and karyotyping.

Chromatin and DNA packaging

  • Chromatin = DNA + associated proteins that organise and regulate it.
  • Human cells (except eggs and sperm) have 46 chromosomes = 23 pairs.
  • After DNA replication, each chromosome has two identical sister chromatids held together by cohesin proteins.
  • Each cell holds about 2 metres of DNA with 3.2 billion base pairs, packed into a nucleus a few micrometres wide.
  • DNA wraps around histone proteins to form nucleosomes, the basic structural units, like “beads on a string”.

Chromatin and cell activity

  • The heterochromatin : euchromatin ratio reflects how active a cell is.
  • Active cells (e.g. neurons): more euchromatin → a pale, large nucleus.
  • Inactive cells (e.g. resting lymphocytes): more heterochromatin → a dark, compact nucleus.
  • During cell division chromatin becomes highly compacted into visible chromosomes.

Chromosome organisation

  • Heterochromatin is often near the nuclear lamina.
  • FISH shows each chromosome occupies its own territory in the interphase nucleus.
  • Gene-poor chromosomes sit near the nuclear envelope; gene-rich ones are deeper inside.
  • Humans have 22 pairs of autosomes and 1 pair of sex chromosomes: females XX, males XY.
  • Each pair has one chromosome from the mother and one from the father: homologous (similar genes, not identical alleles).

Ploidy and karyotype

  • Most cells are diploid (2n) = 46 chromosomes.
  • Gametes (sperm and eggs) are haploid (n) = 23, because homologues separate in meiosis.
  • Karyotype analysis: chromosomes are studied in mitotic metaphase, when they are most condensed.
  • Colchicine (blocks spindle microtubules) arrests cells in metaphase; stained chromosomes are arranged in pairs by size and banding into a karyotype.

Exam traps

Exam trap
  • H1 is not in the octamer core.
  • Sister chromatids are held by cohesin.
  • Centromeres and telomeres = constitutive heterochromatin.
  • Barr body = facultative heterochromatin.
  • Pale nucleus = active (euchromatin); dark nucleus = inactive.
  • Gene-poor chromosomes lie at the periphery.
  • Karyotypes use metaphase (colchicine).

Key terms

Chromatin
DNA plus its packaging proteins.
Nucleosome
DNA wrapped around a histone octamer.
Linker DNA
DNA between nucleosomes.
Cohesin
Protein holding sister chromatids together.
Euchromatin
Loose, active chromatin.
Heterochromatin
Dense, inactive chromatin.
Barr body
Condensed inactive X chromosome.
Chromosome territory
Region of the nucleus occupied by one chromosome.
Karyotype
Ordered display of a cell’s chromosomes.
