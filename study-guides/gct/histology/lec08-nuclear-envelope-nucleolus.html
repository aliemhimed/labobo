The Nucleus: Nuclear Envelope and Nucleolus

What the nucleus does, the double nuclear envelope with its lamina and pore complexes, how molecules are moved in and out (importins, exportins, GTP), and how the nucleolus builds ribosomes. Chromatin from the same deck is in the Lecture 9 guide.

Labobo topic: Histo Lec 8: Nuclear Envelope and Nucleolus

In 60 seconds

  • The nucleus is the command centre: DNA plus the machinery for replication and for making and processing RNA. Usually the largest structure, round or oval, central.
  • Three components: nuclear envelope, chromatin, nucleolus.
  • Separating nucleus and cytoplasm means RNA processing finishes before translation.
  • Envelope: two membranes + perinuclear space. Outer membrane is continuous with the RER; inner membrane is supported by the nuclear lamina (lamins).
  • Nuclear pore complexes (made of nucleoporins) join the two membranes; transport uses importins/exportins and GTP energy.
  • Nucleolus: spherical, strongly basophilic (rRNA); forms around rRNA genes; fibrillar and granular parts; large in protein-making cells; assembles ribosomal subunits.

The nucleus

  • Contains the genetic information (DNA) and the machinery for DNA replication and RNA synthesis and processing.
  • Typically the largest structure in the cell; usually round or oval, near the centre.
  • Components: nuclear envelope, chromatin, nucleoli.
  • In interphase, nuclear pores control traffic:
    • RNA moves out to the cytoplasm for protein synthesis;
    • proteins move in for nuclear functions (histones, polymerases, transcription factors).
  • This separation ensures RNA processing is complete before translation (contrast bacteria, which translate while transcribing).

Nuclear envelope

  • A selectively permeable barrier between nucleus and cytoplasm.
  • Two membranes separated by a narrow perinuclear space.
  • Outer membrane: continuous with the RER (often has ribosomes on it); the perinuclear space is continuous with the ER lumen.
  • Inner membrane: supported by the nuclear lamina, a meshwork of lamins (intermediate filaments) that stabilises the envelope and anchors chromatin.

Nuclear pore complexes and transport

  • Nuclear pore complexes (NPCs) bridge the inner and outer membranes, forming channels for macromolecules.
  • Made of proteins called nucleoporins.
  • They control movement of ribosomal subunits (out), RNA (out), chromatin proteins and transcription factors (in).
  • Large molecules carry signal sequences (nuclear localisation or export signals) that are recognised by carrier proteins, importins (in) and exportins (out).
  • Transport needs energy from GTP (via the small protein Ran).

The nucleolus

  • A spherical, intensely basophilic structure inside the nucleus, prominent in cells active in protein synthesis (they need many ribosomes).
  • Its basophilia comes from its high rRNA content.
  • It forms around the chromosome regions holding rRNA genes (nucleolar organiser regions).
  • It has no membrane. Two subregions reflect stages of rRNA maturation:
    • fibrillar part: rRNA genes being transcribed and early processing;
    • granular part: maturing ribosomal subunits.
  1. rRNA is transcribed and processed.
  2. Ribosomal proteins made in the cytoplasm are imported.
  3. rRNA + proteins → ribosomal subunits.
  4. Subunits are exported to the cytoplasm through nuclear pores.

Exam traps

Exam trap
  • The outer nuclear membrane is continuous with the RER; the inner one has the lamina.
  • Pore proteins = nucleoporins; lamina proteins = lamins.
  • Nuclear transport uses GTP.
  • The nucleolus has no membrane and is basophilic because of rRNA.
  • Ribosomal proteins are made in the cytoplasm, not the nucleolus.

Key terms

Perinuclear space
Gap between the two nuclear membranes.
Nuclear lamina
Lamin meshwork under the inner membrane.
Nuclear pore complex
Gated channel through the envelope.
Nucleoporins
Proteins of the pore complex.
Importin / exportin
Carriers moving cargo in / out.
Nucleolus
Site of rRNA synthesis and ribosome assembly.
