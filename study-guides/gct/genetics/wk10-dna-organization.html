DNA Organization in Viral and Bacterial Chromosomes

How viruses and bacteria fit very long DNA into tiny spaces: compact genomes, the nucleoid and its proteins, supercoiling explained step by step with the linking number, and the topoisomerases (including DNA gyrase) that control it.

Labobo topic: MG Wk 10: DNA Organization in Chromosomes

In 60 seconds

  • Viral genomes can be DNA or RNA, single or double, linear or circular, segmented or not; all are very compact.
  • Phage λ DNA ≈ 17 μm long in a head ≈ 50 nm wide; it is pumped into the capsid under pressure.
  • Bacteria: one circular dsDNA in the nucleoid; E. coli DNA ≈ 1.5 mm, compacted > 1000-fold by supercoiling, loops and proteins HU, IHF, H-NS, Fis, Dps.
  • Linking number (L) = how many times the strands wind round each other; in closed DNA it can change only by cutting.
  • Negative supercoils = underwound, easier to open (natural state of bacteria). Positive = overwound, form ahead of polymerases.
  • Type I topoisomerase: cuts one strand, L changes by 1, no ATP. Type II: cuts both, L changes by 2, needs ATP; DNA gyrase adds negative supercoils.

Viral chromosomes

DNA’s chemistry is universal, but how it is packed differs between viruses, bacteria and eukaryotes. Viral genomes vary the most:

  • DNA or RNA; single- or double-stranded; linear or circular; segmented or one piece.
  • All must be extremely compact to fit inside the capsid, tightly bound to capsid proteins.
  • Viral genomes are efficient: only the genes needed for infection and replication.

Bacteriophage λ

Fully stretched, λ DNA is about 17 μm long, while the phage head is only about 50 nm wide. The DNA is folded so tightly that it sits under physical pressure and must be actively pumped into the capsid during assembly.

Bacterial chromosomes

  • Usually one circular double-stranded DNA molecule.
  • No nucleus: the DNA is confined to a region called the nucleoid.
  • E. coli DNA is about 1.5 mm long, in a cell a few micrometres long, so it must be compacted more than 1000-fold.
  • Compaction uses supercoiling, DNA loops (looped domains) and DNA-binding proteins.

Nucleoid-associated proteins

HU, IHF, H-NS, Fis and Dps bend, bridge or wrap DNA and stabilise the loops. They also regulate genes, respond to stress and protect DNA from damage. This organisation lets bacteria replicate fast and adapt quickly.

Why supercoiling is needed

  • Reminder: DNA is two strands paired A–T and G–C, twisting as a right-handed helix with about one turn per 10 bp. It can be linear (two free ends) or a closed circle.
  • The DNA of bacteria and viruses is far longer than the space available. Relaxed DNA would not fit and would be hard to open quickly.
  • Cells need DNA that is compact but still easy to open for replication and transcription. Supercoiling does both.

Supercoiling = the whole double helix coiling around itself, like an over-twisted telephone cord. It happens when closed DNA (which can’t spin freely) is underwound or overwound; the strain is relieved by the duplex twisting into supercoils, becoming shorter and more compact.

Supercoiling step by step (linking number)

The linking number (L) = the number of times the two strands wind around each other.

  1. (a) Linear DNA with 20 helical turns: L = 20. Ends are free, so it can rotate: no strain.
  2. (b) Relaxed circle: ends joined without adding or removing turns: still L = 20, normal number of turns, no strain.
  3. (c) Underwound circle: cut, remove 2 turns, reseal: L = 18. It can’t rotate, so it is strained (a distorted region).
  4. (d) Supercoiled: to relieve the strain the circle coils on itself, forming two negative supercoils. L stays 18, but the molecule is compact and stable.
Key rule

In a closed circle, L cannot change unless a strand is cut. Molecules with the same sequence but different L are topoisomers.

The problem replication and transcription create

DNA and RNA polymerases unwind the helix as they move. This piles up positive supercoils in front and negative supercoils behind. Without relief, the DNA ahead becomes so overwound that the polymerase stalls. Cells need enzymes that cut, untwist and reseal DNA in a controlled way.

Topoisomerases

Topoisomerases change the topology of DNA: they cut one or both strands, let DNA pass through the break, and reseal it. They convert one topoisomer into another, adding, removing or redistributing supercoils.

In the clinic: topoisomerase drugs

Some antibiotics and anticancer drugs work by blocking topoisomerases. From pharmacology: fluoroquinolones (e.g. ciprofloxacin) inhibit bacterial DNA gyrase; etoposide and doxorubicin inhibit human topo II; irinotecan and topotecan inhibit topo I.

Comparison with eukaryotes

  • Eukaryotes wrap DNA around histones into nucleosomes, forming chromatin, which packs very large genomes into the nucleus.
  • Viruses and bacteria have no nucleosomes, so they rely mainly on supercoiling and DNA-binding proteins.
  • Eukaryotic chromosomes are linear, but chromatin loops are anchored to protein scaffolds. The anchors act as fixed ends, so each loop behaves like closed DNA and can supercoil.
  • The same torsional stress arises in eukaryotic replication and transcription, so topoisomerases are again needed to remove positive supercoils and prevent tangling.

Exam traps

Exam trap
  • Supercoiling into a compact shape does not change L; only cutting does.
  • Bacterial DNA is naturally negatively supercoiled.
  • Positive supercoils form ahead of the fork.
  • Type II needs ATP; type I usually doesn’t.
  • Eukaryotic topo II mainly decatenates; it doesn’t introduce negative supercoils like gyrase.

Key terms

Capsid
Protein shell of a virus.
Nucleoid
Region holding the bacterial chromosome.
Supercoiling
Coiling of the whole double helix on itself.
Linking number
Times the two strands wind round each other in closed DNA.
Relaxed DNA
Closed DNA with its normal number of turns, no strain.
Negative / positive supercoil
From underwinding / overwinding.
Topoisomer
Same sequence, different linking number.
Topoisomerase
Enzyme that cuts and reseals DNA to change its topology.
DNA gyrase
Bacterial type II topoisomerase adding negative supercoils.
Decatenation
Untangling of interlinked DNA molecules.
Nucleosome
DNA wrapped around histones (eukaryotes).
