Sex Determination and Differentiation in Humans

How the father’s sperm decides genetic sex, what the X and Y chromosomes carry, how SRY switches on the testis pathway, how hormones then build male or female anatomy, the Y genes needed for sperm (AZF), and the three human sex ratios.

Labobo topic: MG Wk 5: Sex Determination

In 60 seconds

  • Sex determination = the genetic decision at fertilisation; sex differentiation = the anatomy that follows.
  • Every egg carries X; sperm carry X or Y → the father decides sex. Primary sex ratio ≈ 1 : 1.
  • X: large, > 1000 genes. Y: small, < 200 genes. PAR1/PAR2 let X and Y pair; MSY (~95% of Y) doesn’t recombine.
  • SRY (Yp11.3, HMG transcription factor) at ~6 weeks activates SOX9 → Sertoli cells → testis. SRY is necessary and sufficient.
  • Sertoli → AMH (Müllerian ducts regress). Leydig → testosterone (Wolffian ducts). 5α-reductase → DHT (penis, scrotum, prostate).
  • No SRY → ovaries; Müllerian ducts persist; Wolffian ducts regress: the default pathway.
  • AZF regions on Yq: a (Sertoli-cell-only), b (maturation arrest), c (DAZ; oligo- to azoospermia). Secondary sex ratio ≈ 105 : 100.

Chromosomal basis of sex

  • 46 chromosomes: 22 pairs of autosomes + 1 pair of sex chromosomes. Female XX, male XY.
  • Meiosis makes haploid gametes with 23 chromosomes:
    • every egg carries one X;
    • sperm are of two types: half carry X, half carry Y.
  • X sperm + egg → XX (female). Y sperm + egg → XY (male).
  • So the father determines the child’s sex. Equal numbers of X and Y sperm give a primary sex ratio of about 1 : 1.

The X and Y chromosomes

X chromosomeY chromosome
SizeLarge, gene-richSmall, gene-poor
Genes> 1000; mostly metabolism, growth, nerve development (not sex determination)< 200 functional; many for male development and reproduction
  • Pseudoautosomal regions (PAR1 and PAR2): small matching regions at the tips of X and Y. They let X and Y pair and recombine in meiosis and behave like autosomal genes; essential for correct segregation.
  • Male-specific region of the Y (MSY): between the PARs, about 95% of Y. It does not recombine with X and passes almost unchanged from father to son. It carries testis-formation and spermatogenesis genes.

The indifferent stage

Genetic sex is fixed at fertilisation, but for the first weeks XX and XY embryos look identical. The reproductive system is bipotential; genes, hormones and receptors begin acting around week 6.

At about 4–5 weeks, every embryo has:

  • Gonadal ridges: bulges on the mesonephros (primitive kidney) that become testes or ovaries.
  • Two pairs of ducts: Wolffian (mesonephric) and Müllerian (paramesonephric).
  • External genitalia: the genital tubercle, urogenital folds and labioscrotal swellings.

SRY and testis formation

  • SRY (sex-determining region of the Y) is on the short arm of Y, Yp11.3: the master switch for testis formation.
  • SRY protein is an HMG (high-mobility-group) transcription factor: it binds and bends DNA, changing chromatin to switch on downstream genes, especially SOX9.
  • SOX9 drives Sertoli cell differentiation and testis cord formation, and keeps itself on by positive feedback, so the male pathway continues after SRY switches off. It also suppresses the ovarian pathway.
  • SRY acts briefly, around week 6 (6–7), but decisively.
  • No working SRY → the bipotential gonad becomes an ovary, even with a Y present.

Male differentiation is an active process that needs each hormonal step.

In the clinic (textbook)

5α-reductase deficiency: XY with testes and male internal ducts, but female-looking or ambiguous external genitalia at birth (no DHT). Androgen insensitivity: XY with testes and AMH (no uterus), but no response to testosterone → female external appearance.

Female differentiation

  • XX: no Y, no SRY → gonads become ovaries; supporting cells → granulosa cells, interstitial cells → theca cells.
  • No Sertoli cells → no AMH → Müllerian ducts stay and form the fallopian tubes, uterus and upper vagina.
  • No testosterone → Wolffian ducts regress.
  • No DHT → external genitalia take the female form.

Because female development happens when male signals are absent, it is called the default pathway.

Y genes and spermatogenesis

Beyond SRY, the long arm (Yq) carries the azoospermia factor (AZF) regions needed for sperm production. Deletions here are a major genetic cause of male infertility.

RegionGenesDeletion causes
AZFaUSP9Y, DDX3Y (germ-cell survival)Sertoli-cell-only syndrome (no germ cells)
AZFb—Maturation arrest at the spermatocyte stage
AZFcDAZ (deleted in azoospermia) family: RNA-binding proteins for germ-cell maturationVariable: oligospermia to complete azoospermia
  • Other testis-expressed Y genes: RBMY, PRY, HSFY, TSPY (spermatid differentiation and meiosis).
  • Many lie in multiple copies inside large palindromes, which allow limited recombination within the Y to correct mutations.
  • The same repeats make the Y unstable, prone to microdeletions that impair fertility.

Exam traps

Exam trap
  • The father, not the mother, decides the baby’s sex.
  • SRY is on the short arm (Yp); AZF regions are on the long arm (Yq).
  • 46,XX with SRY → male; 46,XY without SRY → female.
  • AMH comes from Sertoli cells, not Leydig.
  • The secondary sex ratio favours males slightly; older adults are mostly female.
