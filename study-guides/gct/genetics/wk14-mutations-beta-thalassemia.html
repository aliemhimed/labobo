Spontaneous and Induced Mutations, and β-Thalassemia

Where mutations come from (replication errors, tautomers, slippage, UV, ionising radiation, chemicals), how single base-pair changes cause disease, and β-thalassemia as the model of one gene with hundreds of mutations and a spectrum of severity.

Labobo topic: MG Wk 14: Mutations and Beta-Thalassemia

In 60 seconds

  • Spontaneous mutations come from normal processes; induced mutations come from mutagens. Both are random, not directed.
  • Spontaneous causes: polymerase errors, tautomeric shifts (a hydrogen moves → wrong pairing), replication slippage in repeats (insertions/deletions, frameshifts; hot spots).
  • Physical mutagens: UV → pyrimidine (thymine) dimers; ionising radiation (X, γ) → free radicals, strand breaks, chromosome changes; dose-dependent.
  • Chemical mutagens: base analogues (mispair later), alkylating agents (mostly on guanine; cross-links).
  • ~30% of disease mutations are nonsense; ~15% of point mutations affect splicing (85% of these hit the 5′/3′ splice sites).
  • β-thalassemia: autosomal recessive, HBB on 11p, ~1.6 kb, 3 exons + 2 introns, 146 amino acids; about 400 mutations.
  • Promoter/5′UTR and poly(A) mutations → milder; start codon, nonsense, frameshift, splice-site → severe.

How spontaneous mutations occur

1. Replication errors

DNA polymerase occasionally inserts the wrong nucleotide. Most errors are fixed by proofreading and repair; the few that escape become permanent after the next round of replication.

2. Tautomeric shifts

  • Rarely, a base shifts a hydrogen atom, taking a temporary alternative form: a tautomer.
  • In that form it can pair with the wrong base during replication.
  • If not repaired, the next replication fixes a permanent base substitution.
  • Random and unpreventable; part of the natural background rate.

3. Replication slippage

  • Happens in repetitive sequences, where the polymerase loses its place.
  • New strand slips back → extra nucleotides → insertion. Template slips forward → nucleotides skipped → deletion.
  • Can shift the reading frame (frameshift).
  • Repeats are therefore mutation hot spots, involved in several diseases (e.g. triplet repeat disorders).

Physical mutagens

AgentDamageResult
Ultraviolet (UV) light (sunlight)Abnormal bonds between neighbouring pyrimidines, mostly thymine → pyrimidine (thymine) dimers that distort the helixPolymerase struggles to read it; wrong bases inserted if not repaired first
Ionising radiation (X-rays, γ-rays)Penetrates cells, makes free radicals → modified bases, broken backbone, single- and double-strand breaksPoint mutations and large chromosome changes (deletions, translocations, fragmentation)

Mutation frequency rises with radiation dose; even low doses of ionising radiation can be mutagenic.

Chemical mutagens

GroupHow it works
Base analoguesLook like normal bases, so they are built into DNA; in later replications they mispair → base substitutions (e.g. 5-bromouracil)
Alkylating agentsAdd alkyl groups, most often to guanine, changing its pairing → mispairing; also strand breaks and cross-links that block replication and transcription (e.g. mustard gas, EMS)

Chemical mutations are the same kinds of change as spontaneous ones (substitutions, insertions, deletions); chemicals simply make them more frequent.

Single base-pair mutations in disease

  • Most human diseases are polygenic, but a change in one of the ~20,000 genes can be enough: monogenic disease.
  • Single base-pair mutations are among the most common defects.

β-Thalassemia: genetic basis

  • Autosomal recessive blood disorder: mutations in HBB (β-globin) reduce or abolish β-chain synthesis → less or abnormal hemoglobin.
  • Described as the most common single-gene disease worldwide, especially in Mediterranean, North African, Middle Eastern, Central Asian and Southeast Asian populations.
The HBB gene

This is extreme genetic heterogeneity: one gene, many possible mutations. Still, most cases worldwide come from a few common mutations, and each population has its own typical set.

Rule of thumb

Mutations that only lower the amount of normal mRNA (promoter, 5′UTR, poly(A)) → milder (β⁺). Mutations that destroy the protein or mRNA (start codon, nonsense, frameshift, splice site) → severe (β⁰).

Consequences and clinical variability

  • Structural changes (missense, nonsense, frameshift): short or unstable β-chains that can’t form normal Hb → little or no functional β-globin → severe disease.
  • Expression changes (transcription, splicing, mRNA stability, translation): the coding sequence may be fine, but less β-globin is made; severity depends on how much less.

So patients range from severe anemia early in life with regular transfusions to mild anemia with few symptoms.

Common clinical features
Weakness; delayed growth and development
Jaundice
Enlarged spleen and liver
Complications of chronic anemia and of transfusions (iron overload)

Severity depends not only on which gene is hit, but on how the mutation disrupts expression and protein production.

Exam traps

Exam trap
  • Induced mutations are the same types as spontaneous ones, just more frequent.
  • Mutations are random, not adaptive responses.
  • UV mainly links pyrimidines (thymine), not purines.
  • β-thalassemia is autosomal recessive and caused by many different mutations.
  • Poly(A) signal mutations usually give milder disease.

Key terms

Spontaneous mutation
Arises from normal cellular processes.
Induced mutation
Caused by a mutagen.
Mutagen
Physical or chemical agent that raises mutation rate.
Tautomer
Rare alternative form of a base that mispairs.
Replication slippage
Polymerase misalignment in repeats → insertion/deletion.
Hot spot
Region with an unusually high mutation rate.
Pyrimidine dimer
UV-induced bond between adjacent pyrimidines.
Base analogue
Chemical resembling a base that mispairs.
Alkylating agent
Adds alkyl groups to bases.
Monogenic disease
Disease caused by mutation in one gene.
Splice-site mutation
Change at an intron–exon boundary disrupting splicing.
Genetic heterogeneity
Many different mutations giving the same disease.
