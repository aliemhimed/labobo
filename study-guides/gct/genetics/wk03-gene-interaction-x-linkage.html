Gene Interaction, X-Linkage, Penetrance and Imprinting

How two genes shape one trait (epistasis and its modified ratios), how one gene shapes many traits (pleiotropy), X-linked and sex-dependent inheritance, why the same genotype can look different (penetrance, expressivity), and parent-of-origin effects (Prader–Willi and Angelman).

Labobo topic: MG Wk 3: Gene Interaction & X-Linkage

In 60 seconds

  • Mendel’s 4th postulate: independent assortment → dihybrid ratio 9 : 3 : 3 : 1.
  • Epistasis ratios: recessive 9 : 3 : 4, dominant 12 : 3 : 1, complementary 9 : 7, duplicate 15 : 1.
  • Pleiotropy: one gene, many traits (Marfan: FBN1 → eye, heart, skeleton).
  • X-linked recessive (colour blindness, hemophilia, DMD): mostly males, no father→son. X-linked dominant (vitamin D-resistant rickets, Rett): affected father → all daughters.
  • Sex-limited = expressed in one sex only (milk, beard). Sex-influenced = dominance differs by sex (pattern baldness).
  • Penetrance = does it show (%); expressivity = how strongly (NF1).
  • Imprinting at 15q11–q13: lose paternal → Prader–Willi; lose maternal (UBE3A) → Angelman.

Mendel’s fourth postulate: independent assortment

During gamete formation, allele pairs for different traits sort into gametes independently. In peas, inheriting seed colour (yellow/green) does not affect inheriting seed shape (round/wrinkled).

A dihybrid cross AaBb × AaBb therefore gives 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 (9 A_B_ : 3 A_bb : 3 aaB_ : 1 aabb), from 16 equal combinations.

Gene interaction

Gene interaction: two or more different genes (not alleles of one gene) influence one trait. The 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 ratio then often changes, because the genes’ effects depend on each other.

Picture a factory line: each worker (gene) does one step. Gene A makes a colourless substance; gene B turns it into red pigment. If either fails, there is no colour. Both are needed.

Mendel’s laws still hold

With gene interaction the genes still segregate and assort independently. Only the phenotypes are grouped differently, so the 16 parts are shared out in a new way (9 : 7, 12 : 3 : 1…). The total is always 16.

Epistasis and its ratios

Epistasis (Greek epi = upon, stasis = standing/stopping): one gene masks the effect of another gene at a different locus. The masking gene is epistatic; the hidden gene is hypostatic.

TypeWhat hidesRatioExample and logic
Recessive epistasisHomozygous recessive aa9 : 3 : 4Mouse coat: A = makes pigment, a = albino; B = black, b = brown. 9 A_B_ black : 3 A_bb brown : 4 aa__ albino (no pigment for B to colour)
Dominant epistasisA dominant allele A12 : 3 : 1Summer squash: A blocks pigment → white. 12 A___ white : 3 aaB_ yellow : 1 aabb green
Complementary gene actionEither gene homozygous recessive9 : 7Sweet pea: both C and P needed for purple. 9 C_P_ purple : 7 white (C_pp, ccP_, ccpp)
Duplicate dominantEither dominant gene is enough15 : 1A or B both give a triangular flower/fruit; only aabb is ovoid. 15 triangular : 1 ovoid
How to rebuild each ratio from 9 : 3 : 3 : 1
  • 9 : 3 : (3 + 1) = 9 : 3 : 4 → recessive
  • (9 + 3) : 3 : 1 = 12 : 3 : 1 → dominant
  • 9 : (3 + 3 + 1) = 9 : 7 → complementary
  • (9 + 3 + 3) : 1 = 15 : 1 → duplicate

Pleiotropy

Pleiotropy: one gene affects two or more different traits. The same protein is used in several organs, so one mutation gives many symptoms.

Two or more genes → one traitOne gene → several traits
Sweet-pea flower colourMarfan syndrome

Marfan syndrome

  • Gene FBN1 → fibrillin-1, which builds connective tissue throughout the body.
  • Mutation weakens connective tissue everywhere:
    • skeleton: long arms and fingers;
    • eye: lens dislocation;
    • cardiovascular: weak aorta → aneurysm.

The objectives also name PKU (phenylketonuria): one enzyme defect (phenylalanine hydroxylase) causes intellectual disability, light skin and hair, and a musty odour.

X-linked inheritance

Humans have 22 pairs of autosomes and one pair of sex chromosomes (XX female, XY male). A trait whose gene is on the X chromosome is X-linked.

X-linked recessive

  • Females need two mutant copies; males need only one (they have one X, they are hemizygous). So males are affected far more often.
  • Crisscross pattern: an affected man passes the allele to all his daughters (carriers), who pass it to half their sons.
  • Examples: red–green colour blindness, hemophilia, Duchenne muscular dystrophy.

X-linked dominant

  • Both sexes affected, often more females (two X chromosomes = two chances).
  • Affected father → all daughters affected, no sons (sons get his Y).
  • Affected mother (heterozygous) → 50% of sons and daughters affected.
  • Examples: vitamin D-resistant rickets, Rett syndrome; fragile X shows dominant-like inheritance in some forms.

Genomic imprinting

  • Expression of a gene depends on which parent it came from.
  • An epigenetic process: one parental allele is expressed, the other silenced, by DNA methylation at CpG islands and histone modification. The DNA sequence does not change.
  • Some genes are expressed only from the father, others only from the mother.
  • Set during gametogenesis (before fertilisation), then maintained through mitosis after conception.
  • Imprinted genes: one allele expressed. Non-imprinted genes: both expressed.

Why it matters

  • Growth and development: fetal growth, placenta, brain development.
  • Disease: Prader–Willi, Angelman, Beckwith–Wiedemann, Silver–Russell.
  • Cancer: loss of imprinting (LOI) in Wilms tumour and colorectal cancer.
  • It shows how epigenetics links inheritance, environment and disease.

Prader–Willi and Angelman syndromes

Both involve chromosome 15q11–q13, the most studied imprinted region.

Watch out

Watch out: uniparental disomy in Angelman

Slide 27 says “paternal disomy much less common than maternal”. In Angelman, the disomy that causes disease is paternal (two paternal copies, no maternal UBE3A); in Prader–Willi it is maternal. Uniparental disomy is a minor cause in Angelman (3–5%) but a major one in Prader–Willi (20–25%). Learn the rule: the missing parent’s copy causes the syndrome.

Exam traps

Exam trap
  • Epistasis does not break Mendel’s laws; it only changes phenotype ratios.
  • X-linked inheritance never goes father → son.
  • An affected father with an X-linked dominant trait has all daughters affected.
  • Pattern baldness is autosomal (sex-influenced), not X-linked.
  • NF1 shows variable expressivity with 100% penetrance.
  • Imprinting changes methylation, not the DNA sequence.

Check yourself

  1. AaBb × AaBb in mice gives 9 black : 3 brown : 4 albino. This is:

    1. Dominant epistasis
    2. Recessive epistasis
    3. Complementary genes
    4. Duplicate genes
    Show answer

    B. aa blocks pigment whatever B is.

  2. The ratio 12 : 3 : 1 indicates:

    1. Recessive epistasis
    2. Dominant epistasis
    3. Complementary genes
    4. Codominance
    Show answer

    B. Summer squash colour.

  3. Two white sweet-pea lines give purple F1; F2 is 9 purple : 7 white. The genes show:

    1. Complementary gene action
    2. Dominant epistasis
    3. Pleiotropy
    4. Duplicate dominance
    Show answer

    A. Both C and P are needed.

  4. Marfan syndrome affecting the eye, aorta and skeleton is an example of:

    1. Epistasis
    2. Pleiotropy
    3. Incomplete penetrance
    4. Imprinting
    Show answer

    B. One gene (FBN1), many traits.

  5. A colour-blind man and a homozygous normal woman have children. Their daughters will be:

    1. All colour-blind
    2. All carriers
    3. Half carriers
    4. All normal non-carriers
    Show answer

    B. Each daughter gets his X; sons get his Y and are normal.

  6. An affected father with vitamin D-resistant rickets will have:

    1. All sons affected
    2. All daughters affected, no sons
    3. 50% of all children affected
    4. No affected children
    Show answer

    B. X-linked dominant.

  7. A Bb man is bald but a Bb woman is not. This is:

    1. Sex-limited
    2. Sex-influenced
    3. X-linked
    4. Imprinting
    Show answer

    B. Dominance differs between sexes.

  8. Milk yield genes carried by bulls but expressed only in cows show:

    1. Sex-limited inheritance
    2. Sex-influenced inheritance
    3. Y-linkage
    4. Pleiotropy
    Show answer

    A. The trait appears in only one sex.

  9. Only 7 of 10 people with a dominant allele show the trait. The penetrance is:

    1. 30%
    2. 70%
    3. 100%
    4. Variable expressivity
    Show answer

    B. Incomplete penetrance.

  10. NF1 patients range from a few café-au-lait spots to many tumours. This is:

    1. Incomplete penetrance
    2. Variable expressivity
    3. Epistasis
    4. Anticipation
    Show answer

    B. All show it, with different severity.

  11. Genomic imprinting is mainly caused by:

    1. Point mutations
    2. DNA methylation at CpG islands
    3. Crossing-over
    4. Nondisjunction
    Show answer

    B. An epigenetic mark set in gametogenesis.

  12. A child with neonatal hypotonia, later hyperphagia, obesity and hypogonadism most likely lacks:

    1. Maternal 15q11–q13
    2. Paternal 15q11–q13
    3. UBE3A only
    4. An X chromosome
    Show answer

    B. Prader–Willi syndrome.

Key terms

Independent assortment
Alleles of different genes sort into gametes independently.
Dihybrid cross
Cross following two genes.
Gene interaction
Several genes affecting one trait.
Epistasis
One gene masking another gene’s effect.
Epistatic / hypostatic
Masking / masked gene.
Pleiotropy
One gene affecting several traits.
Hemizygous
Having one copy of a gene (X genes in males).
Carrier
Heterozygote for a recessive allele who shows no disease.
Sex-limited trait
Expressed in only one sex.
Sex-influenced trait
Dominance of an allele differs between sexes.
Penetrance
% of genotype carriers showing the phenotype.
Expressivity
Degree to which the phenotype is expressed.
Genomic imprinting
Parent-of-origin-specific gene silencing.
Uniparental disomy
Both copies of a chromosome from one parent.
Loss of imprinting
Both alleles expressed when one should be silent; linked to cancer.
