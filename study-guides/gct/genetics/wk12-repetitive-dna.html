Repetitive DNA: VNTRs, STRs, rRNA Genes, LINEs, SINEs and Pseudogenes

The 98% of the genome that is not protein-coding: tandem repeats used in DNA fingerprinting and forensics (and that cause triplet repeat diseases), the repeated rRNA genes, the jumping LINE and Alu elements, and pseudogenes.

Labobo topic: MG Wk 12: Repetitive DNA Sequences

In 60 seconds

  • Protein-coding DNA is < 2%; repetitive DNA is > 50% of the genome.
  • VNTRs (minisatellites): 15–100 bp units; arrays up to 1–20 kb; Jeffreys’ DNA fingerprinting (Southern blot).
  • STRs (microsatellites): 2–6 bp units, usually < 400 bp total; PCR-friendly; 20 CODIS loci; MSI in Lynch syndrome; triplet expansions (HD, fragile X…).
  • Variation arises by unequal crossing-over and replication slippage.
  • 45S rRNA genes on short arms of 13, 14, 15, 21, 22 → 18S, 5.8S, 28S; 5S on chromosome 1.
  • LINE-1: ~6 kb, ~850,000 copies, 17–21% of genome; autonomous (ORF1 + ORF2 reverse transcriptase/endonuclease).
  • Alu (SINE): ~300 bp, > 1.5 million copies, > 10% of genome; from 7SL RNA; non-autonomous (uses L1 machinery).

Learning objectives

By the end you can:

  • describe the make-up of the eukaryotic genome and classify repetitive DNA;
  • compare VNTRs and STRs: structure, origin, function and uses;
  • explain the clinical importance of STRs (triplet expansions, microsatellite instability);
  • describe the repeated rRNA genes;
  • explain LINEs and SINEs and how they move (retrotransposition);
  • define pseudogenes.

The genome landscape

About 20,000 protein-coding genes are spread over 23 chromosomes, but coding DNA is less than 2% of the genome. The other 98% is:

  • introns;
  • regulatory elements;
  • repetitive DNA (over 50%);
  • pseudogenes;
  • transposable elements.

VNTRs (minisatellites)

  • A 15–100 bp unit repeated many times in a row; the number of repeats varies between people.
  • Usually in non-coding DNA (between genes or in introns).
  • A locus can span a few hundred bp up to 1–20 kb.
  • You inherit one VNTR allele from each parent, so each person has characteristic fragment sizes.

Why they vary

MechanismHow
Unequal crossing-overIn meiosis the repeats of two homologues misalign; recombination at the wrong place gives one chromosome extra repeats and the other fewer
Replication slippageDNA polymerase briefly detaches and re-attaches out of register, making a loop → repeat units added or deleted

These events are frequent over evolutionary time, so VNTRs are among the most polymorphic regions of the genome.

Function

Not protein-coding, but VNTRs near promoters or enhancers can change spacing and access for transcription factors, subtly altering expression. They can be recombination hotspots. Many have no known function (selectively neutral).

Uses

  • Alec Jeffreys used their variable lengths for DNA fingerprinting: forensics, paternity testing, identity.
  • Method: restriction enzyme digestion → gel electrophoresis → Southern blotting, giving a band pattern unique to each person (except identical twins).
  • Population genetics: diversity, ancestry, migration.
  • Today forensics mainly uses STRs (PCR-friendly); VNTRs remain historically important.

STRs (microsatellites)

  • A 2–6 bp motif repeated many times, e.g. (CA)₁₀ or (GATA)₇; highly polymorphic.
  • Found at thousands of sites on all chromosomes; mostly non-coding, but some in introns, UTRs or even coding sequence.
  • Usually < 400 bp long in total: short and stable enough for routine PCR.

Function

STRs near promoters can change packaging, transcription-factor binding or polymerase start, so repeat length can subtly change expression. Some help structure replication origins and recombination hotspots. Many are neutral but mark regions of possible instability.

Uses

  • The gold standard markers for forensics, ancestry, identity and paternity: they amplify by PCR even from tiny or degraded samples.
  • Forensic labs use a standard set: the 20 CODIS loci (FBI). The chance two unrelated people match is extremely low.
  • Research: human migration, linkage maps, tracking disease genes in families, population structure.
  • Cancer: microsatellite instability flags mismatch-repair defects.

The expanded repeat disrupts gene expression, causes abnormal protein aggregation, or interferes with cell processes.

In the clinic: microsatellite instability (MSI)

In some cancers, especially colorectal cancer in Lynch syndrome, defective mismatch repair lets STRs shorten and lengthen all over the genome. MSI is a diagnostic and prognostic marker. STR length can also affect normal traits (gene regulation, stress responses, immunity), so they are not “junk”.

Multiple-copy rRNA genes

Some genes are kept in many copies because the cell needs huge amounts of their product. The best example is the ribosomal RNA genes, repeated hundreds of times. They make RNA (not protein) for ribosomes, which cannot work without it.

Gene clusterLocationProducts
45S rRNA (tandem arrays)Short arms of acrocentric chromosomes 13, 14, 15, 21, 22One big precursor processed into 18S (→ 40S small subunit), 5.8S and 28S (→ 60S large subunit)
5S rRNAChromosome 15S (→ 60S large subunit)

Every cell needs millions of ribosomes, especially when growing. One copy of each gene could never make enough rRNA, so evolution made large repeated clusters.

LINEs (long interspersed nuclear elements)

  • About 6000 bp long; about 850,000 copies, only a few still able to move.
  • Main family: LINE-1 (L1), about 17–21% of the genome; one of the only currently active autonomous transposable elements in humans (it carries its own machinery).

How LINE-1 moves: retrotransposition (“copy and paste”)

  1. An active L1 is transcribed into RNA, which goes to the cytoplasm.
  2. Its two genes are translated: ORF1 (RNA-binding protein) and ORF2 (reverse transcriptase + endonuclease).
  3. The proteins bind their own RNA and carry it back into the nucleus.
  4. ORF2 cuts genomic DNA and uses the RNA as a template to make a new DNA copy right at the cut: target-primed reverse transcription.

Most copies are mutated and inactive, but a few still reshape the genome today.

In the clinic (slide 17 diagram; textbook examples)

A new L1 insertion can disrupt a gene: the classic example is hemophilia A from an L1 inserted into the factor VIII gene; L1 insertions have also caused Duchenne muscular dystrophy and some cancers.

SINEs and Alu

  • Usually < 500 bp; often > 1.5 million copies; encode no protein; remnants of mobile elements.
  • Main human SINE: the Alu element, about 300 bp, named after the AluI restriction site inside it; > 10% of the genome.
  • They come from small cellular RNAs such as 7SL RNA (from the signal recognition particle).
  • They spread by retrotransposition but are non-autonomous: they borrow LINE-1 ORF2 reverse transcriptase and endonuclease.

Function

  • Their abundance lets them affect chromatin, gene regulation and splicing, supplying promoters, polyadenylation signals, enhancers and transcription-factor sites.
  • Alu elements are CpG-rich and often methylated, so they shape epigenetic regulation; changes in their methylation alter chromatin and expression, especially in embryos.
  • From the textbook: Alu insertions and Alu–Alu recombination have caused diseases (e.g. some cases of neurofibromatosis and familial hypercholesterolaemia).

Pseudogenes

  • DNA sequences that closely resemble real genes but make no functional product, because of insertions, deletions or promoter defects.
  • Evolutionary remnants of past gene duplication or gene loss.
  • Useful for understanding genome evolution; may subtly influence regulation; can occasionally cause disease through abnormal recombination with the real gene.

Exam traps

Exam trap
  • Modern forensics uses STRs, not VNTRs.
  • SINEs can’t move alone; they need LINE-1 enzymes.
  • Retrotransposition goes through an RNA intermediate (copy and paste).
  • 18S rRNA belongs to the small (40S) subunit; 28S, 5.8S and 5S to the large (60S).
  • Alu is named after a restriction site (AluI).
  • MSI points to mismatch-repair defects (Lynch syndrome).

Key terms

Tandem repeat
Repeats arranged head-to-tail in a block.
VNTR / minisatellite
15–100 bp repeat units, variable number.
STR / microsatellite
2–6 bp repeat units.
Unequal crossing-over
Misaligned recombination changing repeat number.
Replication slippage
Polymerase misalignment adding/deleting repeats.
DNA fingerprinting
Identifying people by repeat-length patterns.
CODIS
The FBI’s standard set of 20 STR loci.
Microsatellite instability
Genome-wide STR length changes from mismatch-repair failure.
Retrotransposition
Moving via an RNA copy that is reverse-transcribed into DNA.
LINE-1
Autonomous long interspersed element.
Alu
~300 bp SINE, non-autonomous.
Pseudogene
Non-functional gene copy.
