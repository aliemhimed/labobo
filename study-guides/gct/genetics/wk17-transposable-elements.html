Transposable Elements (“Jumping Genes”)

DNA that moves: the structure and cut-and-paste mechanism of DNA transposons, the copy-and-paste retrotransposons (LTR and non-LTR: LINEs, SINEs), how much of our genome they make up, and the human diseases they have caused.

Labobo topic: MG Wk 17: Transposable Elements

In 60 seconds

  • Transposable elements (TEs) move within or between chromosomes; 50–10,000 bp; found in all organisms; nearly 50% of the human genome.
  • DNA transposons: move as DNA, cut and paste; inverted terminal repeats (9–40 bp) + transposase gene; leave direct repeats in host DNA. Only ~3% of our genome.
  • Retrotransposons: RNA intermediate, copy and paste (original stays); reverse transcriptase + integrase; ~42% of our genome (maize up to 78%).
  • Two groups: LTR (look like retroviruses) and non-LTR (LINEs autonomous, SINEs non-autonomous). LINEs + SINEs ≈ 34% of our genome.
  • Coding DNA ≈ 1%: 40–50× more TE DNA than gene DNA.
  • Disease: LINE in factor VIII (hemophilia A), dystrophin (DMD), APC/c-MYC (cancer); ≥ 11 LINE diseases. Alu in BRCA2, factor IX, cholinesterase, NF1; > 30 Alu diseases.

Learning objectives

  • Define transposable elements and why they are called jumping genes.
  • Distinguish DNA transposons and retrotransposons, and describe their structure and mechanisms.
  • Identify the major TEs in the human genome.
  • Explain how TEs cause mutation and disease, and their evolutionary significance.

What transposable elements are

  • Transposable elements (TEs), “jumping genes”, are DNA sequences that move to new positions in the same or another chromosome.
  • Size 50–10,000 bp; present in all organisms, from bacteria to humans (an ancient origin).
  • Nearly 50% of the human genome comes from TEs; in salamanders and barley they can reach 85%.

Effects

  • Some genes probably evolved from TEs; TEs have restructured genomes over evolution.
  • Insertions can disrupt genes, cause mutations, cause chromosome damage (double-strand breaks), and act as sites for rearrangement when similar TE copies recombine.

Research tools

Used as mutagens (to disrupt genes), as cloning tags (to locate genes) and as vectors to put foreign DNA into model organisms.

DNA transposons

  • Move directly as DNA, with no RNA step.
  • Found in bacteria, fungi, plants and animals, including humans (most human copies are no longer active).
  • The element is physically excised and inserted elsewhere, so both donor and recipient sites can change.
  • Movement can disrupt genes or regulatory regions, cause rearrangements, and drive instability and evolution.

Cut-and-paste mechanism

  1. Transposase binds the ITRs at both ends and brings them together into a complex.
  2. It makes double-strand breaks at both junctions → the transposon is released (often staying bound to transposase).
  3. Transposase makes staggered cuts at a new target site, leaving short single-stranded overhangs.
  4. The transposon is inserted into the cut site.
  5. DNA polymerase fills the gaps and ligase seals them → short direct repeats flank the insertion (the hallmark of transposition).
  6. The donor site is usually repaired cleanly; inaccurate repair can leave a mutation there.

Retrotransposons

  • Move via an RNA intermediate: “copy and paste”. The original stays; new copies go elsewhere, so copy number keeps rising and genomes expand.
  • They resemble retroviruses (both use reverse transcriptase), but don’t make all the proteins for infectious particles, so they can’t leave the cell.
  • Abundance: up to 78% of the maize genome; about 42% of the human genome (vs ~3% DNA transposons).

Like DNA transposons, they can be autonomous or non-autonomous; they encode reverse transcriptase and integrase, and their insertions are flanked by short direct repeats.

Copy-and-paste mechanism

  1. Transcription: the cell’s RNA polymerase makes RNA copies of the element.
  2. Translation: the RNA makes reverse transcriptase and integrase.
  3. Reverse transcription: RNA → double-stranded DNA.
  4. Integration: integrase inserts the DNA at a new site with staggered cuts; repair makes flanking direct repeats.

One element can make many RNAs → many new insertions, while the original remains. So retrotransposons drive genome expansion and are a major source of mutation.

TEs in humans and disease

  • Almost 50% of the human genome is TE-derived. LINEs + SINEs (non-LTR) ≈ 34%; other families ≈ 11%.
  • Protein-coding sequence ≈ 1%, so there is 40–50 times more TE DNA than gene DNA.
  • Most human TEs are inactive, but recent insertions can still disrupt genes.
In the clinic: proof that TEs move in humans

A boy with hemophilia A (X-linked, factor VIII) had a LINE inserted in his factor VIII gene. The insertion was absent from both of his unaffected mother’s X chromosomes, but the source LINE was on chromosome 22 in both parents. So the LINE jumped during the mother’s gametogenesis and was passed to her son: direct evidence of TE movement in humans.

TEs, mutation and evolution

The last slide is a diagram. In short: TEs are a natural source of mutation; their insertions and the recombination between copies rearrange genomes, add regulatory sequences and new exons, and expand genome size. This has shaped genome evolution, while also causing disease when a gene is hit.

Exam traps

Exam trap
  • Direct repeats are created by insertion, not carried by the transposon.
  • Retrotransposition keeps the original copy (copy and paste).
  • In humans, retrotransposons (~42%) far outnumber DNA transposons (~3%).
  • The hemophilia A LINE came from chromosome 22 and jumped in the mother’s germ line.
  • Alu (a SINE) caused the BRCA2 insertion, not a LINE.

Key terms

Transposable element
DNA sequence that can move in the genome.
DNA transposon
TE that moves as DNA by cut and paste.
Retrotransposon
TE that moves via RNA by copy and paste.
Transposase
Enzyme that excises and inserts DNA transposons.
Integrase
Enzyme inserting retrotransposon DNA.
Inverted terminal repeats
Opposite-facing repeats at transposon ends.
Direct repeats
Host sequence duplications flanking an insertion.
LTR
Long terminal repeat of retrovirus-like elements.
Autonomous / non-autonomous
Encodes / lacks its own transposition enzymes.
