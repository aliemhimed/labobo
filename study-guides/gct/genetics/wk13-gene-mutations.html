Gene Mutations: Types, Effects and Repair

What a gene mutation is, the three ways to classify one (by molecular change, by effect on function, by the cell it occurs in), and a first look at DNA repair and transposition.

Labobo topic: MG Wk 13: Gene Mutations and DNA Repair

In 60 seconds

  • A gene mutation = any change in the nucleotide sequence; the source of new alleles, of evolution, and of disease and cancer.
  • Point mutations: missense (new amino acid), nonsense (stop codon UAA, UAG, UGA), silent (same amino acid).
  • Transition = purine↔purine or pyrimidine↔pyrimidine; transversion = purine↔pyrimidine.
  • Insertions/deletions not divisible by 3 → frameshift: every downstream codon changes, often an early stop.
  • Loss-of-function (usually recessive; dominant by dominant-negative or haploinsufficiency, e.g. Marfan). Gain-of-function: usually dominant. Suppressor: second mutation that cancels the first.
  • Somatic = not inherited; germ-line = inherited, in every cell of the child.
  • Repair: mismatch repair (replication errors), nucleotide excision repair (UV thymine dimers). Transposons: cut-and-paste vs retrotransposons.

What a mutation is

  • A gene mutation is any change in the nucleotide sequence: a single base substitution, an insertion or deletion of nucleotides, or larger changes.
  • Without mutations there would be no variation, adaptation or evolution. They are the main source of new alleles.
  • Downside: they can cause cell death, genetic disease and cancer.
  • Mutations are also the tool of genetic analysis: different phenotypes let us find, map and follow genes. Mendel could not have found his laws if all peas looked alike.
  • Earlier lectures covered chromosomal mutations (large segments). This lecture covers gene mutations: single base pairs or small regions.
  • They can be in coding regions (change the protein) or non-coding regions such as introns and regulatory sequences (change expression). Most mutations land in non-coding DNA and have no effect.

Point mutations

A point mutation (base substitution) replaces one base pair with another.

Memory hook

Transition stays in the same class (like a train staying on the same line). Transversion crosses over between purines (A, G: “Pure As Gold”) and pyrimidines (C, T, U).

Insertions, deletions and frameshifts

  • Adding or removing nucleotides in a number not divisible by 3 shifts the reading frame: a frameshift.
  • Translation reads codons in threes, so every codon downstream changes, giving many wrong amino acids and often a premature stop (UAA, UAG, UGA).
  • Frameshifts are among the most damaging mutations, especially near the start of a gene, where they prevent essential domains forming.
  • An insertion or deletion of 3 (or a multiple of 3) bases adds or removes whole amino acids but keeps the frame (in-frame).

Example: THE CAT ATE THE RAT → delete the first E → THC ATA TET HER AT… (every word after the deletion changes).

Effect on gene function

Loss-of-function

  • Reduces or eliminates the activity of the gene product. Can result from any change, from one base to deletion of the whole gene. Total loss = null mutation.
  • Most are recessive: one working allele makes enough product.
  • Some act dominant:
    • Dominant-negative: the mutant product interferes with the normal one (e.g. a faulty subunit joins and spoils a protein complex).
    • Haploinsufficiency: one working allele is not enough. Example: Marfan syndrome, one faulty FBN1 copy lowers fibrillin-1 below the needed threshold.

Gain-of-function

The product has enhanced, altered or new activity, or the gene is expressed too much or in the wrong tissue. Because the mutant allele actively does something, these are usually dominant.

Suppressor mutations

A second mutation that cancels the effect of the first.

TypeWhereExample
IntragenicSame geneAn insertion near a frameshift deletion restores the frame; or a second base change restores the original (or a similar) amino acid
IntergenicDifferent geneA change in a partner protein lets it bind the mutated protein again, restoring the complex or pathway

Overall: some mutations have no effect (non-essential genes); others cause mild to severe disease. Recessive mutations are hidden by a working allele; dominant ones (dominant-negative, haploinsufficiency, gain-of-function) show in heterozygotes.

Somatic vs germ-line mutations

SomaticGerm-line
CellAny body cell not making gametesCells that give rise to eggs or sperm
Passed on?No; affects only the individualYes; present in every cell of the child
EffectRecessive autosomal ones usually show nothing (other allele works); can cause tumoursInherited disease, diversity, evolution

Germ-line: autosomal vs sex-linked

  • Autosomal dominant: expressed immediately in the next generation (one copy is enough).
  • Autosomal recessive: may stay hidden for generations in carriers; appears when two carriers have a child who inherits two copies.
  • X-linked recessive: a new mutation in a mother’s egg can show in her sons, who have one X and nothing to mask it. Females usually need two copies.

DNA repair (introduction)

DNA is constantly hit by replication errors, chemicals and environmental stress. DNA repair detects and corrects damage; without it mutations would pile up and threaten survival, development and health.

PathwayFixesHow
Mismatch repair (MMR)Replication errors that escaped proofreading: wrong base pairs, small insertion–deletion loopsFinds the mismatch on the new strand and replaces it
Nucleotide excision repair (NER)Bulky damage such as UV thymine dimersCuts out a short stretch of nucleotides and re-synthesises it

Defects: MMR → Lynch syndrome (microsatellite instability); NER → xeroderma pigmentosum (Week 16).

Transposition (introduction)

Transposition = movement of transposable elements from one chromosomal site to another. Inserting into a new site can disrupt a gene or change its expression, so it is a natural source of mutation and genome evolution.

TypeHow it movesExample
DNA transposons“Cut and paste”: transposase cuts the element out and inserts it elsewhereBacterial IS elements, maize Ac/Ds
RetrotransposonsThrough an RNA intermediate: transcribed, reverse-transcribed into DNA, inserted (copy and paste)Human LINEs and SINEs

Intragenic vs intergenic suppressorSame gene vs different gene.
MMR vs NERReplication mismatches vs bulky lesions (UV dimers).

Exam traps

Exam trap
  • A deletion of 3 bases does not cause a frameshift.
  • Silent mutations can still cause disease if they disrupt splicing.
  • Most loss-of-function mutations are recessive; gain-of-function are usually dominant.
  • Marfan syndrome = haploinsufficiency of FBN1.
  • Somatic mutations are not inherited even if they cause cancer.
  • LINEs and SINEs are retrotransposons, not cut-and-paste.

Key terms

Gene mutation
Change in the nucleotide sequence of a gene.
Point mutation
Substitution of one base pair.
Missense / nonsense / silent
New amino acid / stop codon / same amino acid.
Transition / transversion
Same-class / cross-class base change.
Frameshift
Insertion or deletion not a multiple of 3, shifting the reading frame.
Null mutation
Complete loss of function.
Dominant-negative
Mutant product interferes with the normal product.
Haploinsufficiency
One normal copy cannot maintain the normal phenotype.
Gain-of-function
New or increased activity.
Suppressor mutation
Second mutation cancelling the first.
Germ-line / somatic
Inherited / not inherited mutation.
Transposable element
DNA sequence that can move in the genome.
