Incomplete Dominance, Codominance and the ABO System

When alleles don’t follow simple dominance: intermediate heterozygotes (pink flowers, Tay–Sachs carriers), both alleles expressed side by side (MN and ABO blood groups), and how a second gene can hide ABO completely (the Bombay phenotype).

Labobo topic: MG Wk 2: Dominance, Codominance & ABO

In 60 seconds

  • Incomplete dominance: heterozygote is intermediate (red × white → pink), because of gene dosage.
  • Codominance: heterozygote shows both products side by side (MN, AB blood types). Ratio 1 : 2 : 1 for genotype and phenotype.
  • Tay–Sachs: HEXA (chromosome 15q23–q24), Hex A degrades GM2. Carriers have ~50% activity: biochemically intermediate but clinically normal (threshold effect).
  • ABO gene on 9q34; alleles Iᴬ, Iᴮ (codominant) and i (null, recessive) = multiple alleles.
  • A antigen = H substance + GalNAc; B antigen = H + galactose; O = H only.
  • Bombay (hh): no FUT1 → no H substance → no A or B; has anti-A, anti-B and anti-H; can receive only Bombay blood. An example of epistasis.

Learning objectives

Course textbook, Chapter 4, pages 62–68. By the end you can:

  • define incomplete dominance and contrast it with complete dominance;
  • describe Tay–Sachs disease as biochemical incomplete dominance;
  • define codominance and distinguish it from incomplete dominance;

  • explain the Bombay phenotype (FUT1, no H substance) and its transfusion consequences.

Incomplete (partial) dominance

In Mendel’s crosses one allele was fully dominant. In incomplete dominance the heterozygote has a phenotype between the two homozygotes, not like either parent.

Molecular explanation: gene dosage

GenotypeEnzyme for red pigment (anthocyanin)Flower
R¹R¹Two working copies → high pigmentRed
R¹R²One working copy → about half the pigmentPink
R²R²R² is loss-of-function → no pigmentWhite

So the heterozygote’s phenotype is proportional to the amount of working enzyme. Crossing two pink plants gives red : pink : white = 1 : 2 : 1 (phenotype matches genotype).

Tay–Sachs disease: incomplete dominance at the biochemical level

FeatureDetails
InheritanceAutosomal recessive: both alleles must be mutated for disease
GeneHEXA, chromosome 15q23–q24
ProteinThe α-subunit of β-hexosaminidase A (Hex A)
Normal jobHex A degrades GM2 ganglioside in lysosomes, especially in neurons
MutationsFrameshift, nonsense, splice-site or missense: abolish or greatly reduce Hex A

Enzyme activity by genotype

GenotypeHex A activityResult
HEXA⁺/HEXA⁺100%Normal; GM2 degraded
HEXA⁺/HEXA⁻ (carrier)~50%Biochemically intermediate, clinically normal
HEXA⁻/HEXA⁻~0%GM2 builds up in neurons → lysosomal swelling, cell dysfunction
The threshold effect

Many enzyme processes need only part of full activity. For Tay–Sachs the threshold for normal nerve function is below 50%, so carriers have no symptoms. So the disease is recessive clinically but shows incomplete dominance biochemically. In other disorders the threshold may be higher, and carriers may have mild symptoms.

Codominance and the MN system

In codominance, both alleles of a gene are expressed independently and at the same time in the heterozygote. Both products are made and can be detected. There is no blending.

PatternHeterozygote
Complete dominanceLooks like the dominant homozygote
Incomplete dominanceIntermediate, blended (pink)
CodominanceShows both traits fully, side by side (MN, AB)

The MN blood group

  • Discovered by Karl Landsteiner and Philip Levine; based on two forms of a glycoprotein antigen on red cells.
  • Locus on chromosome 4; two alleles Lᴹ and Lᴺ.
  • MN × MN gives M : MN : N = 1 : 2 : 1 for both genotype and phenotype.
  • Both M and N glycoproteins are detected on MN red cells: an unambiguous example of codominance.

Codominance features: equal, parallel expression of both alleles; each product separately detectable; genotype and phenotype ratios both 1 : 2 : 1.

The ABO system

ABO blood groups are carbohydrate antigens on the red cell surface; they decide transfusion and transplant compatibility.

  • ABO gene on chromosome 9 (9q34), with three main alleles:
    • Iᴬ → A antigen
    • Iᴮ → B antigen
    • i → no antigen (null allele)
  • Iᴬ and Iᴮ are codominant; each is dominant over i. So ABO shows codominance and multiple alleles.
GenotypeAntigen on RBCBlood typeAntibodies in plasma
IᴬIᴬ or IᴬiAAAnti-B
IᴮIᴮ or IᴮiBBAnti-A
IᴬIᴮA and BABNone
iiNone (H substance only)OAnti-A and anti-B
In the clinic: transfusion

Plasma naturally contains antibodies against any ABO antigen missing from your own red cells. A mismatched transfusion causes agglutination and hemolysis. Example: a type A patient (anti-B) cannot receive B or AB blood. ABO typing is also used in organ transplantation and forensic identification.

How A and B antigens are made

A and B antigens are terminal sugars on glycolipids and glycoproteins sticking out of the red cell membrane. They are added to a common precursor, the H substance.

  • H substance: an oligosaccharide chain containing galactose, N-acetylglucosamine and fucose. The fucose is added by a fucosyltransferase encoded by FUT1 (the H gene).
AlleleEnzymeAdds to HResult
IᴬN-acetylgalactosaminyltransferaseGalNAcA antigen
IᴮGalactosyltransferaseGalactoseB antigen
iNone (usually loss-of-function)NothingH stays unmodified → O

In an IᴬIᴮ person both enzymes act independently on the many H sites, so some molecules get GalNAc (A) and others galactose (B): codominance at the molecular level.

Memory hook

A gets the Amino sugar (N-acetylgalactosamine); B gets plain galactose.

The Bombay phenotype

First identified in Mumbai (Bombay) in 1952; an example of epistasis (one gene masking another).

  1. Normal people have at least one working FUT1 (H) allele → H substance made.
  2. People homozygous for non-working FUT1 (hh) make no H substance.
  3. With no H, the Iᴬ and Iᴮ enzymes have no substrate to add sugars to.
  4. No A or B antigens appear, even if the person carries Iᴬ or Iᴮ: they type as O, though their genotype may be IᴬIᴬ, IᴮIᴮ or IᴬIᴮ.
In the clinic
  • Plasma contains anti-A, anti-B and anti-H.
  • They cannot receive any normal blood, even type O, because all normal types carry H antigen.
  • They can receive blood only from another Bombay donor.
  • Frequency: about 1 in 10,000 in India and 1 in a million elsewhere.

Exam traps

Exam trap
  • Tay–Sachs is autosomal recessive clinically; “incomplete dominance” refers only to enzyme levels.
  • Type AB has no ABO antibodies; type O has both.
  • A Bombay person can’t receive O blood.
  • Bombay is epistasis, not codominance.
  • Hex A’s gene HEXA codes the α-subunit.
  • In codominance the phenotypic ratio is 1 : 2 : 1, not 3 : 1.

Key terms

Incomplete dominance
Heterozygote intermediate between homozygotes.
Codominance
Both alleles fully expressed in the heterozygote.
Gene dosage
Phenotype depends on how many working copies are present.
Loss-of-function allele
Allele whose product does not work.
Threshold effect
Only part of full enzyme activity is needed for normal function.
Hex A
β-Hexosaminidase A, degrades GM2 ganglioside.
Multiple alleles
More than two alleles of one gene in a population.
Null allele
Allele that makes no product (i in ABO).
H substance
Fucose-containing precursor of A and B antigens.
FUT1
Fucosyltransferase gene that makes H substance.
Bombay phenotype
hh; no H, A or B antigens.
Epistasis
One gene masking the expression of another.
Agglutination
Clumping of red cells by antibodies.
