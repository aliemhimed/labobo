Breast and diaphragm

The pectoral region and the breast (structure, vessels, nerves, lymph drainage, clinical points), the pectoral muscles and clavipectoral fascia, and the diaphragm: attachments, openings, crura, vessels, nerve supply and its movement in breathing.

Labobo topic: Anatomy Lec 15: Breast & Diaphragm

In 60 seconds

  • The breast is mammary gland, superficial fascia and skin, in the pectoral region. Its base runs from rib II to rib VI and from the sternum to the midaxillary line.
  • 15 to 20 lactiferous ducts open on the nipple. Cooper's ligaments support the breast; cancer pulls on them and causes skin pitting.
  • About 75% of lymph goes to the axillary nodes; most of the rest to parasternal nodes. The nipple is supplied by the fourth intercostal nerve.
  • Pectoral muscles: pectoralis major, pectoralis minor, subclavius.
  • The diaphragm seals the inferior thoracic aperture. Right dome is higher (to rib V). Openings: IVC at TVIII (central tendon), esophagus at TX (with vagi), aorta at TXII (with thoracic duct).
  • Nerve supply: phrenic nerves C3, C4, C5, entering from the abdominal surface. Largest arteries: inferior phrenic (from the abdominal aorta).

Pectoral region

The pectoral region lies outside the anterior thoracic wall and helps anchor the upper limb to the trunk. It has two compartments.

  • Superficial: skin, superficial fascia and the breasts.
  • Deep: muscles and associated structures.

Nerves, vessels and lymphatics of the superficial compartment emerge from the thoracic wall, the axilla and the neck.

Breast structure

Each breast is made of mammary glands, superficial fascia and overlying skin, on the anterior thoracic wall.

  • The mammary glands are modified sweat glands in the superficial fascia, in front of the pectoral muscles and thoracic wall.
  • They are made of ducts and secretory lobules. These converge to form 15 to 20 lactiferous ducts, each opening separately on the nipple. A circular pigmented skin area, the areola, surrounds the nipple.
  • A connective tissue stroma surrounds the ducts and lobules. In places it condenses into the suspensory ligaments of the breast (Cooper's ligaments), which are continuous with the dermis and support the breast.
  • In non-lactating women the breast is mostly fat. In lactating women glandular tissue is more abundant.
  • The breast lies on the deep fascia of pectoralis major and neighbouring muscles. A layer of loose connective tissue, the retromammary space, separates it from the deep fascia and lets it move over the muscle.
  • The base extends vertically from rib II to rib VI and transversely from the sternum to the midaxillary line.

Watch out

Breast skin sensation comes from the intercostal nerves, but the range differs. The slide and Gray's p. 140 say the 2nd to 6th intercostal nerves. Gray's p. 133 says the lateral and anterior branches of the 4th to 6th intercostal nerves carry general sensation from the breast skin. Use 2nd to 6th, as on the slide. The nipple is the 4th in both.

Lymphatic drainage

  1. About 75% drains laterally and superiorly to the axillary nodes.
  2. Most of the rest drains to the parasternal nodes, beside the internal thoracic artery, deep to the anterior chest wall. Vessels of the medial breast follow the perforating arteries to these nodes.
  3. Some drains along the lateral branches of the posterior intercostal arteries to intercostal nodes near the heads and necks of the ribs.

Breast in men and clinical points

  • The breast in men is rudimentary: small ducts, often cords of cells, that normally do not go beyond the areola. Breast cancer can occur in men.
In the clinic: skin changes in breast cancer
  • Carcinoma creates tension on the suspensory ligaments, causing pitting of the skin.
  • Subcutaneous lymphatic obstruction and tumour growth pull on the ligaments, giving an orange-peel texture (peau d'orange).
  • Further subcutaneous spread can give a rare hard, woody skin texture: cancer en cuirasse.
In the clinic: mastectomy

In the axilla, breast tissue must be removed from the medial axillary wall. The long thoracic nerve lies closely against this wall. Damage causes paralysis of serratus anterior and a winged scapula. The nerve to latissimus dorsi can also be damaged, which may affect extension, medial rotation and adduction of the humerus.

Pectoral muscles and clavipectoral fascia

The pectoral region holds pectoralis major, pectoralis minor and subclavius. All arise from the anterior thoracic wall and insert on bones of the upper limb.

Clavipectoral fascia

  • A thick connective tissue sheet from the clavicle to the floor of the axilla. It encloses subclavius and pectoralis minor and spans the gap between them.
  • Structures cross between the axilla and the anterior axillary wall either between pectoralis minor and subclavius or below pectoralis minor.
  • Passing between subclavius and pectoralis minor: the cephalic vein, the thoraco-acromial artery and the lateral pectoral nerve.

Diaphragm: form and attachments

  • The musculotendinous diaphragm seals the inferior thoracic aperture and separates the thoracic and abdominal cavities.
  • Muscle fibres arise radially from the margins of the aperture and converge on a large central tendon. The pericardium attaches to the middle of the central tendon.
  • Peripheral attachments: xiphoid process, costal margin, ends of ribs XI and XII, ligaments across the posterior abdominal wall, and lumbar vertebrae.
  • Because the thoracic aperture is oblique, the posterior attachment lies lower than the anterior one. In the median sagittal plane it slopes down from the xiphoid (about TVIII/IX) to the median arcuate ligament, in front of the aorta at about TXII.
  • It is not flat: it forms domes on each side. The right dome is higher, reaching rib V.
  • When it contracts, the domes drop, and the volume of the thorax increases.
  • The esophagus and IVC penetrate the diaphragm; the aorta passes behind it.

Other structures lateral to the aortic hiatus, outside the posterior attachments, include the sympathetic trunks. The greater, lesser and least splanchnic nerves penetrate the crura.

Watch out

The table on slide 30 lists the right phrenic nerve in the caval hiatus and the azygos vein in the aortic hiatus. Gray's (p. 163-164) and the text of slide 29 give only the IVC through the central tendon, and say only that the azygos and hemiazygos veins may pass through the aortic hiatus or through the crura. The phrenic nerves are not listed in an opening there; they penetrate the diaphragm and supply it from its abdominal surface. For the exam, use the book wording: IVC alone at TVIII; azygos only as "may pass" at TXII. If a question follows the slide table, expect the slide version.

Crura

  • The crura are musculotendinous bundles attached to the anterior surface of the vertebral column, the intervertebral discs and the anterior longitudinal ligament.
  • Origin: lumbar vertebrae L1, L2, L3, and the medial and lateral arcuate ligaments. Insertion: central tendon.
  • The right crus is longer; the left is shorter.
  • The right and left crura join across the midline by the median arcuate ligament. The diaphragm also attaches to the medial and lateral arcuate ligaments.
In the clinic: median arcuate ligament syndrome (MALS)

A very rare disease in which the median arcuate ligament presses on the celiac artery and the nearby celiac plexus. Typical symptom: intense upper abdominal pain, usually after eating. Treatment is surgery to separate the ligament from the artery and plexus.

In the clinic: diaphragmatic paralysis

Gray's p. 165: phrenic nerve palsy raises the diaphragm on the affected side. Lung cancer infiltrating the nerve is the important cause not to overlook; others include postviral neuropathy, trauma, injury in thoracic surgery and compression of the C3-C5 roots. Most patients with one-sided paralysis have no symptoms. Both-sided paralysis is rare and causes significant respiratory distress.

Movements in breathing

The thoracic wall and diaphragm change thoracic volume to move air. The thorax changes in vertical, lateral and anteroposterior dimensions.

MovementWhat happens
VerticalThe diaphragm contracts and flattens (depression), increasing vertical size. It relaxes and rises (elevation)
Pump handle (anteroposterior)The anterior rib ends lie below the posterior ends. Raising the ribs moves the sternum up and forward; the angle between body and manubrium may become slightly less acute. Depressing the ribs moves it down and back
Bucket handle (lateral)The middle of each rib shaft lies lower than its ends. Raising the shafts moves the middles laterally, widening the thorax

Any muscle attached to the ribs can act as an accessory respiratory muscle. Neck and abdominal muscles can fix or alter the upper and lower ribs.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • The IVC goes through the diaphragm at TVIII; the aorta goes behind it at TXII.
  • The right dome is higher than the left.
  • The phrenic nerve enters from the abdominal surface. Its roots are C3-C5, not C1-C2.
  • The largest diaphragm arteries are the inferior phrenics, not the pericardiacophrenic.
  • The nipple is T4 (4th intercostal nerve).
  • 75% of lymph goes to the axillary nodes, not the parasternal nodes.
  • Long thoracic nerve injury gives a winged scapula (serratus anterior).
  • The posterior attachment of the diaphragm is lower than the anterior.

Key terms

Lactiferous duct
Duct draining a mammary gland lobe to the nipple; 15 to 20 per breast.
Areola
Pigmented skin round the nipple.
Suspensory ligaments (Cooper's)
Stromal bands joining the breast to the dermis.
Retromammary space
Loose tissue between the breast and deep fascia.
Peau d'orange
Orange-peel skin texture from lymphatic obstruction and tumour pull.
Cancer en cuirasse
Hard, woody skin from further subcutaneous spread.
Clavipectoral fascia
Fascial sheet enclosing subclavius and pectoralis minor.
Central tendon
Tendinous centre of the diaphragm, where the IVC passes.
Crus
Musculotendinous bundle of the diaphragm from the lumbar vertebrae.
Median arcuate ligament
Fibrous arch joining the crura in front of the aorta at about TXII.
Phrenic nerve
Nerve from C3-C5 that supplies the diaphragm.
Pump handle
Anteroposterior thoracic expansion by sternal movement.
Bucket handle
Lateral thoracic expansion by rib shaft elevation.
