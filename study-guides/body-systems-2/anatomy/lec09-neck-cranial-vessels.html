Neck and cranial vascular system

The jugular veins, the carotid arteries and their branches, the thyroid blood supply, the subclavian artery and its four branches, the arteries and veins of the pharynx and larynx, the circle of Willis, the dural arteries, the dural venous sinuses and the cavernous sinus.

Labobo topic: Anatomy Lec 9: Neck & Cranial Vascular System

In 60 seconds

  • External jugular vein = posterior division of the retromandibular vein + posterior auricular vein; it ends in the subclavian vein.
  • Right CCA from the brachiocephalic trunk; left CCA straight from the aortic arch. Neither gives branches in the neck. They divide at the upper edge of the thyroid cartilage.
  • Carotid sinus = pressure receptors (IX). Carotid body = oxygen and chemical receptors (IX and X).
  • ECA branches in order: superior thyroid, ascending pharyngeal, lingual, facial, occipital, posterior auricular, superficial temporal, maxillary.
  • Thyroid: superior thyroid (from the ECA) and inferior thyroid (from the thyrocervical trunk). Occasional thyroid ima.
  • Subclavian: vertebral, thyrocervical trunk, internal thoracic, costocervical trunk. Anterior scalene splits it into three parts.
  • Brain supply: 2 vertebral + 2 internal carotid arteries join as the circle of Willis.
  • Cavernous sinus: ICA and VI inside; III, IV, V1, V2 in the lateral wall. Facial vein → superior ophthalmic vein → cavernous sinus is the infection route.

Learning objectives

Syllabus, Week X: arteries and veins of the neck; cranial vascular system. Source: Gray's Anatomy for Students, 4th edition. By the end you can:

  • trace the jugular veins and the subclavian vein and their tributaries;
  • follow the carotid system and name every external carotid branch with what it supplies;
  • describe the thyroid blood supply, the subclavian branches and the vessels of the pharynx and larynx;
  • describe the arteries of the brain, the dural arteries, the dural venous sinuses and the cavernous sinus with its clinical importance.

External and anterior jugular veins

The external and anterior jugular veins are the main channels of superficial venous drainage of the neck.

  1. Superficial temporal + maxillary veins join in the parotid gland: retromandibular vein.
  2. At the angle of the mandible it divides into anterior and posterior divisions.
  3. Posterior division + posterior auricular vein (scalp behind and above the ear) = external jugular vein (EJV).
  4. Anterior division + facial vein = common facial vein, which passes deep and drains into the internal jugular vein (IJV).

Internal carotid artery

  • In the neck it ascends to the skull base and gives no branches. It enters the cranium through the carotid canal in the petrous temporal bone.
  • It supplies the cerebral hemispheres, the eyes and orbit, and the forehead.
  • In the skull it passes forward through the cavernous sinus. Beyond the sinus it gives: ophthalmic (orbit), posterior communicating (part of the circle of Willis), anterior choroidal (brain structures for motor control and vision) and anterior cerebral arteries. It then continues as the middle cerebral artery, supplying the lateral cerebrum.
Watch out

The deck says the ICA gives the ophthalmic, posterior communicating, anterior choroidal and anterior cerebral arteries, then continues as the middle cerebral artery. Gray's (p. 870) says it gives off the ophthalmic, posterior communicating, middle cerebral and anterior cerebral arteries, and does not list the anterior choroidal. Follow the deck; both agree the ICA ends by supplying the MCA.

Internal jugular vein

  • Collects blood from the skull, brain, superficial face and parts of the neck.
  • It begins as the dilated continuation of the sigmoid sinus; the first dilated part is the superior bulb, which soon receives the inferior petrosal sinus.
  • It leaves the skull through the jugular foramen with nerves IX, X and XI, and enters the carotid sheath.
  • In the neck it is first behind the ICA, then more lateral. It stays lateral to the CCA; the vagus nerve lies behind and partly between the two vessels.
  • IJV + subclavian vein (behind the sternal end of the clavicle) = brachiocephalic vein.
  • Tributaries: inferior petrosal sinus, facial, lingual, pharyngeal, occipital, superior thyroid and middle thyroid veins.
In the clinic

The jugular venous pulse lets the doctor judge venous pressure and waveform. It reflects the function of the right side of the heart.

Superior thyroidFirst branch of the ECA; along the lateral margin of thyrohyoid to the superior pole of the lateral lobeAnterior glandular branch: along the upper border; joins its opposite twin across the isthmus. Posterior glandular branch: to the back; may join the inferior thyroid
Inferior thyroidFrom the thyrocervical trunk (first part of the subclavian); up along the medial edge of anterior scalene; behind the carotid sheath; to the inferior poleInferior branch (joins the superior thyroid posterior branch); ascending branch (supplies the parathyroid glands). Also gives the ascending cervical artery to the prevertebral muscles and spinal cord
Thyroid imaOccasional and small; from the brachiocephalic trunk or aortic arch; up the front of the tracheaSupplies the thyroid
  • Veins: superior thyroid vein (area of the superior artery); middle and inferior thyroid veins (the rest). Superior and middle veins go to the IJV; the inferior thyroid veins go to the right and left brachiocephalic veins.
  • Lymph: paratracheal nodes and deep cervical nodes below omohyoid, along the IJV.
In the clinic: thyroidectomy
  • Partial or near-total removal of the gland, usually for multinodular goitre or thyroid cancer.
  • At risk: the parathyroid glands and the recurrent laryngeal nerve. The nerves lie close to the ligaments that bind the gland to the larynx and trachea, so the vocal folds are checked before and after surgery.
  • The recurrent laryngeal nerve branches from the vagus, loops round the subclavian artery (right) or aortic arch (left), and ascends in the groove between trachea and oesophagus behind the lateral lobe.
  • The parathyroids are two pairs on the deep surface of the lateral lobes, supplied by the inferior thyroid arteries.
Watch out

The inferior thyroid artery is "along the medial edge of anterior scalene" on one slide and "anterior to anterior scalene" on another. Gray's says both (pp. 1008 and 1021). It is described differently in different places; learn it as ascending on the front of anterior scalene near its medial edge, then turning medially behind the carotid sheath.

Watch out

The deck calls the deep branch of the transverse cervical artery the dorsal scapular artery (and the transverse cervical the "cervicodorsal trunk"). Gray's (p. 1015) describes the dorsal scapular artery as a single branch that may arise from the 3rd part of the subclavian, and does not use the other names. Follow the deck for this lecture; know that the book treats it differently.

Posterior triangle and subclavian vein

  • Contents of the posterior triangle. Arteries: suprascapular, transverse cervical, 3rd part of the subclavian, part of the occipital. Vein: external jugular. Nerves: spinal accessory, brachial plexus, cervical plexus.
  • The omohyoid inferior belly crosses the lower triangle and divides it into a small omoclavicular (subclavian) triangle and a larger occipital triangle (Gray's).
  • The subclavian vein continues the axillary vein from the lateral border of rib I, passes in front of anterior scalene, and joins the IJV to form the brachiocephalic vein. It lies in front of and slightly below the subclavian artery.
  • The EJV and possibly the suprascapular and transverse cervical veins enter it.
Watch out

The deck (S42) and Gray's p. 1016 say the EJV and possibly the suprascapular and transverse cervical veins enter the subclavian vein. Slide 6 and Gray's p. 993 call the transverse cervical and suprascapular veins tributaries of the EJV. Gray's p. 1022 says the only tributary of the subclavian vein is the EJV. Gray's disagrees with itself; follow the deck: they join either the EJV or the first part of the subclavian vein.

Arteries of the brain

  • Two pairs of vessels, the vertebral and internal carotid arteries, interconnect in the cranium as the cerebral arterial circle (circle of Willis). It gives collateral circulation: if one part or feeding artery is blocked or narrowed, the others may keep perfusion good enough to avoid symptoms.
  • The vertebral arteries enter through the foramen magnum and fuse just below the pons into the basilar artery. The ICAs enter through the carotid canals.
ArteryKey points
Vertebral (intracranial)Small meningeal branch on entering. Then three branches before joining its twin: posterior inferior cerebellar artery (first), posterior spinal artery (down the back of the cord at the posterior roots; one each side), and a branch that joins its twin to make the single anterior spinal artery (in the anterior median fissure). Gray's: posterior spinal arteries more often come from the PICAs.
BasilarRuns along the front of the pons. Branches caudal to rostral: anterior inferior cerebellar arteries, small pontine arteries, superior cerebellar arteries. Ends by dividing into the two posterior cerebral arteries.
Circle of WillisAnterior cerebral arteries (joined by the anterior communicating), ICAs at their tips, posterior communicating arteries (ICA to posterior cerebral), posterior cerebral arteries (from the basilar).
Watch out

The slide on the circle of Willis says the middle cerebral arteries are also considered part of it. Gray's (p. 871) lists only the vertebrobasilar and internal carotid systems joined by the anterior communicating and two posterior communicating arteries; it does not include the MCA. Follow the deck, but for an exam about the classic circle, the connecting arteries are the three communicating arteries.

In the clinic: stroke, endarterectomy, aneurysm
  • Stroke: interrupted blood flow to the brain with deficit lasting more than 24 hours; under 24 hours is a TIA. It is ischaemic (thrombotic or embolic; embolic is the commonest, often from carotid plaque) or haemorrhagic.
  • Risk factors: diabetes, hypertension, smoking. Signs: sudden weakness or sensory loss on one side, visual field loss, dysarthria, ataxia, reduced consciousness. Thrombolytics help if given within 3 to 4.5 hours; CT comes first to exclude haemorrhage.
  • Endarterectomy removes atheromatous plaque, which tends to form at bifurcations and can embolise.
  • Aneurysms arise around the circle of Willis: anterior and posterior communicating arteries, MCA branches, basilar tip, PICA. Rupture gives a thunderclap headache, neck stiffness and vomiting; CT shows subarachnoid blood. Treatment: clipping the neck or coil embolisation by catheter from the femoral artery.

Veins of the brain and dural venous sinuses

  • Brain veins start as small networks, join larger cerebral, cerebellar and brainstem veins, and empty into dural venous sinuses.
  • The sinuses are endothelium-lined spaces between the outer periosteal and inner meningeal layers of the dura. They lead to the IJVs.
  • Diploic veins run between the inner and outer tables of the skull roof. Emissary veins link extracranial veins with the sinuses; they have no valves, so they can carry infection into the cranium.

Cavernous sinus

  • Paired, in the middle cranial fossa on each side of the sella turcica, which holds the pituitary gland.
  • It receives cerebral veins, ophthalmic veins and emissary veins from the pterygoid plexus, so infection can pass from outside to inside the skull.
PositionStructures
Through the sinusInternal carotid artery and the abducent nerve [VI]
Lateral wall, top to bottomOculomotor [III], trochlear [IV], ophthalmic [V1], maxillary [V2]
In the clinic
  • The cavernous sinus connects to the facial vein through the superior ophthalmic vein. A facial injury in the danger area can cause cavernous sinus infection.
  • Thrombophlebitis of the facial vein can send clot into the sinus, giving cavernous sinus thrombosis; infection may then spread to other dural sinuses. Bursting pimples and facial lacerations are sources.
  • The ICA is the only artery in the body that runs completely through a venous structure. If it ruptures in the sinus, a carotid-cavernous fistula forms.
  • Inflammation may affect single nerves or all nerves in the sinus.
  • Danger triangle of the face: from the bridge of the nose to the corners of the mouth.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • The CCA and ICA give no branches in the neck; the first branch of the ECA is the superior thyroid.
  • Do not mix up the lingual artery (deep to XII) with the facial artery (deep to stylohyoid and the posterior belly of digastric).
  • The ascending pharyngeal is the smallest ECA branch; the maxillary is the larger terminal branch.
  • The posterior division of the retromandibular vein forms the EJV; the anterior division forms the common facial vein.
  • The right costocervical trunk comes from the 2nd part of the subclavian; the left from the 1st part.
  • The vertebral artery enters at CVI, not CVII.
  • Inferior thyroid veins drain to the brachiocephalic veins, not the IJV.
  • The abducent nerve runs inside the cavernous sinus; the other nerves lie in its wall.
  • Emissary veins are dangerous because they have no valves.

Key terms

Carotid sheath: fascial sleeve around CCA, IJV and vagus · Carotid triangle: part of the anterior triangle containing the bifurcation · Carotid sinus: pressure receptor dilation at the bifurcation · Carotid body: chemoreceptor organ at the bifurcation · Retromandibular vein: formed in the parotid from the superficial temporal and maxillary veins · Thyrocervical trunk: gives inferior thyroid, transverse cervical and suprascapular arteries · Costocervical trunk: gives deep cervical and supreme intercostal arteries · Thyroid ima: occasional small thyroid artery · Circle of Willis: arterial ring at the base of the brain · Basilar artery: formed by the two vertebral arteries · Dural venous sinus: blood channel between dural layers · Emissary vein: valveless vein linking scalp and sinuses · Diploic vein: vein in the skull bone between its tables · Cavernous sinus: sinus beside the sella turcica containing the ICA and VI · Jugulodigastric node: node where the facial vein meets the IJV · TIA: stroke symptoms resolving within 24 hours · Endarterectomy: surgical removal of plaque from an artery.
