Vessels of the lower limb

The arteries from the femoral artery to the plantar arch, the gluteal and obturator arteries, the hip and knee anastomoses, the superficial and deep veins, the lymph nodes of the limb, and the deep fascia with the saphenous opening. Clinical links: varicose veins, deep vein thrombosis, avascular necrosis and claudication.

Labobo topic: Anatomy Lec 8: Vessels of the Lower Limb

In 60 seconds

  • External iliac artery → femoral artery under the inguinal ligament → popliteal artery at the adductor hiatus → divides into anterior and posterior tibial arteries.
  • Femoral triangle, lateral to medial: nerve, artery, vein, femoral canal (lymphatics). The femoral pulse is midway between the ASIS and the pubic symphysis.
  • The deep artery of the thigh is the main supply of the thigh. Its medial circumflex femoral branch is the key supply of the femoral neck and head.
  • Superior gluteal artery leaves the pelvis above piriformis; inferior gluteal artery below it. The obturator artery uses the obturator canal.
  • Foot: the lateral plantar artery forms the deep plantar arch and joins the dorsalis pedis between metatarsals I and II.
  • Great saphenous vein: medial dorsal venous arch → saphenous opening → femoral vein. Small saphenous vein: lateral arch → popliteal vein.
  • Superficial inguinal nodes (about 10) and deep inguinal nodes (up to 3) drain to the external iliac nodes; popliteal nodes drain to the inguinal nodes.

Learning objectives

Syllabus, Week IX: subcutaneous tissue and fascia; venous drainage of the lower limb; lymphatic drainage of the lower limb. Source: Gray's Anatomy for Students, lower limb chapter. By the end you can:

  • describe the deep fascia (fascia lata, iliotibial tract) and the saphenous opening;
  • trace the superficial and deep veins of the limb and explain varicose veins and DVT;
  • name the lymph node groups of the limb and what each one drains;
  • follow the arteries from the gluteal region to the sole and find the four pulse points.

Arterial supply: overview

The main artery of the limb is the femoral artery, the continuation of the external iliac artery. The external iliac becomes the femoral artery as it passes under the inguinal ligament into the femoral triangle. Its branches supply most of the thigh and all of the leg and foot.

Other vessels reach the limb from the pelvis as branches of the internal iliac artery: the superior and inferior gluteal arteries and the obturator artery. Together with branches of the internal pudendal artery, they form an anastomotic network in the upper thigh and gluteal region. This network can give collateral circulation if one vessel is blocked.

  1. External iliac artery (abdomen)
  2. Femoral artery (under the inguinal ligament, femoral triangle, adductor canal)
  3. Popliteal artery (after the adductor hiatus)
  4. Anterior tibial and posterior tibial arteries
  5. Dorsalis pedis (from anterior tibial) and plantar arteries (from posterior tibial)

Veins of the lower limb

Veins form superficial and deep groups. The deep veins generally follow the arteries (femoral, superior gluteal, inferior gluteal, obturator). The major deep vein is the femoral vein. It becomes the external iliac vein when it passes under the inguinal ligament.

The superficial veins lie in the subcutaneous connective tissue and drain into the deep veins. They form two channels, both starting from the dorsal venous arch of the foot.

In the clinic: varicose veins
  • Normal flow depends on competent valves and on calf muscle pumping. Incompetent valves put extra pressure on the valves below, giving dilated, tortuous superficial veins along the great and small saphenous systems.
  • More common in women; symptoms are worse in pregnancy; some people are predisposed.
  • Valves can also be destroyed after a DVT: the clot incorporates the valve and healing (recanalization) destroys it.
  • Typical sites of incompetence: the great saphenous–femoral junction, perforating veins in the mid-thigh, and the small saphenous–popliteal junction.
  • High pressure can cause brown skin pigmentation, venous eczema and ulcers. Treatment includes tying off the valve, stripping the saphenous systems and valve reconstruction.
In the clinic: deep vein thrombosis (DVT)
  • Virchow's triad: venous stasis, injury to the vessel wall, hypercoagulable state.
  • A calf DVT can spread into the femoral veins. A piece can break off, pass through the heart and block the pulmonary artery.
  • Surgical patients get prophylaxis: anticoagulant injections and graduated stockings.
  • There may be no signs. Clues: calf tenderness, postoperative fever, limb swelling.
  • Diagnosis is mainly by duplex Doppler sonography, rarely by ascending venography. Confirmed DVT is treated with anticoagulation.

Lymphatics

Most lymph from the lower limb drains to the superficial and deep inguinal nodes in the fascia just below the inguinal ligament.

Superficial inguinalAbout 10; in the superficial fascia, parallel to the inguinal ligament; medially along the end of the great saphenous veinGluteal region, lower abdominal wall, perineum, superficial limbExternal iliac nodes, by vessels with the femoral vessels
Deep inguinalUp to 3; medial to the femoral veinDeep lymphatics with the femoral vessels; glans penis (or clitoris)External iliac nodes, along the medial side of the femoral vein (through the femoral canal)
PoplitealSmall group of deep nodes behind the knee, by the popliteal vesselsSuperficial vessels with the small saphenous vein; deep leg and footDeep and superficial inguinal nodes

The space through which lymphatics pass under the inguinal ligament is the femoral canal. Deep gluteal lymphatics follow the vessels into the pelvis to the internal iliac nodes; superficial gluteal lymphatics drain to the superficial inguinal nodes.

Deep fascia and the saphenous opening

  • The outer layer of deep fascia is a thick, stocking-like membrane under the superficial fascia. In the thigh and gluteal region it is very thick and is called the fascia lata. Below, it continues as the deep fascia of the leg.
  • Its upper attachment follows the inguinal ligament, iliac crest, sacrum, coccyx, sacrotuberous ligament, and the inferior ramus, body and superior ramus of the pubic bone.
  • Laterally it thickens into the iliotibial tract, from the tubercle of the iliac crest to a bony attachment just below the knee.
  • The fascia lata splits to enclose tensor fasciae latae (in front) and gluteus maximus (behind). Tensor fasciae latae inserts into the superior and anterior tract; most of gluteus maximus inserts into the posterior tract.
  • Through the tract the two muscles hold the knee extended once other muscles have extended it, and stabilise the hip by stopping the femur moving laterally away from the acetabulum.
  • The fascia lata has one large opening, the saphenous opening (saphenous ring), just below the medial end of the inguinal ligament. The great saphenous vein passes through it to join the femoral vein.
  • Its margin is the free medial edge of the fascia lata, which curls round the lateral side of the great saphenous vein and under the femoral vein to attach to the pectineal line.

Gluteal region vessels

Two arteries enter the gluteal region from the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen. They supply the gluteal region and posterior thigh and anastomose with femoral artery branches.

Superior gluteal arteryInferior gluteal artery
OriginPosterior trunk of the internal iliacAnterior trunk of the internal iliac
Leaves pelvisWith the superior gluteal nerve, above piriformisWith the inferior gluteal nerve, below piriformis
BranchesSuperficial branch (deep surface of gluteus maximus); deep branch (between gluteus medius and minimus)Supplies adjacent muscles, a branch to the sciatic nerve, and descends in the posterior thigh

The gluteal veins follow the arteries into the pelvis and join the pelvic venous plexus. Peripherally they join superficial gluteal veins that drain forward to the femoral vein.

Femoral artery and the femoral triangle

  • The femoral artery is felt in the femoral triangle just below the inguinal ligament, midway between the anterior superior iliac spine and the pubic symphysis.
  • It runs vertically through the triangle, down the thigh in the adductor canal, leaves through the adductor hiatus in adductor magnus and becomes the popliteal artery.
  • Four small branches arise in the triangle for the skin of the upper thigh, lower abdomen and perineum: superficial epigastric, superficial circumflex iliac, superficial external pudendal and deep external pudendal arteries.

  2. Femoral artery

Deep artery of the thigh and hip anastomoses

The deep artery of the thigh (profunda femoris) is the largest branch of the femoral artery and the major supply of the thigh. It arises from the lateral side of the femoral artery in the femoral triangle. It passes backward between pectineus and adductor longus, then between adductor longus and brevis, then between adductor longus and magnus, and finally pierces adductor magnus to join popliteal branches. It gives the lateral and medial circumflex femoral arteries and three perforating arteries.

ArteryCourse and branches
Lateral circumflex femoralUsually from the lateral side of the deep artery (may come from the femoral). Passes deep to sartorius and rectus femoris. Ascending branch: under tensor fasciae latae, joins the medial circumflex to circle the femoral neck. Descending branch: behind rectus femoris, through vastus lateralis, joins a popliteal branch near the knee. Transverse branch: pierces vastus lateralis, circles the proximal femoral shaft, forms the cruciate anastomosis.
Medial circumflex femoralUsually from the posteromedial deep artery (may come from the femoral). Winds medially round the femoral shaft. A small branch enters the hip joint through the acetabular notch and joins the obturator acetabular branch. The trunk passes over adductor magnus and splits deep to quadratus femoris: one branch to the trochanteric fossa (joins gluteal and lateral circumflex branches), one branch joins the hip anastomosis.
Perforating arteries (3)First above, second in front of, third below adductor brevis. All pierce adductor magnus near the linea aspera and supply the posterior compartment. Ascending and descending branches link above with the hip network and below with popliteal branches.
In the clinic: cruciate anastomosis and avascular necrosis
  • The cruciate anastomosis in the upper thigh is formed by the inferior gluteal, lateral and medial circumflex femoral arteries and the first perforating artery. It gives an alternative route if the femoral artery supply is interrupted.
  • The medial circumflex femoral artery is the major supply of the femoral neck and head (the lateral circumflex also contributes). Injury in fractures or dislocations can cause avascular necrosis of the femoral head: bone dies, with joint pain, limited movement and possible collapse.

Obturator artery

  • A branch of the internal iliac in the pelvis. It enters the medial compartment of the thigh through the obturator canal.
  • In the canal it divides into anterior and posterior branches that circle the margin of the obturator membrane, inside the attachment of obturator externus.
  • They supply nearby muscles and anastomose with the inferior gluteal and medial circumflex femoral arteries.
  • An acetabular vessel from the posterior branch enters the hip through the acetabular notch and helps supply the femoral head.

Knee and popliteal artery

  • The knee joint is supplied by descending and genicular branches of the femoral, popliteal and lateral circumflex femoral arteries, and by the circumflex fibular and recurrent branches of the anterior tibial artery. They form an anastomotic network round the joint.
  • There are six genicular arteries; five come from the popliteal artery. The descending genicular artery (highest genicular) is the only one from the femoral artery and has the highest origin. They form the patellar network (genicular anastomosis) and supply the patella together with the descending genicular, anterior tibial recurrent and the descending branch of the lateral circumflex femoral.
  • The popliteal artery is the main supply of the leg and foot. It passes between gastrocnemius and popliteus, then under the tendinous (soleal) arch between the fibular and tibial heads of soleus, and immediately divides into the anterior and posterior tibial arteries.
  • Two large sural arteries supply gastrocnemius, soleus and plantaris.
In the clinic: peripheral vascular disease
  • Narrowing or blockage of the aorta, iliac, femoral, tibial or fibular vessels reduces leg blood flow (chronic leg ischaemia, usually luminal narrowing over 50%).
  • The commonest symptom is intermittent claudication: cramp-like pain on walking that stops with rest and returns at the same distance.
  • Pain in the calf usually means femoral artery narrowing; pain in the buttocks means aorto-iliac narrowing.
  • Critical limb ischaemia: gangrene, ulcers and rest pain in the foot; needs urgent treatment.

Arteries and veins of the foot

The foot is supplied by the posterior tibial and dorsalis pedis arteries. The plantar arch supplies most of the foot.

ArteryCourse and branches
Posterior tibial → plantar arteriesEnters through the tarsal tunnel; midway between the medial malleolus and the heel it splits into a small medial plantar and a much larger lateral plantar artery.
Lateral plantarRuns anterolaterally (deep to abductor hallucis, then between quadratus plantae and flexor digitorum brevis) to the base of metatarsal V, then curves medially as the deep plantar arch. The arch joins the deep plantar artery of the dorsalis pedis between the bases of metatarsals I and II.
Deep plantar arch branchesA digital branch to the lateral side of the little toe; four plantar metatarsal arteries (digital branches to adjacent sides of toes I–V and the medial side of the great toe); three perforating arteries between the bases of metatarsals II–V to the dorsal foot.
Medial plantarPasses deep to abductor hallucis, then along the groove between abductor hallucis and flexor digitorum brevis. Ends by joining the digital branch for the medial great toe. A superficial branch near the base of metatarsal I splits into three vessels that join the plantar metatarsal arteries.
Dorsalis pedisContinues the anterior tibial across the ankle; over talus, navicular and intermediate cuneiform; then dives between the two heads of the first dorsal interosseous as the deep plantar artery.
Dorsalis pedis branchesTarsal arteries (medial and lateral, to the ankle network); arcuate artery (laterally over the metatarsal bases; gives three dorsal metatarsal arteries to digits II–V and a dorsal digital artery to the lateral side of digit V); first dorsal metatarsal artery (last branch before the deep plantar artery; to the great and second toes).

Veins of the foot. Deep and superficial networks interconnect; the deep veins follow the arteries. Superficial veins drain into the dorsal venous arch over the metatarsals. The great saphenous starts medially and passes in front of the medial malleolus; the small saphenous starts laterally and passes behind the lateral malleolus.

Superficial leg veins often enlarge, and because they are long they can be removed and used as vascular grafts. The great saphenous vein passes through the saphenous ring to join the femoral vein; the small saphenous vein pierces the deep fascia in the upper third of the leg to join the popliteal vein.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • The femoral artery becomes the popliteal artery at the adductor hiatus, not at the inguinal ligament.
  • The order in the femoral triangle is nerve, artery, vein, canal from lateral to medial. The vein is medial to the artery.
  • The medial circumflex femoral artery, not the lateral, is the key supply of the femoral head.
  • The anterior tibial artery enters the front of the leg through the upper opening; the fibular perforating branch uses the lower one.
  • The fibular artery is a branch of the posterior tibial artery in this lecture.
  • Deep leg lymph goes first to the popliteal nodes; deep gluteal lymph goes to the internal iliac nodes, not the inguinal nodes.
  • Deep inguinal nodes drain the glans penis or clitoris; superficial inguinal nodes drain the perineum.
  • DVT is diagnosed mainly by duplex Doppler, not venography.

Check yourself

  1. Which structure lies immediately medial to the femoral vein in the femoral triangle?

    • A. Femoral nerve
    • B. Femoral artery
    • C. Femoral canal
    • D. Great saphenous vein
    Show answer

    C. The femoral canal, with lymphatics, lies medial to the vein, next to the pubic tubercle.

  2. The fascia lata is thickened laterally into which structure?

    • A. Saphenous ring
    • B. Iliotibial tract
    • C. Adductor canal
    • D. Pectineal line
    Show answer

    B. The iliotibial tract runs from the iliac crest tubercle to just below the knee.

  3. The deep branch of the superior gluteal artery runs between which muscles?

    • A. Gluteus maximus and medius
    • B. Gluteus medius and minimus
    • C. Piriformis and gemellus superior
    • D. Tensor fasciae latae and rectus femoris
    Show answer

    B. The superficial branch goes under gluteus maximus; the deep branch passes between gluteus medius and minimus.

  4. Which branch of the lateral circumflex femoral artery helps form the cruciate anastomosis?

    • A. Ascending branch
    • B. Descending branch
    • C. Transverse branch
    • D. Acetabular branch
    Show answer

    C. The transverse branch circles the femoral shaft and joins the medial circumflex, inferior gluteal and first perforating arteries.

  5. Lymph from the glans penis drains first to which nodes?

    • A. Popliteal nodes
    • B. Internal iliac nodes
    • C. Deep inguinal nodes
    • D. Para-aortic nodes
    Show answer

    C. The deep inguinal nodes receive lymph from the glans penis or clitoris.

  6. Which vessel passes through the tarsal tunnel behind the medial malleolus?

    • A. Dorsalis pedis artery
    • B. Fibular artery
    • C. Posterior tibial artery
    • D. Lateral plantar artery
    Show answer

    C. The posterior tibial artery enters the sole through the tarsal tunnel and splits into the medial and lateral plantar arteries.

  7. The popliteal artery divides after passing under which structure?

    • A. The adductor hiatus
    • B. The tendinous arch of soleus
    • C. The inguinal ligament
    • D. The flexor retinaculum
    Show answer

    B. It passes under the soleal arch and then divides into the anterior and posterior tibial arteries.

  8. A patient has cramp-like buttock pain on walking that eases with rest. Narrowing is most likely in which segment?

    • A. Femoral artery
    • B. Popliteal artery
    • C. Aorto-iliac segment
    • D. Posterior tibial artery
    Show answer

    C. Buttock claudication suggests aorto-iliac disease; calf claudication suggests the femoral artery.

  9. Which dorsalis pedis branch gives the dorsal metatarsal arteries of digits II to V?

    • A. Tarsal artery
    • B. Arcuate artery
    • C. Deep plantar artery
    • D. Medial plantar artery
    Show answer

    B. The arcuate artery gives three dorsal metatarsal arteries; the first dorsal metatarsal artery is a separate, later branch.

  10. Where does the great saphenous vein join the femoral vein?

    • A. At the adductor hiatus
    • B. In the popliteal fossa
    • C. Just below the inguinal ligament, through the saphenous opening
    • D. At the obturator canal
    Show answer

    C. It passes through the saphenous opening in the fascia lata into the femoral triangle.

Key terms

Femoral triangle: depression in the upper thigh bounded by the inguinal ligament, sartorius and adductor longus · Adductor hiatus: gap in adductor magnus where the femoral becomes the popliteal artery · Profunda femoris: deep artery of the thigh · Cruciate anastomosis: arterial network round the upper femur · Fascia lata: deep fascia of the thigh and gluteal region · Iliotibial tract: lateral thickening of the fascia lata · Saphenous opening: gap in the fascia lata for the great saphenous vein · Dorsal venous arch: foot veins from which both saphenous veins start · Femoral canal: medial compartment of the femoral sheath, holds lymphatics · Tarsal tunnel: passage behind the medial malleolus · Deep plantar arch: arterial arch of the sole from the lateral plantar artery · Dorsalis pedis: continuation of the anterior tibial artery · Genicular arteries: six arteries round the knee · Virchow's triad: stasis, vessel injury, hypercoagulability · Intermittent claudication: pain on walking relieved by rest · Avascular necrosis: bone death from lost blood supply.
