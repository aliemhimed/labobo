Pleura and lungs

The pleural cavities and their reflections and recesses, the pleural nerve supply, the form, root, lobes and fissures of each lung, the bronchial tree and bronchopulmonary segments, and the pulmonary and bronchial vessels.

Labobo topic: Anatomy Lec 14: Pleura & Lungs

In 60 seconds

  • Each pleural cavity is a potential space between parietal and visceral pleura, holding only a thin layer of serous fluid. Parietal parts: costal, diaphragmatic, mediastinal, cervical.
  • Pleura reaches rib VIII (midclavicular), rib X (midaxillary), TXII (back). The lung reaches rib VI, rib VIII, TX in quiet breathing. The gap is the costodiaphragmatic recess.
  • Parietal pleura is pain-sensitive (intercostal nerves for costal; phrenic C3-C5 for diaphragmatic and mediastinal). The visceral pleura does not generally give pain.
  • Right lung: 3 lobes, 2 fissures (oblique, horizontal). Left lung: 2 lobes, 1 oblique fissure, a lingula and a cardiac notch.
  • Root: pulmonary artery (superior), two pulmonary veins (inferior), main bronchus (posterior). Vagus is behind the root, phrenic in front.
  • The right main bronchus is wider and more vertical: foreign bodies lodge there. 10 bronchopulmonary segments per lung.

Pleural cavities and pleura

Two pleural cavities lie one on each side of the mediastinum and surround the lungs.

  • Superiorly they extend above rib I into the root of the neck.
  • Inferiorly they extend to just above the costal margin.
  • The medial wall of each cavity is the mediastinum.

Each cavity is lined by a single layer of flat cells, the mesothelium, plus supporting connective tissue. Together these form the pleura.

  • Parietal pleura: pleura on the walls of the cavity.
  • Visceral pleura: pleura that reflects off the medial wall onto the lung surface and adheres to it.

The pleural cavity is the potential space between the two layers. It normally holds only a very thin layer of serous fluid, so the lung surface slides freely against the parietal pleura.

The parietal pleura has somatic afferent fibres.

Suprapleural membrane

A dome of fascia covers the cervical pleura. It attaches laterally to the medial margin of rib I and behind to the transverse process of CVII. Deep neck muscles (scalenes) send fibres into it and keep it taut. It gives apical support to the pleural cavity in the root of the neck.

Root of the lung

At about TV to TVII the mediastinal pleura reflects off the mediastinum as a sleeve around the structures (airway, vessels, nerves, lymphatics) passing between lung and mediastinum. This sleeve with its contents is the root of the lung. It joins the lung at the hilum, where the mediastinal pleura becomes visceral pleura.

Peripheral reflections

The peripheral reflections of the parietal pleura mark the extent of the pleural cavities.

  • Above: the cavity can project as much as 3 to 4 cm above the first costal cartilage but not above the neck of rib I, because rib I slopes down to the manubrium.
  • In front: the cavities approach each other behind the upper sternum. Behind the lower sternum, the left pleura stays further from the midline than the right, because the middle mediastinum (pericardium and heart) bulges left.
  • Below: costal pleura reflects onto the diaphragm above the costal margin.

Visceral pleura

  • Continuous with the parietal pleura at the hilum of each lung.
  • Firmly attached to the lung surface, including both surfaces of the fissures that divide the lung into lobes.
  • It has visceral afferent nerves that accompany the bronchial vessels, but pain is generally not elicited from it.
  • Pleuritic chest pain (sharp, worse with breathing or coughing) comes from the parietal pleura. In pleurisy, inflammation of the parietal layer causes the pain.
Watch out

The slide says the visceral pleura "lacks sensory nerves", but the same slide says it has visceral afferent nerves, and Gray's p. 169 gives only the afferent statement (pain is generally not elicited). These do not fit together. For the exam, use the safe version: the visceral pleura has visceral afferents that accompany bronchial vessels, but pain is generally not felt from it. Pain comes from the parietal pleura.

Pleural recesses

The lungs do not fill the anterior or posterior inferior parts of the cavities. Here two layers of parietal pleura lie against each other. The lung expands into these recesses only in forced inspiration. They are potential spaces where fluid can collect and from which it can be aspirated.

RecessWhereNotes
CostomediastinalIn front, where costal pleura meets mediastinal pleuraLargest on the left, over the heart
CostodiaphragmaticBetween costal and diaphragmatic pleura, between the lower lung margin and the lower pleural marginLargest and clinically most important. Deepest after forced expiration, shallowest after forced inspiration

In expiration the lung's lower margin rises and the costodiaphragmatic recess becomes larger.

Pneumothorax and pleural effusion

The slides give these two conditions as headings only. The facts below are from Gray's p. 169.

In the clinic: pneumothorax

Air in the pleural cavity. The lung collapses because of its own elasticity. In a tension pneumothorax the air pushes the mediastinum to the opposite side and compromises the other lung; it needs urgent treatment. Symptoms: pain, shortness of breath, and cardiorespiratory collapse if severe.

In the clinic: pleural effusion

Excess fluid in the pleural space; the lung may collapse as the volume rises. Fluid is often aspirated to find the cause (infection, malignancy, cardiac failure, hepatic disease, pulmonary embolism). A large effusion is drained so the lung can re-expand.

Lungs: shape, root and hilum

The lungs are the organs of respiration, on either side of the mediastinum, each surrounded by a pleural cavity. Air passes through the main bronchi from the trachea. The right lung is a little larger than the left because the heart bulges more to the left.

Each lung is a half cone with a base, an apex, two surfaces and three borders.

FeatureDescription
BaseSits on the diaphragm
ApexProjects above rib I into the root of the neck
Costal surfaceAgainst the ribs and intercostal spaces
Mediastinal surfaceAgainst the mediastinum in front and the vertebral column behind. Has the comma-shaped hilum
Inferior borderSharp; separates base from costal surface
Anterior borderSharp; separates costal from medial surface
Posterior borderSmooth and rounded

The heart, great vessels and ribs indent the lung surfaces. A tumour or abnormality in one structure can affect its neighbour.

Root and hilum

  • The root is a short tube of structures attaching the lung to the mediastinum, covered by a sleeve of mediastinal pleura.
  • The pleural reflection outlines the hilum on the medial surface.
  • The pulmonary ligament is a thin blade-like pleural fold hanging below the root from hilum to mediastinum. It may stabilise the inferior lobe and allow the root structures to move down and up in breathing.
  • In the mediastinum, the vagus nerves pass immediately behind the roots; the phrenic nerves pass immediately in front.

Contents of each root and hilum: a pulmonary artery, two pulmonary veins, a main bronchus, bronchial vessels, nerves and lymphatics.

  1. Pulmonary artery: generally superior.
  2. Pulmonary veins: inferior.
  3. Bronchi: somewhat posterior.

On the right, the lobar bronchus to the superior lobe branches off in the root, above the pulmonary artery. On the left it branches within the lung.

  • The lobes move freely against each other because the fissures are invaginations of visceral pleura reaching almost to the hilum.
  • The fissure positions tell clinicians where to place the stethoscope to hear each lobe.

Bronchial tree and segments

Trachea

  • A flexible tube from CVI to TIV/V, where it divides into right and left main bronchi.
  • Held open by C-shaped cartilage rings, open part facing backward. The posterior wall is mostly smooth muscle.
  • The lowest ring has a hook-shaped carina projecting backward between the origins of the main bronchi.

Bronchi

  1. Main bronchus enters the root and passes through the hilum.
  2. Lobar (secondary) bronchi, one per lobe. On the right, the superior lobar bronchus starts in the root.
  3. Segmental (tertiary) bronchi, supplying the bronchopulmonary segments.
  4. Many divisions, then bronchioles, which supply the respiratory surfaces.
  • The right main bronchus is wider and more vertical, so inhaled foreign bodies lodge more often on the right.
  • Bronchial walls are held open by discontinuous plates of cartilage. Bronchioles have none.

Bronchopulmonary segments

  • The lung area supplied by a segmental bronchus and its accompanying pulmonary artery branch.
  • Pulmonary vein tributaries run between segments.
  • Each is an irregular cone: apex at the segmental bronchus origin, base on the lung surface.
  • It is the smallest functionally independent lung region and the smallest that can be removed without affecting its neighbours.
  • Ten segments in each lung; some fuse in the left lung.

Pulmonary and bronchial vessels

VesselKey facts
Pulmonary arteriesFrom the pulmonary trunk; carry deoxygenated blood from the right ventricle. The trunk bifurcates left of the midline just below TIV/V, anteroinferior and to the left of the tracheal bifurcation
Right pulmonary arteryLonger; passes horizontally. Goes in front of and slightly below the tracheal bifurcation and in front of the right main bronchus, but behind the ascending aorta, SVC and upper right pulmonary vein. Gives a large branch to the superior lobe in the root; after the hilum a second (recurrent) superior branch, then supplies middle and inferior lobes
Left pulmonary arteryShorter; in front of the descending aorta and behind the superior pulmonary vein; branches within the lung
Pulmonary veinsA superior and an inferior vein on each side carry oxygenated blood; begin at the hilum and drain straight into the left atrium

One right, usually from the third posterior intercostal artery (occasionally the upper left bronchial). Two left, directly from the thoracic aorta: superior at TV, inferior below the left bronchus. Exam traps

Exam traps
  • The pleural margin is always two ribs lower than the lung margin (VIII vs VI, X vs VIII, TXII vs TX), which makes the recess.
  • The cavity does not extend above the neck of rib I, only 3 to 4 cm above the first costal cartilage.
  • The right lung has the horizontal fissure and three lobes. The left has the lingula and cardiac notch.
  • The pulmonary artery is superior and the veins inferior at the hilum.
  • There are two pulmonary veins per side but each drains into the left atrium directly.
  • The right main bronchus is wider and more vertical, so aspirated objects go right.
  • The lung is the larger on the right, because the heart bulges left.

Check yourself

  1. Behind the lower sternum, the left pleural cavity stays further from the midline than the right. Why?

    1. The liver pushes the right pleura medially
    2. The aortic arch pushes the left pleura laterally
    3. The middle mediastinum with the heart bulges to the left
    4. The left lung is larger than the right
    Show answer

    C. The pericardium and heart bulge left, so the left pleura is held further from the midline.

  2. Pain from the diaphragmatic pleura is referred to the:

    1. Chest wall over the affected area
    2. Lateral neck and supraclavicular shoulder (C3-C5)
    3. Epigastrium
    4. Inferior angle of the scapula
    Show answer

    B. It is supplied mainly by the phrenic nerves (C3, C4, C5).

  3. Which structure keeps the suprapleural membrane taut?

    1. The diaphragm
    2. The scalene muscles
    3. The intercostal muscles
    4. The sternocleidomastoid
    Show answer

    B. Scalene fibres enter the membrane, which attaches to rib I and the transverse process of CVII.

  4. The pulmonary ligament hangs from the root of the lung to the:

    1. Diaphragm
    2. Mediastinum
    3. Sternum
    4. First rib
    Show answer

    B. It is a blade-like pleural fold running from the hilum to the mediastinum.

  5. At the hilum of the right lung, which structure is most superior?

    1. Inferior pulmonary vein
    2. Superior lobar bronchus and the pulmonary artery region
    3. Main bronchus only
    4. Bronchial artery
    Show answer

    B. The right superior lobar bronchus branches in the root, above the pulmonary artery, which is itself generally superior to the veins.

  6. The posterior border of the lung is:

    1. Sharp
    2. Smooth and rounded
    3. Notched by the heart
    4. Absent
    Show answer

    B. The anterior and inferior borders are sharp.

  7. Which vein is a medial relation of the right lung but not of the left?

    1. Subclavian vein
    2. Azygos vein
    3. Pulmonary vein
    4. Brachiocephalic vein
    Show answer

    B. The right lung lies by the azygos vein and SVC; the left lies by the aortic arch and thoracic aorta.

  8. Which structure is found in the lowest tracheal ring?

    1. Carina
    2. Cricoid
    3. Lingula
    4. Hilum
    Show answer

    A. The hook-shaped carina projects backward between the main bronchi.

  9. How do bronchial veins on the left side drain?

    1. Only into the azygos vein
    2. Into the pulmonary veins, left atrium, superior intercostal or hemiazygos vein
    3. Into the coronary sinus
    4. Into the inferior vena cava
    Show answer

    B. On the right they drain to the azygos vein.

  10. Which statement about pleural effusion is correct according to Gray's?

    1. It is air in the pleural cavity
    2. It is excess fluid that may collapse the underlying lung
    3. It affects only the visceral pleura
    4. It never needs drainage
    Show answer

    B. Fluid is often aspirated to find the cause, and a large effusion is drained.

Key terms

Mesothelium
Single layer of flat cells lining the pleural cavity.
Cervical pleura
Dome of parietal pleura in the neck (cupola).
Suprapleural membrane
Fascial dome over the cervical pleura.
Costodiaphragmatic recess
Pleural space below the lung between costal and diaphragmatic pleura.
Root of the lung
Tube of structures attaching the lung to the mediastinum.
Hilum
Area on the medial surface where the root structures enter the lung.
Pulmonary ligament
Pleural fold below the root.
Oblique fissure
Fissure in both lungs separating the inferior lobe.
Horizontal fissure
Right lung fissure between the superior and middle lobes.
Lingula
Tongue-like extension of the left superior lobe over the heart.
Carina
Hook-shaped ridge of the lowest tracheal ring.
Bronchopulmonary segment
Lung area supplied by one segmental bronchus and pulmonary artery branch.
Bronchial arteries
Nutritive arteries of lung tissue.
