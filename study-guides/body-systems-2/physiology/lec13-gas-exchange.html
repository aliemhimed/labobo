Principles of gas exchange and diffusion of O₂ and CO₂

The physics of gas diffusion (partial pressures, solubility, water vapour), how alveolar air differs from atmospheric air, what sets alveolar PO₂ and PCO₂, the structure and diffusing capacity of the respiratory membrane, and the ventilation-perfusion ratio with its shunt and dead space. Written from Guyton & Hall 14e, chapter 40 (no deck for this week).

Labobo topic: Physio Lec 13: Principles of Gas Exchange

In 60 seconds

  • Gases move from high to low partial pressure. Partial pressure = fraction × total pressure (O₂: 21% of 760 gives about 160 mm Hg).
  • Henry's law: partial pressure = concentration / solubility coefficient. CO₂ is more than 20 times as soluble as O₂, and diffuses about 20 times faster.
  • Water vapour pressure at 37 °C is 47 mm Hg; humidifying inspired air dilutes PO₂ from 159 to 149.
  • Alveolar air: PO₂ 104, PCO₂ 40. Only about one-seventh of alveolar air is replaced per breath.
  • Alveolar PO₂ and PCO₂ depend on alveolar ventilation and on the rate of O₂ uptake or CO₂ output.
  • Respiratory membrane: about 0.6 µm thick, 70 m². O₂ diffusing capacity at rest 21 ml/min per mm Hg, about three times higher in exercise.
  • VA/Q = 0: shunt (PO₂ 40, PCO₂ 45). VA/Q = infinity: dead space (PO₂ 149, PCO₂ 0).

Physics of gas diffusion and partial pressures

  • Diffusion is the random motion of molecules, powered by their kinetic energy. Net diffusion runs from high to low concentration.
  • Gas pressure comes from molecular impacts, so it is proportional to concentration. In a mixture, each gas has its own partial pressure (PO₂, PCO₂, PN₂). They add up to the total.
  • Air is 79% N₂ and 21% O₂ at 760 mm Hg: PN₂ is 600 and PO₂ is 160 mm Hg.
  • Dissolved gases exert partial pressure in the same way.

Henry's law and solubility

Partial pressure = concentration of dissolved gas / solubility coefficient. A soluble gas can dissolve in large amounts without building up pressure.

GasSolubility coefficientRelative diffusion coefficient (O₂ = 1)
Oxygen0.0241.0
Carbon dioxide0.5720.3
Carbon monoxide0.0180.81
Nitrogen0.0120.53
Helium0.0080.95
  • CO₂ is more than 20 times as soluble as O₂, so its partial pressure for a given concentration is less than one-twentieth (5%) of that of O₂.
  • Net movement between alveolus and blood follows the partial pressure difference: O₂ goes into the blood (higher in the alveolus), CO₂ goes into the alveolus (higher in the blood).

Vapour pressure of water

  • Inspired air is humidified by the moist airway surfaces. At 37 °C the vapour pressure of water is 47 mm Hg, the key value to remember.
  • It depends only on temperature: 5 mm Hg at 0 °C, 760 mm Hg at 100 °C.

Rate of diffusion in fluids

Five factors: the pressure difference, the solubility of the gas, the cross-sectional area, the distance, and the molecular weight (temperature is constant in the body). They combine into:

D ∝ ΔP · A · S / (d · √MW)

  • D is diffusion rate, ΔP the pressure difference, A the area, S solubility, d the distance, MW the molecular weight.
  • Solubility and molecular weight together give the diffusion coefficient, proportional to S/√MW.
  • These gases dissolve well in lipids, so the limit in tissues is diffusion through tissue water, not through cell membranes. Diffusion through the respiratory membrane is therefore almost the same as in water.
Watch out

The text gives the PO₂ of dry air as 160 mm Hg (0.21 × 760) but Table 40-1 gives 159, because the table uses 20.84% O₂. Treat them as the same figure. Also, the extracted formula lost its operators; the standard form is D ∝ ΔP · A · S / (d · √MW).

Alveolar air and atmospheric air

Alveolar air differs from atmospheric air because: it is only partly replaced each breath; O₂ is constantly absorbed; CO₂ constantly enters it; and incoming air is humidified.

All values in mm Hg at sea level; each row totals 760.

Humidification

Water vapour (47 mm Hg) dilutes the other gases, because total pressure cannot exceed 760. PO₂ falls from 159 to 149 and PN₂ from 597 to 563.

Slow renewal of alveolar air

  • Functional residual capacity is about 2300 ml, but only 350 ml of new air reaches the alveoli per breath. So only one-seventh is replaced each breath.
  • With normal alveolar ventilation, half of an excess gas is removed in about 17 s; at half-normal ventilation, 34 s; at twice normal, about 8 s.
  • This slow renewal stabilises respiratory control. It prevents sudden swings in blood O₂, CO₂ and pH if breathing is briefly interrupted.

Expired air

  • Expired air is a mix of dead space air (first, humidified atmospheric air) and alveolar air. Its composition lies between the two.
  • To collect alveolar air, sample the last part of a forced expiration, after dead space air has been washed out.

The respiratory membrane

  • Respiratory unit (lobule): respiratory bronchiole, alveolar ducts, atria and alveoli. There are about 300 million alveoli, each about 0.2 mm across.
  • Between alveoli is an almost solid network of capillaries: a sheet of flowing blood. Gas exchange happens across all terminal membranes, collectively the respiratory (pulmonary) membrane.

Layers (from the air side)

  1. Fluid layer with surfactant (lowers surface tension).
  2. Alveolar epithelium (thin cells).
  3. Epithelial basement membrane.
  4. Thin interstitial space.
  5. Capillary basement membrane (often fused with the epithelial one).
  6. Capillary endothelium.
  • Thickness: as little as 0.2 µm, average about 0.6 µm. Total area about 70 m² in a healthy man, with only 60-140 ml of blood in the lung capillaries at any moment.
  • Capillaries are about 5 µm wide, so red cells squeeze through and touch the wall; little plasma lies in the diffusion path.

Diffusing capacity

Diffusing capacity is the volume of a gas that diffuses through the membrane each minute for a pressure difference of 1 mm Hg.

GasAt rest (ml/min per mm Hg)Exercise
O₂21About 3 times the resting value
CO17Rises too
CO₂400-450 (calculated, never measured)1200-1300 (calculated)
  • O₂ at rest: mean pressure difference about 11 mm Hg × 21 = about 230 ml/min, equal to the resting O₂ use.
  • Why it rises in exercise: (1) dormant capillaries open and open ones dilate, adding surface area; (2) a better ventilation-perfusion match.
  • CO₂ is too diffusible to measure: the mean PCO₂ difference between blood and alveoli is under 1 mm Hg.

Carbon monoxide method

  1. Breathe in a small amount of CO and sample alveolar CO.
  2. Blood CO pressure stays near zero because hemoglobin binds CO so fast. So the pressure difference equals alveolar CO pressure.
  3. CO diffusing capacity = CO absorbed per minute / alveolar CO pressure.
  4. Multiply by 1.23 (O₂ diffuses 1.23 times as fast as CO) to get the O₂ value.

Direct O₂ measurement is impractical because blood capillary PO₂ is hard to measure.

Ventilation-perfusion ratio

Total ventilation and blood flow can be normal and yet gas exchange poor, if they go to different alveoli. VA/Q compares alveolar ventilation (VA) with blood flow (Q) for the same alveolus.

  • At zero or infinity, no gas exchange occurs in those alveoli.
  • The PO₂-PCO₂ VA/Q diagram plots a curve from the venous point (PO₂ 40, PCO₂ 45) to the inspired air point (149, 0), with normal alveolar air (104, 40) between.

Shunt, dead space and abnormal VA/Q

ConceptWhenMeaning and measure
Physiological shuntVA/Q below normalVenous blood that fails to be oxygenated, plus bronchial flow (about 2% of cardiac output). Found from O₂ content of mixed venous and arterial blood and cardiac output: shunt fraction = (CiO₂ - CaO₂) / (CiO₂ - CvO₂)
Physiological dead spaceVA/Q above normalWasted ventilation: anatomical dead space plus alveoli ventilated but poorly perfused. Bohr equation: VDphys/VT = (PaCO₂ - PeCO₂) / PaCO₂

CiO₂ is O₂ content of arterial blood at an ideal VA/Q, CaO₂ is measured arterial, CvO₂ is mixed venous. In the Bohr equation PeCO₂ is the mean PCO₂ in all expired air and VT is tidal volume. A large dead space means much ventilation work is wasted.

Normal lung (upright)

  • Both flow and ventilation are lower at the top, but flow falls more. At the apex VA/Q is up to 2.5 times ideal (moderate dead space).
  • At the base VA/Q is as low as 0.6 times ideal (a small shunt).
  • In exercise, flow to the upper lung rises and dead space falls, so exchange approaches optimum.

Chronic obstructive lung disease

  • Long-term smoking causes bronchial obstruction and emphysema.
  • Obstructed bronchioles leave alveoli unventilated: VA/Q near zero, shunt. Destroyed alveolar walls leave ventilation without flow: dead space.
  • Lung effectiveness can fall to as little as one-tenth of normal. It is the commonest cause of pulmonary disability today.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • Alveolar PO₂ can never exceed 149 mm Hg on room air at sea level, whatever the ventilation.
  • CO₂ diffuses faster than O₂, yet the CO₂ gradient is small (under 1 mm Hg on average).
  • Doubling alveolar ventilation halves alveolar PCO₂; halving it doubles PCO₂ (inverse proportion).
  • Only one-seventh of alveolar air is renewed per breath, not all of the 350 ml tidal air mixes.
  • Thickness matters only once it passes 2-3 times normal.
  • CO pressure in blood is near zero, so the CO pressure difference equals alveolar CO pressure.
  • The apex has a high VA/Q (dead space); the base has a low VA/Q (shunt).
  • Diffusion coefficient depends on the square root of molecular weight, not on MW itself.

Key terms

Partial pressure: pressure exerted by one gas in a mixture · Henry's law: partial pressure = concentration / solubility coefficient · Solubility coefficient: how much gas dissolves per unit pressure · Diffusion coefficient: proportional to solubility / √MW · Vapour pressure of water: 47 mm Hg at 37 °C · Alveolar air: gas in the alveoli (PO₂ 104, PCO₂ 40) · Respiratory unit: respiratory bronchiole, ducts, atria, alveoli · Respiratory membrane: layers separating alveolar air from blood · Surfactant: lowers alveolar surface tension · Diffusing capacity: ml of gas per minute per mm Hg · VA/Q: ratio of alveolar ventilation to blood flow · Physiological shunt: blood not oxygenated in the lungs · Physiological dead space: ventilation that does not exchange gas · Bohr equation: measures physiological dead space · Emphysema: destruction of alveolar walls.
