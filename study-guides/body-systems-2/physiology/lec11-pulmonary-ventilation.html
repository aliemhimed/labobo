Pulmonary ventilation

The muscles and pressures that move air in and out of the lungs, compliance and surfactant, lung volumes and capacities and how they are measured, minute and alveolar ventilation with dead space, and the protective functions of the airways: bronchial control, mucus and cilia, cough, sneeze and the nose, with a short note on speech. Source: Guyton and Hall, 14th ed., chapter 38.

Labobo topic: Physio Lec 11: Pulmonary Ventilation

In 60 seconds

  • Quiet breathing uses the diaphragm; expiration is passive (elastic recoil). Heavy breathing adds the external intercostals and neck muscles for inspiration, and the abdominal muscles and internal intercostals for expiration.
  • Pleural pressure is about −5 cm H₂O before inspiration and −7.5 during it. Alveolar pressure swings between about −1 and +1 cm H₂O. Transpulmonary pressure = alveolar minus pleural pressure.
  • Compliance of both lungs is about 200 ml/cm H₂O; lungs plus chest wall about 110. About two-thirds of lung elasticity is surface tension, which surfactant (type II cells) reduces.
  • Men: TV 500, IRV 3000, ERV 1100, RV 1200 ml; IC 3500, FRC 2300, VC 4600, TLC 5800 ml.
  • Minute ventilation = rate × tidal volume = about 6 L/min. Alveolar ventilation = rate × (tidal volume − dead space) = 12 × (500 − 150) = 4200 ml/min.
  • Residual volume, FRC and TLC cannot be read from a spirometer alone; the helium dilution method measures FRC.
  • Airway resistance is highest in the larger bronchi; sympathetic (epinephrine) dilates bronchioles, parasympathetic, histamine and irritants constrict them.

Respiratory muscles

Respiration has four components: pulmonary ventilation (air in and out of the alveoli), diffusion of O₂ and CO₂ between alveoli and blood, transport in blood and body fluids, and regulation. This chapter covers ventilation.

The lungs expand and contract in two ways: by the diaphragm moving down or up (lengthening or shortening the chest cavity), and by raising or lowering the ribs (changing the anteroposterior diameter).

  • Quiet breathing is almost entirely diaphragmatic. Contraction pulls the lung bases down. In expiration the diaphragm relaxes and the elastic recoil of the lungs, chest wall and abdominal structures expels air.
  • Heavy breathing: recoil is not strong enough, so the abdominal muscles contract and push the abdominal contents up against the diaphragm.
  • Raising the rib cage: at rest the ribs slant downward; when raised they project almost straight forward and the sternum moves away from the spine. The anteroposterior chest thickness is about 20% greater in maximal inspiration than in expiration.

The external intercostals slope forward and downward. When they contract they pull the upper ribs forward relative to the lower ribs, which raises the ribs. The internal intercostals slope the opposite way, so they lower the ribs.

Pressures that move air

The lung is an elastic structure that collapses like a balloon when nothing holds it inflated. It is not attached to the chest wall except at the hilum; it "floats" in the thorax in a thin layer of pleural fluid. Continual suction of excess fluid into lymphatics keeps a slight suction between the visceral and parietal pleura, so the lungs are held to the chest wall as if glued, yet can slide.

PressureDefinitionValues in quiet breathing
PleuralPressure of the fluid between the lung and chest wall pleura; a slight suctionAbout −5 cm H₂O at the start of inspiration (holds the lungs at resting level); about −7.5 during normal inspiration
AlveolarAir pressure inside alveoli. With the glottis open and no flow it equals atmospheric pressure (0 reference)About −1 cm H₂O in inspiration (pulls 0.5 L in over about 2 s); about +1 in expiration (expels it over 2 to 3 s)
TranspulmonaryAlveolar pressure minus pleural pressure; measure of the elastic forces tending to collapse the lungs (recoil pressure)Rises as pleural pressure becomes more negative

During normal inspiration, lung volume increases by 0.5 L as pleural pressure goes from −5 to −7.5 cm H₂O. Expiration reverses the events.

Compliance and surface tension

  • Lung compliance: the volume change per unit rise in transpulmonary pressure (when enough time is allowed to reach equilibrium). Normal total compliance of both lungs is about 200 ml of air per cm H₂O. A 1 cm H₂O rise expands the lungs 200 ml after 10 to 20 seconds.
  • The compliance diagram has an inspiratory and an expiratory curve. Pleural pressure is changed in small steps and volume is allowed to settle.

What makes the lungs elastic

  1. Tissue elastic forces: elastin and collagen fibres, kinked in the deflated lung and stretched when it expands. This is about one-third of total elasticity.
  2. Surface tension of the fluid lining the alveoli: about two-thirds.

Evidence: pressures needed to expand air-filled lungs are about three times those needed for saline-filled lungs, which have no air-fluid interface and only tissue elasticity.

Water at an air surface contracts because surface molecules attract each other strongly. In the alveoli this tends to force air out and collapse the alveoli: the surface tension elastic force.

Surfactant

  • Surfactant is a surface-active agent that greatly reduces the surface tension of water.
  • It is secreted by type II alveolar epithelial cells (about 10% of the alveolar surface), which are granular with lipid inclusions.
  • It is a mixture of phospholipids, proteins and ions. The key parts are dipalmitoyl phosphatidylcholine, surfactant apoproteins and calcium ions. The phospholipids reduce tension because part of each molecule dissolves while the rest spreads over the surface.
FluidSurface tension
Pure water72 dynes/cm
Alveolar lining fluid without surfactant50 dynes/cm
Alveolar lining fluid with normal surfactant5 to 30 dynes/cm

Pressure in an occluded alveolus

Pressure = 2 × surface tension ÷ radius

  • For an average alveolus (radius about 100 µm) with normal surfactant: about 4 cm H₂O (3 mm Hg). With pure water and no surfactant: about 18 cm H₂O, which is 4.5 times as great.
  • The pressure is inversely related to radius: half the radius (50 µm) doubles the pressure.
In the clinic: respiratory distress syndrome of the newborn

Surfactant is not secreted until between the sixth and seventh months of gestation, sometimes later. Premature infants have little or none, and their alveoli are often less than 25% of the adult radius. The lungs may tend to collapse six to eight times more than in a normal adult. This is fatal if untreated; properly applied continuous positive pressure breathing is the main treatment given in the book.

Watch out

The book describes the effect of surfactant two ways: the surface has "one-twelfth to one-half the surface tension of a pure water surface" (which works out to about 6 to 36 dynes/cm), and a list of values of "5 to 30" dynes/cm with normal surfactant. The two ranges are close but not identical. Use the dyne values (72, 50, 5 to 30) when numbers are asked, and the idea "greatly reduces" when they are not.

Thoracic cage, work and energy

  • The thoracic cage has its own elastic and viscous properties. The compliance of the lungs and thorax together is measured in a relaxed or paralysed subject by forcing air in a little at a time.
  • Inflating the whole system needs almost twice the pressure needed for the lungs alone. Compliance of the combined system is about 110 ml/cm H₂O, compared with 200 for the lungs alone.
  • At very high or very low lung volumes, the chest limits expansion so much that combined compliance can be below 20% of that of the lungs alone.
  • In quiet breathing, muscles work during inspiration only; expiration is passive.
Work of inspirationWhat it overcomes
Compliance (elastic) workLung and chest elastic forces
Tissue resistance workViscosity of lung and chest wall structures
Airway resistance workResistance to air movement into the lungs
  • Quiet breathing needs only 3% to 5% of the body's total energy. In heavy exercise this can rise as much as 50-fold, especially with higher airway resistance or lower compliance. Respiratory energy supply is one of the main limits on exercise intensity.

Lung volumes and capacities

The four volumes add up to the maximum volume of the lungs. A capacity is a combination of two or more volumes. Values vary with fitness, age, height, sex and altitude.

Watch out

The women's values in Table 38-1 do not add up exactly with the book's own definitions. Inspiratory capacity should be VT + IRV = 400 + 1900 = 2300, but the table gives 2400. Vital capacity should be IRV + VT + ERV = 1900 + 400 + 700 = 3000, but the table gives 3100. TLC (4200 = 3100 + 1100) matches the table's vital capacity, and FRC (700 + 1100 = 1800) is exact. The men's values add up exactly. For women, remember the table values if a question asks for them, and use the formulas if a question gives you the volumes. Some women's values are also more than 30% below the men's (for example vital capacity, 3100 against 4600), so "20% to 30% less" is only a rough guide.

Using the formulas

  • FRC = ERV + RV; so RV = FRC − ERV.
  • TLC = IC + FRC.
  • VC = IRV + VT + ERV; TLC = VC + RV.

Spirometry and helium dilution

  • Spirometry records air moved into and out of the lungs. A basic spirometer is a drum inverted over a water chamber, counterbalanced by a weight, with air or O₂ inside and a tube to the mouth. The drum rises and falls with breathing and the movement is recorded.
  • A spirometer cannot measure FRC, RV or TLC directly, because the residual volume cannot be expired into it. RV makes up about half of the FRC.

Helium dilution method

  1. A spirometer of known volume is filled with air and helium at a known concentration.
  2. The person expires normally, so the lungs hold the FRC.
  3. Immediately the person breathes from the spirometer. Helium mixes with the gas in the lungs and becomes diluted.
  4. FRC is calculated from the final helium concentration compared with the initial concentration and the initial spirometer volume.
  5. Then RV = FRC − ERV (ERV from normal spirometry) and TLC = IC + FRC.

Standard abbreviations include VT, FRC, ERV, RV, IC, IRV, TLC, VC; Raw for airway resistance; Palv for alveolar pressure; Ppl for pleural pressure; PO₂ and PCO₂ for partial pressures; PaO₂ and PaCO₂ for arterial values.

Minute and alveolar ventilation

Minute respiratory volume

  • Minute respiratory volume = tidal volume × respiratory rate. With 500 ml and about 12 breaths/min it is about 6 L/min.
  • A person can live for a short time with as little as 1.5 L/min and a rate of 2 to 4 breaths/min.
  • At a rate of 40 to 50 breaths/min with a tidal volume as large as the vital capacity (about 4600 ml), the minute volume can exceed 200 L/min, more than 30 times normal. Most people cannot sustain more than half to two-thirds of this for over 1 minute.

Dead space

Some inspired air never reaches gas exchange areas but fills the nose, pharynx and trachea: dead space air. On expiration it comes out first, so dead space is a disadvantage for removing expired gases.

  1. The subject takes a deep breath of 100% O₂, which fills the dead space with pure O₂.
  2. The person expires through a rapid nitrogen meter. First the nitrogen reading is 0 (dead space air), then it rises as alveolar air arrives, then reaches a plateau equal to the alveolar concentration.
  3. The area of air with no nitrogen (gray area) is a measure of dead space: VD = gray area ÷ (gray + pink areas) × total expired volume.

Book example: gray area 30, pink area 70, expired volume 500 ml gives 30 ÷ (30 + 70) × 500 = 150 ml.

  • Normal dead space in a young man is about 150 ml; it increases slightly with age.
  • Anatomical dead space: all respiratory spaces other than the alveoli and related gas exchange areas.
  • Physiological dead space: anatomical dead space plus alveoli that are non-functional or only partly functional because of absent or poor capillary blood flow.
  • In healthy lungs, the two are nearly equal. In disease, the physiological dead space may be as much as 10 times the anatomical, or 1 to 2 liters.

Alveolar ventilation

Alveolar ventilation is the new air entering the gas exchange areas per minute: respiratory rate × (tidal volume − physiological dead space). The gas exchange areas are the alveoli, alveolar sacs, alveolar ducts and respiratory bronchioles.

V̇A = Freq × (VT − VD). With 12 breaths/min, 500 ml and 150 ml: 12 × (500 − 150) = 4200 ml/min.

Alveolar ventilation is one of the major determinants of alveolar O₂ and CO₂ concentrations, so later chapters focus on it.

Airways and bronchial control

Structure

  • Trachea: cartilage rings extend about five-sixths of the way around. Bronchi: curved cartilage plates that become less extensive in later generations and are absent in bronchioles (diameter usually under 1.5 mm).
  • Bronchioles are kept open mainly by the same transpulmonary pressures that expand the alveoli. As alveoli enlarge, bronchioles enlarge too, but less.
  • Where there is no cartilage, walls are mostly smooth muscle. The respiratory bronchiole is mainly epithelium, fibrous tissue and a few smooth muscle fibres. Many obstructive lung diseases come from narrowing of small bronchi and larger bronchioles, often by excessive smooth muscle contraction.

Resistance to airflow

  • Less than 1 cm H₂O alveolus-to-atmosphere gradient is enough for quiet breathing.
  • The greatest resistance is in the larger bronchioles and bronchi near the trachea, because there are few of them compared with about 65,000 parallel terminal bronchioles.
  • In disease, the small bronchioles matter more, as they are easily occluded by muscle contraction, wall edema or mucus.

Mucus, cough, sneeze and the nose

Mucus and cilia

  • All passages from the nose to the terminal bronchioles are coated with mucus (from goblet cells and submucosal glands). It moistens the surface and traps particles.
  • The surface is ciliated: about 200 cilia per cell, beating 10 to 20 times/s. The power stroke is always toward the pharynx: upward in the lungs, downward in the nose.
  • Mucus moves at a few millimetres per minute toward the pharynx and is swallowed or coughed out.

The cough reflex

The trachea and bronchi are very sensitive to light touch; the larynx and carina especially. Terminal bronchioles and alveoli respond to corrosive chemicals such as sulfur dioxide or chlorine. Afferents run mainly in the vagus to the medulla.

  1. Up to 2.5 liters of air are inspired rapidly.
  2. The epiglottis closes and the vocal cords shut tightly to trap the air.
  3. The abdominal muscles contract forcefully against the diaphragm, with the internal intercostals. Lung pressure rises to 100 mm Hg or more.
  4. The cords and epiglottis open suddenly and air explodes out, sometimes at 75 to 100 miles/hour.

The compression collapses the bronchi and trachea (the non-cartilaginous parts invaginate), so the air passes through slits and carries out foreign matter.

The sneeze reflex

Like the cough, but triggered by irritation of the nasal passages. Afferents run in the fifth cranial nerve to the medulla. The uvula is depressed, so large amounts of air go through the nose.

Functions of the nose

  • Air conditioning: the air is warmed by the conchae and septum (about 160 cm²), humidified, and partly filtered. Inspired air reaches within 1°F of body temperature and within 2% to 3% of full water saturation before the trachea.
  • Breathing through a tube into the trachea (tracheostomy) bypasses this, so cooling and drying in the lower lung may cause serious crusting and infection.
  • Filtration: hairs at the nostrils remove large particles. More important is turbulent precipitation: particles cannot change direction as fast as air as it hits the conchae (turbinates), septum and pharyngeal wall; they stick to mucus and are carried to the pharynx.
Particle sizeFate
Larger than 6 µmAlmost none enter the lungs through the nose (smaller than a red blood cell)

  • Particles in the alveoli are removed by alveolar macrophages or lung lymphatics. Too many cause fibrous tissue growth in the alveolar septa and permanent debility.

Speech

  • Speech needs cortical speech centres, the brain's respiratory centres, and the articulation and resonance structures of the mouth and nose. It has two mechanical functions: phonation (larynx) and articulation (mouth).
  • The vocal cords (vocal folds) vibrate. In normal breathing they are wide open; in phonation they come together so that air makes them vibrate.
  • Pitch depends mainly on the stretch of the cords, and also on how tightly they are approximated and the mass of their edges.
  • Inside each cord is a strong elastic vocal ligament, attached in front to the thyroid cartilage (Adam's apple) and behind to the vocal processes of the two arytenoid cartilages. These articulate from below with the cricoid cartilage.
  • Cords are stretched by forward rotation of the thyroid cartilage or backward rotation of the arytenoids; the thyroarytenoid muscles loosen them. Slips of these muscles can sharpen the edges for high pitch or blunt them for bass tones.
  • The three organs of articulation are the lips, tongue and soft palate. Resonators include the mouth, nose and sinuses, pharynx and even the chest cavity; a blocked nose changes voice quality.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • Quiet expiration is passive; there is no muscle work in expiration at rest.
  • Pleural pressure is negative (suction); it becomes more negative in inspiration.
  • Alveolar pressure is below atmospheric in inspiration and above it in expiration.
  • Surface tension is two-thirds of lung elasticity, tissue is one-third.
  • The combined lung-thorax compliance is about half the lung compliance alone.
  • Smaller alveoli generate higher collapsing pressure (inverse of radius).
  • RV, FRC and TLC cannot be measured by simple spirometry.
  • Alveolar ventilation uses the physiological dead space, not total volume.
  • The greatest airway resistance is in the larger bronchi and bronchioles, not the terminal ones, in normal health.
  • Cilia beat upward in the lungs but downward in the nose.
  • Women's Table 38-1 capacities do not add exactly (see Watch out).
