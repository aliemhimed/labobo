Regulation of respiration

How the brain stem sets the rhythm and depth of breathing, how CO₂, H⁺ and O₂ control ventilation (central and peripheral chemoreceptors), how ventilation is matched to exercise, and what goes wrong in periodic breathing, sleep apnea and respiratory depression. Written from Guyton & Hall 14e, chapter 42 (no deck for this week).

Labobo topic: Physio Lec 15: Regulation of Respiration

In 60 seconds

  • Three groups of neurons: dorsal (inspiration and rhythm, in the NTS), ventral (mainly expiration, an overdrive for heavy breathing) and the pneumotaxic center (pons, switches the inspiratory ramp off).
  • Inspiration is a ramp: about 2 s on, about 3 s off. Expiration in quiet breathing is passive.
  • The chemosensitive area (0.2 mm below the ventral medulla) is driven by H⁺. CO₂ is the main controller because it crosses the blood-brain barrier and makes H⁺ there.
  • O₂ acts only through peripheral chemoreceptors (carotid and aortic bodies, glomus cells), mainly when PO₂ falls below about 70 mm Hg.
  • CO₂ effect fades to about one-fifth over 1-2 days. Acclimatization raises ventilation 400-500% at altitude.
  • In exercise, ventilation rises almost in step with O₂ use mainly by neurogenic drive; arterial PO₂, PCO₂ and pH stay about normal.
  • Cheyne-Stokes: delayed feedback or high gain. Sleep apnea: mostly obstructive. Overdose of anesthetics or narcotics is the commonest cause of respiratory arrest.

The respiratory center

The nervous system adjusts alveolar ventilation to the body's needs so that arterial PO₂ and PCO₂ hardly change, even in heavy exercise. The respiratory center is a set of neuron groups on both sides of the medulla and pons.

Dorsal group and the ramp signal

  • The NTS is the sensory ending of the vagus and glossopharyngeal nerves. They bring signals from peripheral chemoreceptors, baroreceptors, receptors in the liver, pancreas and gut, and lung receptors.
  • The basic rhythm comes mainly from this group. It keeps firing bursts of inspiratory action potentials even if all incoming nerves are cut and the brain stem is cut above and below the medulla. The cause of the rhythm is unknown (probably a network in which one set of neurons excites a second that inhibits the first).

The inspiratory ramp

  1. The signal to the diaphragm starts weak and rises steadily for about 2 s.
  2. It then stops abruptly for about 3 s; elastic recoil of lungs and chest wall causes expiration.
  3. The cycle repeats.
  • Benefit: a steady rise in lung volume instead of gasps.
  • Two controlled features: (1) the rate of rise of the ramp (faster in heavy breathing); (2) the point where it stops. Stopping earlier shortens inspiration and also expiration, so the rate rises. This is the usual way of controlling respiratory rate.

Pneumotaxic center, ventral group, Hering-Breuer reflex

Pneumotaxic center

  • Controls the switch-off point of the ramp, so the length of the filling phase.
  • Strong signal: inspiration may last only 0.5 s (lungs filled slightly). Weak signal: 5 s or more.
  • Limiting inspiration also shortens the whole cycle: a strong signal can raise the rate to 30-40 breaths/min; a weak one can lower it to 3-5.

Ventral respiratory group

  • Lies about 5 mm anterior and lateral to the dorsal group.
  • Almost inactive in quiet breathing: quiet inspiration comes from the dorsal group, and expiration is passive recoil.
  • Does not take part in the basic rhythm. When drive rises, signals spill over from the dorsal area and it adds extra drive.
  • Some neurons cause inspiration and others expiration. It gives powerful expiratory signals to the abdominal muscles in very heavy expiration: an overdrive mechanism, especially in heavy exercise.

Hering-Breuer inflation reflex

  • Stretch receptors in the muscular walls of bronchi and bronchioles send signals through the vagi to the dorsal group when the lungs are overstretched.
  • They switch off the inspiratory ramp (like the pneumotaxic center) and raise the rate.
  • In humans it probably acts only when tidal volume exceeds three times normal (about 1.5 L/breath): a protective reflex, not part of normal control.

Chemosensitive area: CO₂ and H⁺

The goal of respiration is to keep O₂, CO₂ and H⁺ in the tissues in range. Excess CO₂ or H⁺ act directly on the respiratory center and strengthen both inspiratory and expiratory signals. O₂ does not act directly on the center; it acts through the peripheral chemoreceptors.

  • The dorsal, ventral and pneumotaxic groups are not directly driven by CO₂ or H⁺. A separate chemosensitive area lies on both sides, only 0.2 mm beneath the ventral surface of the medulla, and excites the rest of the respiratory center.
  • Its neurons are excited by H⁺, probably the only important direct stimulus.

Why CO₂ is stronger than blood H⁺

  1. H⁺ crosses the blood-brain barrier poorly, so blood H⁺ has little effect.
  2. CO₂ crosses it almost freely. Blood PCO₂ rise raises PCO₂ in medullary interstitial fluid and CSF.
  3. There CO₂ + H₂O gives H₂CO₃, then H⁺ + HCO₃⁻. The new H⁺ strongly stimulates the chemosensitive neurons.

Fading of the CO₂ effect

  • Strong in the first hours, then falls over 1-2 days to about one-fifth of the initial effect.
  • Cause: the kidneys raise blood HCO₃⁻, which binds H⁺ in blood and CSF; over hours HCO₃⁻ also diffuses through the barriers and binds H⁺ next to the respiratory neurons.
  • So CO₂ has a potent acute but weak chronic effect.

Quantitative effect

  • Ventilation rises markedly as PCO₂ rises across 35-75 mm Hg.
  • In the normal blood pH range (7.3-7.5) the change in ventilation is less than 10% as great.

Why CO₂, not O₂, is the major controller

  • The hemoglobin buffer keeps tissue O₂ delivery normal as alveolar PO₂ varies from 60 up to 1000 mm Hg. So adequate O₂ delivery is possible despite ventilation from slightly below half normal to 20 or more times normal.
  • CO₂ is different: blood and tissue PCO₂ change inversely with ventilation. So CO₂ is the major controller.

Peripheral chemoreceptors and O₂

  • Chemoreceptors outside the brain detect mainly O₂, and also CO₂ and H⁺ to a lesser extent. They respond when blood O₂ falls too low, mainly below PO₂ 70 mm Hg.
  • Most are in the carotid bodies (at the bifurcation of the common carotid arteries). Fewer are in the aortic bodies (arch of the aorta) and a very few elsewhere in thoracic and abdominal arteries.
BodyAfferent pathwayGoes to
Carotid bodyHering's nerve, then glossopharyngeal nerveDorsal respiratory area of the medulla
Aortic bodyVagus nerveDorsal respiratory area of the medulla
  • Each body has its own tiny artery. Blood flow is about 20 times the weight of the body per minute, so almost no O₂ is removed: receptors see arterial PO₂, not venous.
  • Impulse rate is most sensitive to arterial PO₂ between 60 and 30 mm Hg, where hemoglobin saturation falls steeply.

How low O₂ excites them

  1. Glomus cells (glandular-like cells that synapse with nerve endings) act as the chemoreceptors.
  2. Low PO₂ closes their O₂-sensitive K⁺ channels.
  3. The cell depolarises and voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open; cytosolic Ca²⁺ rises.
  4. A transmitter is released (ATP is probably the key one) and excites the afferent fibers, which stimulate respiration.

CO₂ and H⁺ on the peripheral receptors

  • Also excite them, but the direct effect on the respiratory center is about seven times as powerful.
  • The peripheral route is up to five times faster, so it may speed the response to CO₂ at the start of exercise.
Watch out

The hypoxic threshold is given two ways: the text says the chemoreceptors respond mainly below PO₂ 70 mm Hg; the caption of Fig 42-6 says K⁺ channels close "when the PO₂ decreases below around 60 mm Hg". The text also says ventilation effect is modest above 60-80 mm Hg. Use 70 as the threshold at which the chemoreceptor system starts to respond; 60 is where the response becomes marked (ventilation about doubles).

Combined effects and acclimatization

Low O₂ alone (PCO₂ and pH held constant)

  • Almost no effect on ventilation while arterial PO₂ is above 100 mm Hg.
  • Below 100, ventilation doubles by 60 mm Hg and may rise as much as fivefold at very low PO₂ (the figure shows about 6-fold from 100 to 20).
  • Because the effect is modest above 60-80 mm Hg, the PCO₂ and H⁺ responses regulate breathing in healthy people at sea level.

Acclimatization

  • Climbers who ascend over days breathe much more deeply than those who ascend in hours, and tolerate far lower O₂.
  • Within 2-3 days the respiratory center loses about 80% of its sensitivity to PCO₂ and H⁺. The CO₂ blow-off that would normally restrain breathing does not occur, so low O₂ can drive ventilation much higher.
  • Acute low O₂: about 70% rise in ventilation. After 2-3 days: often 400-500%.

Regulation during exercise

  • O₂ use and CO₂ production can rise up to 20-fold. Ventilation rises almost exactly in step, and arterial PO₂, PCO₂ and pH stay near normal.
  • These values do not change enough to explain the drive. The main cause is neurogenic: the brain sends collateral impulses to the respiratory center at the same time as motor impulses go to the muscles (like the vasomotor center and blood pressure).
  • A large part of the increase starts immediately, before any blood chemistry has changed.

Chemical fine tuning

  • At onset, ventilation jumps almost instantly and arterial PCO₂ may dip below normal (anticipatory drive).
  • After 30-40 s, CO₂ release from the muscles matches the ventilation and PCO₂ returns to normal.
  • If neurogenic drive is too strong or too weak, chemical factors correct it. The neurogenic factor shifts the PCO₂-ventilation curve upward about 20-fold, so PCO₂ stays at 40 at rest and in heavy exercise. In exercise, PCO₂ above 40 adds stimulation and below 40 depresses.

The neurogenic control may be partly a learned response: with repeated exercise the brain gets better at setting the right drive. Experiments that block only the cortex block the learned response, so the cerebral cortex is involved.

Periodic breathing and sleep apnea

Cheyne-Stokes breathing

  • Slowly waxing and waning breathing about every 40-60 s.
  • Mechanism: overbreathing blows off CO₂, but it takes several seconds for the changed blood to reach the brain and inhibit it, so the person overventilates. Then the center is over-depressed and the opposite cycle starts. Depth of breathing follows the PCO₂ in the brain, not in the pulmonary blood.
  • This tendency exists in everyone but is normally damped by large stores of dissolved and bound CO₂ and O₂.
Cause of overriding the dampingDetail
Long lung-to-brain delaySlow blood flow, as in severe cardiac failure; can occur on and off for months in chronic heart failure
Increased feedback gainA 3 mm Hg PCO₂ rise may raise ventilation 10- to 20-fold instead of the normal 2- to 3-fold. Mainly in brain damage to respiratory centers; often a prelude to death

Sleep apnea

  • Apnea = no spontaneous breathing. In sleep apnea, episodes last 10 s or longer and occur 300-500 times a night.
  • Obstructive (the most common cause): pharyngeal muscles relax and a narrow pharynx closes. Loud snoring, a silent pause, falling PO₂ and rising PCO₂ cause gasping, repeated hundreds of times. Result: fragmented sleep, daytime drowsiness, raised sympathetic activity, high heart rate, pulmonary and systemic hypertension and high cardiovascular risk.
  • Typically in older obese people (fat in pharynx or neck). Also with nasal obstruction, large tongue, big tonsils or palate shape.
  • Treatment: surgery (fat removal, uvulopalatopharyngoplasty, tonsils or adenoids, tracheostomy) or CPAP nasal ventilation.
  • Central: neural drive to the muscles transiently stops (damage to centers, strokes, or neuromuscular disorders). Often cause unknown. Very sensitive to sedatives and narcotics. CPAP at night is usually needed.
  • Mixed type is about 15% of cases; pure central under 1%.

Voluntary control

  • Breathing can be controlled voluntarily for short periods; hyperventilation or hypoventilation can seriously change PCO₂, pH and PO₂.
  • Record static breath-hold, without hyperventilating with pure O₂ first: 11 min 54 s. After hyperventilating with pure O₂ and blowing off CO₂: over 24 min underwater.
  • Elite apnea competitors can suppress the urge to breathe until O₂ saturation is about 50%; unconsciousness limits the time.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • Quiet breathing uses only the dorsal group; expiration is passive.
  • The chemosensitive area is not one of the three respiratory center groups.
  • CO₂ stimulates breathing indirectly, through H⁺ formed in the CSF and medullary fluid.
  • O₂ does not act on the medulla directly; only through the peripheral chemoreceptors.
  • Carotid body receptors see arterial PO₂ because their flow is very high.
  • In exercise, arterial PO₂, PCO₂ and pH stay normal; the drive is neurogenic, not chemical.
  • Hering-Breuer is a protective reflex, not important in normal quiet breathing.
  • Acclimatization works by reducing the center's sensitivity to CO₂ and H⁺, not by increasing O₂ sensitivity.
  • The text says "below 70" and the Fig 42-6 caption "around 60" for the hypoxic threshold.

Check yourself

  1. Which structure sets the switch-off point of the inspiratory ramp from the pons?

    2. Nucleus parabrachialis (pneumotaxic center)
    3. Nucleus retroambiguus
    4. Carotid body
    Show answer

    B. The pneumotaxic center lies dorsally in the nucleus parabrachialis of the upper pons.

  2. A strong pneumotaxic signal causes:

    1. Inspiration of 5 s or more and 3-5 breaths/min
    2. Inspiration of about 0.5 s and a rate up to 30-40 breaths/min
    3. Apneustic breathing
    4. No change in breathing rate
    Show answer

    B. Short inspiration shortens the whole cycle, so rate rises.

  3. In the inspiratory ramp, how long does the diaphragm signal rise, and how long is it off?

    1. 1 s on, 1 s off
    2. 2 s on, 3 s off
    3. 3 s on, 2 s off
    4. 5 s on, 5 s off
    Show answer

    B. About 2 s rising, then about 3 s off while recoil causes expiration.

  4. Ventral group neurons cause powerful signals to which muscles during very heavy expiration?

    1. Diaphragm
    2. Abdominal muscles
    3. Pharyngeal muscles
    4. External intercostals
    Show answer

    B. The ventral group is an overdrive mechanism with strong expiratory signals to the abdominal muscles.

  5. Where is the chemosensitive area of the respiratory center?

    1. In the carotid bifurcation
    2. In the pons next to the pneumotaxic center
    3. 0.2 mm beneath the ventral surface of the medulla
    4. In the nucleus of the tractus solitarius
    Show answer

    C. It lies bilaterally, only 0.2 mm under the ventral medullary surface.

  6. After 1-2 days of high blood CO₂, the stimulation falls partly because:

    1. Chemoreceptors are destroyed
    2. Blood HCO₃⁻ rises (renal) and HCO₃⁻ also enters the CSF to bind H⁺
    3. The blood-brain barrier becomes impermeable to CO₂
    4. The pneumotaxic center takes over
    Show answer

    B. HCO₃⁻ binds H⁺ and returns it near normal in blood, CSF and next to the neurons.

  7. Carotid body blood flow is about 20 times the body's weight each minute. What follows?

    1. The receptors sense arterial PO₂
    2. The receptors sense venous PO₂
    3. The receptors cannot detect CO₂
    4. They are insensitive below 60 mm Hg
    Show answer

    A. Virtually no O₂ is extracted, so the receptors see arterial blood.

  8. Which transmitter is probably most important in carotid body hypoxia signalling?

    1. ATP
    2. Dopamine only
    3. Glutamate
    4. Serotonin
    Show answer

    A. Recent studies suggest ATP is the key excitatory transmitter from glomus cells.

  9. Which statement about the effects of PCO₂ and pH on ventilation is correct?

    1. A change in pH within 7.3-7.5 has far more effect than a PCO₂ change
    2. Over PCO₂ 35-75 the effect is large; the normal pH range gives less than 10% as much
    3. They have no effect below PO₂ 100
    4. PCO₂ only acts through the aortic body
    Show answer

    B. CO₂ changes are the tremendous controller; the pH range effect is under 10% as great.

  10. At the start of a bout of exercise, what typically happens to arterial PCO₂?

    1. It rises sharply at once
    2. It may dip slightly below normal, then return to normal after 30-40 s
    3. It falls steadily throughout exercise
    4. It stays high for 5 minutes
    Show answer

    B. Anticipatory neurogenic drive overshoots at first; then muscle CO₂ release matches ventilation.

  11. Cheyne-Stokes breathing from increased feedback gain is typically seen in:

    1. Healthy runners
    2. Patients with damage to the respiratory centers of the brain
    3. Obese older men with pharyngeal fat
    4. People at high altitude only
    Show answer

    B. Brain damage raises gain; the pattern is often a prelude to death from brain malfunction. Delay-type Cheyne-Stokes occurs in severe cardiac failure.

  12. Which share of sleep apnea cases is a mixed obstructive and central type?

    1. Under 1%
    2. About 15%
    3. About 50%
    4. About 90%
    Show answer

    B. Mixed is about 15%; pure central is under 1%; obstruction is the most common cause.

Key terms

Dorsal respiratory group: medullary neurons for inspiration and rhythm (NTS) · Ventral respiratory group: expiration and overdrive · Pneumotaxic center: pontine center that limits inspiration · Inspiratory ramp: steadily rising signal to the diaphragm · Hering-Breuer reflex: lung stretch reflex that stops inspiration · Chemosensitive area: H⁺-sensitive neurons under the ventral medulla · Peripheral chemoreceptors: carotid and aortic bodies, sense O₂ · Glomus cells: O₂-sensing cells of the carotid body · Hering's nerve: carries carotid body fibers to the glossopharyngeal nerve · Acclimatization: stronger hypoxic ventilatory response after days at altitude · J receptors: alveolar receptors stimulated by capillary engorgement · Cheyne-Stokes breathing: waxing and waning periodic breathing · Obstructive sleep apnea: pharyngeal closure during sleep · CPAP: continuous positive airway pressure · Apnea: absence of spontaneous breathing.
