Cardiac output, venous return and their regulation

Normal cardiac output and cardiac index, why venous return normally controls cardiac output, cardiac output curves and venous return curves, how the two are combined to find the equilibrium point, what blood volume, sympathetic activity and an AV fistula do, and how cardiac output is measured. Source: Guyton and Hall, 14th ed., chapter 20.

Labobo topic: Physio Lec 9: Cardiac Output, Venous Return and Their Regulation

In 60 seconds

  • Resting cardiac output is about 5 L/min (men 5.6, women 4.9). Cardiac index is output per m² of body surface: about 3 L/min/m².
  • Normally venous return controls cardiac output. The Frank-Starling mechanism makes the heart pump whatever returns. Tissue metabolism sets local flows, and cardiac output is their sum.
  • The normal heart plateaus at about 13 L/min. Maximal nervous excitation lifts this to about 25 L/min; hypertrophy plus nervous excitation allows 30 to 40 L/min.
  • The venous return curve depends on mean systemic filling pressure (Psf, normal 7 mm Hg), right atrial pressure and resistance to venous return: VR = (Psf − PRA) / RVR.
  • Venous return is zero when right atrial pressure equals Psf, and it plateaus at negative atrial pressures because the large veins collapse.
  • Cardiac output and right atrial pressure are found where the two curves cross.
  • Output is measured by the Fick principle (O₂ uptake divided by arteriovenous O₂ difference) or indicator dilution.

Normal values

  • Cardiac output: blood pumped into the aorta each minute. Venous return: blood flowing from the veins into the right atrium each minute. They are equal except for a few beats while blood is stored in or removed from the heart and lungs.
  • Cardiac output depends on body metabolism, exercise, age and body size.
  • Resting output: young men about 5.6 L/min, women about 4.9 L/min, and for the average adult in round numbers about 5 L/min.
  • Output rises roughly in proportion to body surface area, so it is given as the cardiac index. A 70 kg person has a surface area of about 1.7 m², giving a normal index of about 3 L/min/m².

Age

The cardiac index rises rapidly to more than 4 L/min/m² at age 10 and falls to about 2.4 L/min/m² at age 80. Output follows overall metabolic activity, so the fall reflects less activity and less muscle mass.

Control by venous return

  • Peripheral factors that determine venous return are normally the primary controllers of cardiac output.
  • Reason: the Frank-Starling law. More blood entering stretches the chamber walls, the muscle contracts harder, and the extra blood is ejected at once.
  • Stretching the sinus node raises heart rate by 10% to 15%. The stretched right atrium also starts the Bainbridge reflex: signals to the vasomotor centre and back by sympathetic nerves and vagi, which raises the heart rate.
  • If returning blood exceeds what the heart can pump, the heart becomes the limiting factor.

Cardiac output is the sum of tissue flows

  • Venous return is the sum of all local tissue flows, so cardiac output regulation is the sum of all local flow regulation.
  • Tissue blood flow rises mainly in proportion to metabolism. During exercise, oxygen consumption and cardiac output rise in parallel.

Total peripheral resistance

  • When arterial pressure is unchanged, long-term cardiac output varies inversely with total peripheral resistance (Ohm's law): higher resistance, lower output; lower resistance, higher output.
  • At 100% of normal resistance, output is normal. The book's examples (beriberi, AV shunts, hyperthyroidism, anaemia, pulmonary disease, Paget disease, hypothyroidism, removal of both arms and legs) show this reciprocal relation.

Cardiac output curves

A cardiac output curve (cardiac function curve) plots cardiac output against right atrial pressure. It shows the heart's limits.

  • The normal curve has a plateau of about 13 L/min, which is 2.5 times the normal output of about 5 L/min. A normal heart without special stimulation can handle a venous return up to about 2.5 times normal.
  • Hypereffective hearts lie above the normal curve; hypoeffective hearts lie below it.
FactorEffect
Nervous excitation (sympathetic up, parasympathetic down)Heart rate can rise from 72 to 180 to 200 beats/min in young people, and contractility to twice normal. The plateau rises to almost 25 L/min, nearly double
Hypertrophy (long-term higher workload)Marathon runners' hearts may be 50% to 75% bigger; plateau rises 60% to 100%
Both togetherAs much as 30 to 40 L/min, about 2.5 times an average person, a key factor in running time

Hypoeffective heart: causes

  • Raised arterial pressure to pump against, such as severe hypertension.
  • Inhibition of nervous excitation of the heart.
  • Abnormal heart rhythm or rate.
  • Coronary artery blockage (heart attack).
  • Valvular heart disease; congenital heart disease.
  • Myocarditis (inflammation of the heart muscle); cardiac hypoxia.

Nervous system and cardiac output

  • Dinitrophenol experiment in dogs: it raised metabolism of nearly all tissues about fourfold and dilated peripheral vessels.
    • With nervous control intact: pressure barely changed and output rose almost fourfold.
    • After autonomic block: pressure fell to about half normal and output rose only 1.6-fold.
  • So the nervous system's maintenance of arterial pressure is a prerequisite for high output when peripheral vessels dilate.
  • Exercise: muscle arterioles dilate, so total peripheral resistance falls. The same brain activity that drives the muscles excites circulatory centres: large veins constrict, heart rate and contractility rise. Pressure rises above normal and output rises an extra 30% to 100%.

High and low cardiac output

High output: reduced total peripheral resistance

All come from chronically reduced resistance. None come from over-excitation of the heart.

ConditionMechanismEffect
BeriberiLack of thiamine (vitamin B1): tissues use nutrients poorly, so local mechanisms cause compensatory vasodilationResistance can fall to half normal; output may double
AV fistula (shunt)Blood flows directly from a major artery into a major veinLarge fall in resistance, higher venous return and output
HyperthyroidismHigher metabolism, more O₂ use, vasodilator products releasedOutput often 40% to 80% above normal
AnaemiaLower blood viscosity, and less O₂ delivery causing local vasodilationOutput increases greatly

Low output

  • Cardiac factors: severe coronary blockage and myocardial infarction, severe valvular disease, myocarditis, cardiac tamponade, cardiac metabolic derangements. If output falls so low that tissues starve, it is cardiac shock.
  • Noncardiac peripheral factors (reduced venous return):
    1. Decreased blood volume, the most common, often from hemorrhage.
    2. Acute venous dilation, often from sudden loss of sympathetic activity as in fainting; blood pools and the filling pressure falls.
    3. Obstruction of the large veins (rare).
    4. Decreased tissue mass, especially skeletal muscle (ageing, inactivity).
    5. Decreased metabolic rate (prolonged bed rest, hypothyroidism).
  • If output falls below what tissues need, the person is in circulatory shock, which can be lethal within minutes to hours.
In the clinic: why fainting lowers output

Sudden loss of sympathetic activity dilates the veins. Blood volume no longer fills the vessels to a useful pressure, so blood pools in the periphery and venous return and cardiac output fall.

External pressure on the heart

The normal pressure outside the heart equals intrapleural pressure, about −4 mm Hg. A change in external pressure shifts the whole cardiac output curve sideways by about the same amount, because the atria need extra pressure to fill.

  • Intrapleural pressure rising to −2 mm Hg shifts the curve right by 2 mm Hg.
  • Rising to +2 mm Hg needs a 6 mm Hg rise in right atrial pressure from the normal −4, so the curve shifts 6 mm Hg right.
FactorEffect on cardiac output curve

The final curve is changed by both external pressure and the pumping effectiveness of the heart. A hypereffective heart with higher intrapleural pressure has a higher plateau but is shifted right.

The venous return curve

The systemic circulation was studied by replacing the heart and lungs with a pump and artificial oxygenator. Three factors affect venous return:

  1. Right atrial pressure: a backward force that impedes flow into the atrium.
  2. Mean systemic filling pressure (Psf): the degree of filling, which pushes blood toward the heart.
  3. Resistance to venous return between the periphery and the right atrium.

Shape of the normal curve

  • As right atrial pressure rises, venous return falls. With reflexes blocked, venous return is zero at about +7 mm Hg. This is the Psf.
  • When right atrial pressure falls below zero, venous return reaches a plateau by about −2 mm Hg and stays there even at −20 or −50 mm Hg. The cause is collapse of the large veins where they enter the chest.

Mean circulatory filling pressure and Psf

If the heart is stopped, flow ceases within seconds and pressures equalise everywhere: the mean circulatory filling pressure.

  • Blood volume about 4000 ml gives a filling pressure near zero (the unstressed volume); 5000 ml gives the normal 7 mm Hg; higher volumes raise it almost linearly. The curves are steep, so small changes in volume or capacity have large effects.
  • Maximal sympathetic stimulation constricts vessels and raises the filling pressure to about 14 mm Hg (twice normal). Complete sympathetic inhibition lowers it to about 4 mm Hg.
  • Psf is measured in the systemic circulation alone after clamping the large vessels at the heart. It is nearly equal to the mean circulatory filling pressure because the lungs have less than one-eighth of the capacitance and about one-tenth of the blood volume.
  • A higher Psf shifts the venous return curve up and to the right; a lower Psf shifts it down and left. Psf of 14, 7 and 3.5 mm Hg are shown.
Watch out

The book gives two values for the effect of maximal sympathetic stimulation on the filling pressure. In the volume-pressure discussion (Figure 20-11) it raises the filling pressure to about 14 mm Hg, double the normal 7. In the cardiac output analysis (Figure 20-16) maximal stimulation raises Psf to 17 mm Hg. The two figures come from different graphs. If a question asks for the number, use the one that matches its context: 14 mm Hg for the filling pressure versus blood volume, 17 mm Hg for the combined cardiac output analysis.

Pressure gradient and resistance to venous return

  • The difference between Psf and right atrial pressure is the pressure gradient for venous return. When right atrial pressure equals Psf there is no flow. So the highest right atrial pressure possible, however badly the heart fails, is the Psf.
  • About two-thirds of the resistance to venous return is in the veins and about one-third in the arterioles and small arteries. Veins are highly distensible, so pressure behind a venous block rises little. Arteries have about one-thirtieth the capacitance of veins, so a slight blood build-up raises arterial pressure about 30 times as much.
QuantityNormal adult value
Venous return (VR)5 L/min
Mean systemic filling pressure (Psf)7 mm Hg
Right atrial pressure (PRA)0 mm Hg
Resistance to venous return (RVR)1.4 mm Hg per L/min

VR = (Psf − PRA) / RVR. Check: (7 − 0) / 1.4 = 5 L/min.

  • Halving the resistance to venous return allows twice the flow and rotates the curve upward to twice the slope; doubling the resistance halves the slope. All curves meet at the same point on the pressure axis (venous return zero at PRA = Psf).

Combining the two curves

The heart and systemic circulation must agree on two things: venous return must equal cardiac output, and right atrial pressure is the same for both. So plot the cardiac output curve and the venous return curve together. They cross at one point, the equilibrium point.

  • Normal equilibrium: cardiac output 5 L/min and right atrial pressure 0 mm Hg.
SituationChange in the curvesResult
Blood volume +20%Psf rises to 16 mm Hg (curve shifts right); vessels stretch, so resistance to venous return falls (curve rotates up)Output rises to 2.5 to 3 times normal; right atrial pressure about +8 mm Hg
Moderate to maximal sympathetic stimulationHeart pumps nearly 100% better; Psf rises to 17 mm Hg; resistance to venous return risesOutput about double normal; right atrial pressure hardly changes
Total spinal anaesthesia (sympathetic inhibition)Psf falls to about 4 mm Hg; heart effectiveness falls to about 80%Output falls to about 60% of normal

Compensation after a blood volume increase

  1. Higher output raises capillary pressure, so fluid leaves the capillaries and blood volume falls toward normal.
  2. Veins distend gradually (stress relaxation), especially in the liver and spleen, which lowers Psf.
  3. Excess flow causes autoregulatory vasoconstriction, raising resistance to venous return.
  4. Over 10 to 40 minutes, output returns almost to normal.

Likewise, different degrees of sympathetic stimulation raise output to about twice normal only for short periods until compensations return it near normal.

Measuring cardiac output

In animals, an electromagnetic or ultrasonic flowmeter on the aorta or pulmonary artery can be used. A recording shows flow rising to a peak in systole and then reversing briefly at the end of systole, which closes the aortic valve. In humans, indirect methods are used.

Oxygen Fick principle

  1. Measure O₂ absorbed by the lungs each minute (from the disappearance of O₂ from respired air).
  2. Measure the O₂ concentration in mixed venous blood (catheter to the right atrium, right ventricle or pulmonary artery) and in systemic arterial blood.
  3. Divide: cardiac output = O₂ absorbed per minute ÷ arteriovenous O₂ difference.

Book example: 200 ml O₂ per minute is absorbed; blood entering the right heart has 160 ml/L and blood leaving the left heart has 200 ml/L. Each litre absorbs 40 ml, so output = 200 ÷ 40 = 5 L/min.

Indicator dilution method

  • Inject a small amount of dye (Cardiogreen) into a large vein or the right atrium; record its concentration in a peripheral artery. The dye first appears after about 3 seconds and peaks at about 6 to 7 seconds.
  • Part of the dye recirculates before the concentration reaches zero, so the early downslope is extrapolated to zero.
  • Cardiac output = dye injected × 60 ÷ (average concentration × duration of the curve in seconds).
  • Book example: 5 mg injected; average concentration 0.25 mg/dl for 12 seconds. That equals 20 portions of 100 ml in 12 seconds, so output is 2 L in 12 s, or 10 L/min.

Other methods

  • Echocardiography: ultrasound (chest wall or oesophagus) measures chamber size and the velocity of blood from the left ventricle into the aorta. Stroke volume comes from that velocity and the aortic cross-section (from its diameter); output = stroke volume × heart rate.
  • Impedance cardiography (thoracic electrical bioimpedance): noninvasive; a high-frequency, low-magnitude current flows through the thorax and impedance changes with each beat are used to estimate output. Errors come from electrical interference, motion, fluid around the heart and in the lungs, and arrhythmias. Average error may be as high as 20% to 40%.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • Under normal conditions the periphery (venous return), not the heart, controls cardiac output.
  • Venous return is zero when right atrial pressure equals Psf, not at zero atrial pressure.
  • Resistance to venous return is mainly venous (two-thirds), not arteriolar.
  • A rise in Psf shifts the venous return curve; a rise in resistance changes its slope.
  • Venous return reaches its plateau at negative atrial pressures because large veins collapse.
  • In hypothyroidism and bed rest, cardiac output falls; in hyperthyroidism, beriberi and anaemia it rises because resistance falls.
  • Positive-pressure breathing and cardiac tamponade shift the cardiac output curve to the right.
  • The huge output rise after a volume load lasts only minutes.
  • In the Fick calculation use the arteriovenous O₂ difference (ml per litre), not the arterial value alone.

Check yourself

  1. A 70 kg adult has a body surface area of about 1.7 m² and a normal cardiac index. Roughly what is the cardiac output?

    1. 3 L/min
    2. 5 L/min
    3. 8 L/min
    4. 13 L/min
    Show answer

    B. About 3 L/min/m² × 1.7 m² is about 5 L/min.

  2. What happens to the cardiac index between age 10 and age 80?

    1. It rises steadily
    2. It stays unchanged
    3. It falls from more than 4 to about 2.4 L/min/m²
    4. It falls from 3 to zero
    Show answer

    C. The decline mirrors falling activity and muscle mass.

  3. The Bainbridge reflex is triggered by:

    1. Stretch of the right atrium, raising heart rate
    2. Low arterial pressure at the carotid sinus
    3. Stretch of the left ventricle, lowering heart rate
    4. A fall in blood oxygen
    Show answer

    A. The signal goes to the vasomotor centre and back by sympathetic nerves and vagi.

  4. Total peripheral resistance doubles chronically while arterial pressure stays the same. What happens to cardiac output?

    1. It doubles
    2. It is unchanged
    3. It halves
    4. It rises by 30%
    Show answer

    C. At constant pressure, output varies inversely with resistance.

  5. What most limits the maximum output of a normal, unstimulated heart?

    1. The plateau of its cardiac output curve, about 13 L/min
    2. Venous return of 5 L/min
    3. The unstressed volume
    4. The right atrial pressure of 0 mm Hg
    Show answer

    A. The heart can pump up to about 2.5 times normal venous return.

  6. A patient has a high cardiac output with a low total peripheral resistance. Which cause fits?

    1. Myocardial infarction
    2. Beriberi
    3. Cardiac tamponade
    4. Hypothyroidism
    Show answer

    B. Thiamine deficiency causes compensatory vasodilation and raised output.

  7. Psf is nearly equal to the mean circulatory filling pressure because the lungs have:

    1. Less than one-eighth of the capacitance and about one-tenth of the blood volume
    2. About half of the capacitance and one-third of the blood volume
    3. More capacitance than the systemic veins
    4. No blood volume at all
    Show answer

    A. Psf is measured in the systemic circulation alone, and the lungs hold little capacitance and blood volume.

  8. A person's resistance to venous return is halved and nothing else changes. Compared with the normal venous return curve, the new curve:

    1. Shifts left
    2. Rotates upward with twice the slope
    3. Rotates downward with half the slope
    4. Shifts up with no change in slope
    Show answer

    B. Venous return doubles at each pressure gradient, and the zero-flow point is unchanged.

  9. After complete sympathetic inhibition by spinal anaesthesia, which pair of changes occurs?

    1. Psf rises and heart effectiveness rises
    2. Psf falls and cardiac output falls to about 60% of normal
    3. Psf is unchanged and cardiac output rises
    4. Psf rises and cardiac output falls
    Show answer

    B. Psf falls to about 4 mm Hg and the heart pumps at about 80% of normal.

  10. A patient absorbs 250 ml O₂ per minute. Mixed venous O₂ is 150 ml/L and arterial O₂ is 200 ml/L. What is the cardiac output by the Fick principle?

    1. 2 L/min
    2. 4 L/min
    3. 5 L/min
    4. 8 L/min
    Show answer

    C. The difference is 50 ml/L, so 250 ÷ 50 = 5 L/min.

  11. In the indicator dilution method, when does the dye first appear in the peripheral artery and when does it peak?

    1. First at about 1 second, peak at about 2 seconds
    2. First at about 3 seconds, peak at about 6 to 7 seconds
    3. First at about 10 seconds, peak at about 20 seconds
    4. First at about 3 seconds, peak at about 12 seconds
    Show answer

    B. The dye first appears after about 3 seconds and peaks at about 6 to 7 seconds.

Key terms

Cardiac output: blood pumped by the heart per minute · Venous return: blood flowing into the right atrium per minute · Cardiac index: cardiac output per m² of body surface · Frank-Starling law: more filling gives a stronger beat · Bainbridge reflex: heart rate rise from right atrial stretch · Cardiac output curve: output versus right atrial pressure · Hypereffective heart: pumps better than normal · Hypoeffective heart: pumps worse than normal · Cardiac tamponade: fluid in the pericardial cavity compressing the heart · Cardiac shock: output too low from heart failure · Beriberi: thiamine deficiency with vasodilation · AV fistula: direct artery-to-vein connection · Venous return curve: venous return versus right atrial pressure · Mean circulatory filling pressure: equal pressure everywhere when flow stops · Mean systemic filling pressure (Psf): same, measured in the systemic circulation only · Unstressed volume: volume at which filling pressure is near zero · Pressure gradient for venous return: Psf minus right atrial pressure · Resistance to venous return: opposition between periphery and right atrium · Equilibrium point: crossing of the two curves · Fick principle: output from O₂ uptake and arteriovenous O₂ difference · Indicator dilution: output from the dilution of an injected dye · Impedance cardiography: noninvasive estimate from thoracic impedance changes.
