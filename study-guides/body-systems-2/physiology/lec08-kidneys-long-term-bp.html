Kidneys in long-term control of arterial pressure

How the kidneys set the long-term level of arterial pressure by balancing salt and water intake against output (pressure natriuresis and diuresis), how the renin-angiotensin system adds to this, what causes the different kinds of hypertension, and how the fast, intermediate and slow pressure controls fit together. Source: Guyton and Hall, 14th ed., chapter 19.

Labobo topic: Physio Lec 8: Kidneys in Long-Term Control of Arterial Pressure

In 60 seconds

  • A rise in arterial pressure makes the kidneys excrete more salt and water (pressure natriuresis and pressure diuresis). Urine output is about zero at 50 mm Hg, normal at 100 and 4 to 6 times normal at 200.
  • Long-term pressure sits where the renal output curve crosses the intake line (the equilibrium point). The mechanism has near-infinite feedback gain: pressure returns all the way to that point.
  • Only two things change long-term pressure: a shift of the renal output curve or a change in salt and water intake. Raising total peripheral resistance alone does not, if the kidneys are normal.
  • The renin-angiotensin system: renin (JG cells) makes angiotensin I from angiotensinogen; ACE in the lungs makes angiotensin II, which constricts arterioles fast and makes the kidneys retain salt and water, directly and through aldosterone.
  • Volume-loading hypertension starts with a high cardiac output; later the output returns toward normal and total peripheral resistance stays high.
  • Goldblatt hypertension follows renal artery constriction; 90% to 95% of hypertension is primary (essential), with obesity as a major driver.
  • Pressure control works in layers: seconds (nervous reflexes), minutes to hours (renin-angiotensin, stress relaxation, capillary fluid shift), days and longer (renal-body fluid).

Renal-body fluid system

The chapter deals with pressure control over weeks and months. It is tied to body fluid volume, which depends on the balance between fluid intake and output. If blood volume rises and vascular capacity does not change, arterial pressure rises. The rising pressure makes the kidneys excrete the extra volume, which brings pressure back down. This system acts slowly but powerfully.

  • It is a primitive mechanism, fully working in the hagfish. The hagfish has a low pressure of 8 to 14 mm Hg that rises almost in proportion to its blood volume. It drinks sea water, which raises volume and pressure; the kidney excretes the excess when pressure is high.
  • In humans, many refinements have been added: nervous, hormonal and local systems. The most important is the renin-angiotensin mechanism.
  • In humans, a rise in arterial pressure of only a few mm Hg can double renal water output (pressure diuresis) and salt output (pressure natriuresis).

Renal output curves and the equilibrium point

The renal function curve

The renal urinary output curve (renal function curve) plots salt and water output against arterial pressure. Sodium output rises by about the same amount as urine volume.

Arterial pressureUrine output (human, isolated kidney curve)
50 mm HgEssentially zero
100 mm HgNormal
200 mm Hg4 to 6 times normal

In the dog experiment (nervous reflexes blocked), infusing about 400 ml of blood doubled cardiac output and raised mean pressure to 205 mm Hg, 115 mm Hg above resting. Urine output rose 12-fold, and both cardiac output and pressure returned to normal within the next hour.

Near-infinite feedback gain

  1. Over a long period, output must equal intake. They are equal only where the curves cross: the equilibrium point.
  2. If pressure rises above it (for example to 150 mm Hg), output is almost three times intake. The body loses fluid, blood volume falls and pressure falls.
  3. This continues until pressure is back exactly at the equilibrium level, even when it is only a few mm Hg too high.
  4. If pressure falls below it, intake exceeds output, volume rises and pressure rises back to the equilibrium point.

This return of pressure always to the equilibrium point is the near-infinite feedback gain principle.

Two determinants of long-term pressure

  • The degree of pressure shift of the renal output curve.
  • The level of salt and water intake.

With the curves as drawn in the book, mean pressure readjusts to 100 mm Hg. Examples from the figures:

  • Shifting the renal output curve 50 mm Hg to the right moves the equilibrium point 50 mm Hg higher.
  • Raising intake fourfold moves the equilibrium point to 160 mm Hg, 60 above normal. Lowering intake lowers pressure.
  • It is impossible to change long-term mean pressure without changing one or both determinants. After a change, pressure is regulated at the point where the two new curves cross.
Watch out

The chapter does not use one value for normal mean arterial pressure. The equilibrium point and most examples use 100 mm Hg (pages 230, 233, 241, 243). The hypertension section says hypertension is a mean pressure above 110 mm Hg and that normal is about 90 mm Hg (page 234). In an exam question, use the number that the question gives. If none is given, 100 mm Hg is the figure used in the renal-output explanations.

Salt sensitivity and the chronic curve

  • The chronic renal output curve (days to months) is much steeper than the acute curve.
  • Reason: a pressure rise has direct effects on the kidney and also indirect nervous and hormonal effects. A higher pressure lowers sympathetic activity and the formation of angiotensin II and aldosterone, which normally reduce salt and water excretion. Less of them amplifies pressure natriuresis.
  • When pressure falls, the sympathetic system is activated and antinatriuretic hormones rise, which adds to the direct effect.
TypeRenal output curveEffect of salt intakeCauses
Salt-insensitive (most people)SteepIntake from one-sixth to six times normal changes pressure by only a few mm HgNormal kidneys and hormones
Salt-sensitiveFlattened, like the acute curveEven moderate salt increases raise pressure significantlyLoss of functioning nephrons; excess angiotensin II or aldosterone
  • Examples that make pressure salt-sensitive: surgical reduction of kidney mass, and kidney injury from hypertension, diabetes or kidney disease.
  • Long-term high salt intake over several years may damage the kidneys and make pressure more salt-sensitive.

Resistance, volume and salt

Total peripheral resistance does not set long-term pressure

  • Pressure = cardiac output × total peripheral resistance, so an acute rise in resistance raises pressure at once.
  • If the kidneys work normally, pressure returns to normal within about 1 or 2 days. Raising resistance everywhere except in the kidneys does not change the kidneys' equilibrium point. The kidneys lose salt and water until pressure is back, and extracellular and blood volume end up below normal.
  • Figure 19-5: in conditions with much lower or higher long-term resistance but normal renal excretion (beriberi, AV shunts, hyperthyroidism, anaemia, pulmonary disease, Paget disease, hypothyroidism, removal of four limbs), cardiac output changes in the opposite direction and arterial pressure is normal.
  • Caution: resistance that also raises intrarenal resistance can shift the renal curve and cause hypertension. The culprit is the renal resistance, not the total peripheral resistance.

How extra fluid raises pressure

  1. Extracellular fluid volume rises.
  2. Blood volume rises.
  3. Mean circulatory filling pressure rises.
  4. Venous return rises.
  5. Cardiac output rises.
  6. Arterial pressure rises, directly and by autoregulation (excess tissue flow makes vessels constrict, raising total peripheral resistance).
  • Only a 5% to 10% rise in cardiac output, with a rise in resistance from autoregulation or other vasoconstriction, can raise pressure from 100 to 150 mm Hg. The small rise in output is often not measurable.

Why salt matters more than water

  • Pure water is excreted almost as fast as it is drunk; salt is not.
  • Extra salt raises extracellular osmolality. This stimulates the thirst centre and the hypothalamic-posterior pituitary release of antidiuretic hormone, which makes the kidneys reabsorb more water. Both raise extracellular fluid volume.
  • This raises pressure only if blood volume rises and vascular capacity does not. With normal kidneys and hormones, extra salt is quickly excreted and pressure barely changes.

Chronic hypertension and volume loading

  • Chronic hypertension: mean pressure above the accepted normal range. The book gives a mean above 110 mm Hg as hypertensive, which corresponds to diastolic above about 90 and systolic above about 135 mm Hg.
  • Severe hypertension: mean 150 to 170 mm Hg, diastolic up to 130, systolic sometimes up to 250 mm Hg.
  • At mean pressures 50% or more above normal, expected survival without treatment is only a few years.

Excess workload on the heartEarly heart failure, coronary heart disease, heart attack
Damage to a brain vesselCerebral infarct (stroke): paralysis, dementia, blindness or death
Injury to the kidneysMany areas of destruction, kidney failure, uremia, death

Volume-loading hypertension

Volume-loading hypertension is caused by excess extracellular fluid. It taught the importance of the renal-body fluid mechanism.

  • Dog experiment: removing 70% of kidney mass (30% left) raised pressure by only 6 mm Hg. Drinking 0.9% saline (which does not quench thirst, so intake rose two to four times) raised pressure by about 40 mm Hg within days. Back to tap water, pressure normalised within 2 days; saline again raised it faster.
  • Why: the kidneys could not excrete the load, so volume built up until pressure was high enough to excrete it.

Two stages

  1. Stage 1: intake raised to about six times normal; extracellular fluid, blood volume and cardiac output rise 20% to 40%. Pressure rises less at first because total peripheral resistance falls (baroreceptor reflex). After 2 to 4 days the baroreceptors reset and pressure rises almost fully, driven by cardiac output.
  2. Stage 2: over weeks, autoregulation constricts arterioles, so total peripheral resistance rises and cardiac output falls back toward normal. Lower capillary pressure lets tissue fluid return to the blood and the high pressure makes the kidneys excrete the excess, so volumes also return nearly to normal.

So the high resistance appears after the hypertension and is secondary to it, not its cause.

In the clinic: dialysis

In a patient with no kidneys on an artificial kidney, fluid volume must be kept normal by removing water and salt at each dialysis. Otherwise hypertension develops in the same two stages and finally looks like a high-resistance hypertension.

Excess aldosterone

  • Primary aldosteronism (a small adrenal tumour secreting aldosterone) raises salt and water reabsorption, blood volume, extracellular volume and pressure. More salt intake makes it worse.
  • Over months or years the high pressure damages the kidneys, which then retain even more salt and water, so it can become lethal.
  • Same pattern as above: output is raised early, then returns almost to normal while resistance becomes secondarily high.

The renin-angiotensin system

Components

  1. Renin is an enzyme made and stored in the juxtaglomerular (JG) cells, modified smooth muscle cells in the afferent arterioles just before the glomeruli. It is released when arterial pressure falls.
  2. Renin acts on angiotensinogen (renin substrate, a plasma globulin) to release the 10-amino acid angiotensin I, which is only a mild vasoconstrictor.
  3. Within seconds to minutes, angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE), mostly in the lung vessel endothelium, splits off two amino acids to make the 8-amino acid angiotensin II. Kidneys and vessels also have ACE.
  4. Angiotensin II is inactivated within 1 or 2 minutes by angiotensinases.
Stimulus for renin releaseDetail
Low pressurePressure-sensitive baroreceptors in the JG cells
Low NaCl deliveryFewer chloride and sodium ions reach the macula densa (early distal tubule)
Sympathetic activityActs on beta-adrenergic receptors of JG cells

Renin stays in the blood for 30 to 60 minutes and keeps making angiotensin I. Strong sympathetic activation also acts on alpha-adrenergic receptors, which can increase renal NaCl reabsorption and reduce glomerular filtration rate.

Two main actions of angiotensin II

  • Fast: vasoconstriction, intense in arterioles and mild in veins. Arteriolar constriction raises total peripheral resistance; venous constriction raises venous return.
  • Slow: salt and water retention by the kidneys, which raises extracellular volume over hours and days. This is more powerful than the vasoconstriction for long-term pressure.
Way angiotensin II makes the kidney retain salt and waterHow
Direct, vascularConstricts renal arterioles, especially the efferent ones. Slower flow lowers peritubular capillary pressure, so tubule fluid is reabsorbed more
Direct, tubularActs on tubule cells to increase sodium and water reabsorption
IndirectStrong stimulator of aldosterone secretion from the adrenals; aldosterone increases sodium reabsorption in the tubules

Combined, these can reduce urine output to less than one-fifth of normal. Excess angiotensin II sets the whole renal-body fluid mechanism to a higher pressure.

Speed and strength

  • After a hemorrhage that dropped pressure to 50 mm Hg, pressure rose to 83 mm Hg with the system working, but only to 60 mm Hg with it blocked by a renin antibody. It restores pressure at least halfway within minutes, so it can be lifesaving in shock.
  • It needs about 20 minutes to be fully active, slower than nervous reflexes.
  • Dog experiment: blocking angiotensin II formation with an ACE inhibitor gave an equilibrium pressure of 75 mm Hg; infusing angiotensin II at about 2.5 times the normal rate gave 115 mm Hg. The renal output curve shifts to higher pressures.

Coping with salt intake

  1. More salt intake raises extracellular volume and pressure a little.
  2. Renin and angiotensin II formation fall.
  3. The kidneys retain less salt and water, so the extra salt is excreted.
  4. Volume and pressure return almost to normal. Lower salt intake does the opposite.
  • With a normal system, pressure rises no more than 4 to 6 mm Hg for up to a 100-fold rise in salt intake.
  • If angiotensin II is infused continuously so it cannot fall, the same rise in salt can raise pressure by 40 mm Hg or more.
  • When salt intake is cut to one-tenth of normal, pressure barely changes. With ACE blocked, pressure falls markedly.
  • Page 244 adds that salt intake can fall to one-tenth of normal or rise to 10 to 15 times normal with only a few mm Hg change in pressure if the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system works.

Hypertension from renin and renal ischaemia

  • Renin-secreting tumour of the JG cells: large amounts of angiotensin II and severe hypertension in every reported case. Long infusions of angiotensin II in animals do the same.
  • Angiotensin II raises pressure by arteriolar constriction (within seconds) and by renal salt and water retention (over days). The renal action is the principal cause of long-term elevation.

Other types of hypertension

Coarctation of the aorta

  • Present in one of every few thousand babies: a constriction beyond the head and arm branches but proximal to the renal arteries. The lower body is supplied by small collateral arteries with high resistance.
  • Upper-body pressure may be 40% to 50% higher than lower-body pressure. The mechanism is almost the same as one-kidney Goldblatt hypertension: kidney pressure falls first, renin, angiotensin and aldosterone rise, then the kidney pressure returns to about normal and renin returns to nearly normal while upper-body pressure stays high.
  • Blood flow in the arms (pressure 40% to 60% above normal) and in the legs is almost exactly normal. The reason is near-complete long-term autoregulation. Blood and nerve supply are the same in both regions, so it is not a hormonal or nervous difference.

Preeclampsia (toxemia of pregnancy)

  • Develops in about 5% to 10% of expectant mothers; the hypertension usually subsides after delivery.
  • Believed to involve placental ischaemia and toxic factors that cause endothelial dysfunction, with less nitric oxide and other vasodilators. The results are vasoconstriction, lower glomerular filtration, impaired pressure natriuresis and hypertension.
  • Thickened glomerular membranes (perhaps autoimmune) may also contribute. These patients are prone to extra hypertension with excess salt.

Neurogenic hypertension

  • Acute: excitement or anxiety strongly stimulates the sympathetic system, causing vasoconstriction everywhere.
  • Cutting the baroreceptor nerves or destroying the tractus solitarius removes the inhibitory signal, so mean pressure can rise from 100 to as high as 160 mm Hg. It returns nearly to normal within about 2 days because of central resetting. So it is mainly acute, not chronic.
  • The sympathetic system does matter in some chronic hypertension, mainly through the renal sympathetic nerves (for example in obesity), which impair pressure natriuresis.

Genetic causes

  • Inherited hypertension exists in several animal strains, for example the Okamoto spontaneously hypertensive rat (sympathetic system more active early; later increased preglomerular resistance and lower glomerular permeability).
  • In humans, monogenic hypertension comes from a single gene mutation. All known forms impair kidney function (higher renal arteriolar resistance, or more tubular salt and water reabsorption, directly or through hormones). They account for less than 1% of human hypertension.
Watch out

The book gives two figures for the pressure rise in the arms in coarctation. It says upper-body pressure may be 40% to 50% higher than lower-body pressure, and later that arm pressure may be 40% to 60% above normal. They are different comparisons (upper versus lower body, and arms versus normal), so read the wording of the question.

Primary (essential) hypertension

  • 90% to 95% of hypertensive people have primary (essential) hypertension: unknown origin, as opposed to secondary forms with a known cause (renal artery stenosis, monogenic forms).
  • Excess weight and a sedentary lifestyle play a major role. Excess adiposity may account for 65% to 75% of the risk. Weight loss and exercise are the first step in treatment.
Feature of obesity-related hypertensionDetail
Cardiac outputIncreased, partly because the extra tissue needs more flow; with sustained hypertension over months and years, resistance may rise

Angiotensin II and aldosteroneIncreased in many obese patients, partly through sympathetic stimulation of renin release

  • If mean pressure is 150 mm Hg in essential hypertension, an acute drop to 100 mm Hg with no other change would cause almost total anuria, and salt and water would be retained until pressure rose back to 150. Effective chronic treatment improves pressure natriuresis, so it does not cause marked retention.
  • Untreated, vascular damage in the kidneys can lower glomerular filtration rate and worsen hypertension, and may end in complete loss of kidney function.

Salt-insensitive and salt-sensitive essential hypertension

  • On a sodium-loading renal function curve, both types are shifted to the right (higher pressure). In salt-insensitive, pressure does not rise much when salt intake rises; in salt-sensitive, high salt makes the hypertension significantly worse.
  • Salt sensitivity is quantitative, not all-or-none, and not fixed: it usually increases with age, especially after 50 or 60 years, as nephron number falls.
  • Causes: gradual nephron loss (chronic kidney disease or ageing) or abnormal renin-angiotensin function.

Treatment

  1. First step: lifestyle changes, more physical activity and weight loss.
  2. If that fails, drugs. Vasodilators increase renal blood flow and glomerular filtration rate. They act by blocking sympathetic signals or transmitter action, relaxing renal vascular smooth muscle directly, or blocking the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system.
  3. Natriuretic or diuretic drugs reduce tubular reabsorption of salt and water, mainly by blocking active sodium transport.

Integrated pressure regulation

Pressure is controlled by several interrelated systems. After severe bleeding there are two problems: first survival (return pressure to a survivable level fast), then returning blood volume and pressure to normal.

Time scaleMechanismsNotes
SecondsBaroreceptor feedback; central nervous system ischaemic response; chemoreceptor mechanismNervous reflexes. After a fall in pressure: veins constrict, heart rate and contractility rise, most arterioles constrict. They act in reverse if pressure rises
Minutes to hoursRenin-angiotensin vasoconstriction; stress relaxation; capillary fluid shiftMostly active within 30 minutes to several hours, while nervous mechanisms fade
Hours, days and longerRenal-body fluid mechanism (with aldosterone)Begins in a few hours; feedback gain eventually nearly infinite
  • Stress relaxation: vessels stretched by high pressure for minutes or hours relax, so pressure falls toward normal. An intermediate-term buffer.
  • Capillary fluid shift: if capillary pressure is low, fluid is absorbed into the circulation; if high, fluid is lost into the tissues.
  • The renal mechanism returns pressure nearly all the way, not just partway, to the level that gives normal salt and water output.
  • Aldosterone: a fall in pressure raises its secretion within minutes, and over hours or days it adjusts the renal-body fluid mechanism.
  • Without a working renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, pressure becomes very sensitive to salt intake.

The order is: nervous controls save life, intermediate controls sustain, and the renal-body fluid mechanism stabilises the long-term level.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • Raising total peripheral resistance alone does not raise long-term pressure if renal function and intake are unchanged.
  • The renin-angiotensin system takes about 20 minutes; it is slower than nervous reflexes, not faster.
  • Angiotensin I is not the powerful vasoconstrictor; angiotensin II is.
  • The lung is the main site of ACE, not the kidney, although kidneys and vessels also have it.
  • Angiotensin II's renal salt and water retention is a more powerful long-term effect than its vasoconstriction.
  • In volume-loading hypertension, cardiac output is high first; resistance rises later while output returns toward normal.
  • Cutting the baroreceptor nerves causes acute hypertension that fades in about 2 days.
  • Salt raises pressure more than water because pure water is excreted quickly.
  • In coarctation the blood flow in arms and legs is about normal despite different pressures.
