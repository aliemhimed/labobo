Transport of O₂ and CO₂ in blood and tissue fluids

How O₂ moves from alveolus to cell and CO₂ moves back: the pressures along the chain, the role of hemoglobin, the O₂-hemoglobin dissociation curve and what shifts it, carbon monoxide, the three forms of CO₂ transport, the Haldane effect and the respiratory exchange ratio. Written from Guyton & Hall 14e, chapter 41 (no deck for this week).

Labobo topic: Physio Lec 14: Transport of O₂ and CO₂ in Blood and Tissue Fluids

In 60 seconds

  • PO₂ chain: alveolus 104, systemic arterial blood 95 (venous admixture), interstitial fluid 40, cell average 23. Cells need only 1-3 mm Hg.
  • About 97% of O₂ is carried on hemoglobin, 3% dissolved. Arterial saturation 97%, venous 75%. Each 100 ml of blood delivers about 5 ml O₂ at rest.
  • Curve shifts right (more O₂ released) with more H⁺, CO₂, temperature and BPG: the Bohr effect.
  • Hemoglobin is a tissue O₂ buffer: tissue PO₂ stays between about 15 and 40 mm Hg.
  • CO binds hemoglobin about 250 times as tightly as O₂, with a normal PO₂ and no cyanosis.
  • CO₂: about 70% bicarbonate (carbonic anhydrase in red cells), carbamino and dissolved make up the rest. Venous PCO₂ is 45, arterial 40.
  • Haldane effect: O₂ binding releases CO₂. R = CO₂ output / O₂ uptake: 1.00 for carbohydrate, 0.7 for fat, 0.825 mixed diet.

O₂ uptake in the lungs

  • Hemoglobin lets blood carry 30 to 100 times more O₂ than dissolved O₂ alone. CO₂ combines with blood chemicals that raise its transport 15- to 20-fold.
  • Transport depends on diffusion (down partial pressure gradients) and on blood flow.
  • Alveolar PO₂ is 104; venous blood arriving at the capillary has PO₂ 40. The starting gradient is 64 mm Hg.
  • Blood PO₂ reaches almost 104 by about one-third of the way along the capillary. Normally the blood stays about three times as long as needed for full oxygenation.

Exercise

  • O₂ need can reach 20 times normal and capillary transit time can fall to less than half normal. Yet blood still leaves almost fully saturated, because of the safety factor.
  • Reasons: O₂ diffusing capacity rises almost threefold (more capillary surface, better ventilation-perfusion in the upper lung), and the blood normally has spare time in the capillary.

O₂ in arterial blood and tissues

  • About 98% of blood entering the left atrium came through alveolar capillaries (PO₂ about 104). About 2% passed through the bronchial circulation and is shunt flow at about venous PO₂ (40).
  • This venous admixture lowers aortic PO₂ to about 95 mm Hg.

Capillary to tissue

  • Arterial blood reaches tissue capillaries at PO₂ 95; interstitial PO₂ is about 40. O₂ diffuses so fast that blood leaving the capillary is also about 40.
  • More blood flow raises tissue PO₂: flow at 400% of normal raises it from 40 to 66 mm Hg. The limit is 95 (arterial PO₂). Less flow lowers it.
  • More metabolism lowers tissue PO₂; less metabolism raises it. Tissue PO₂ = balance between O₂ delivery and O₂ use.
  • Intracellular PO₂ ranges from 5 to 40 and averages 23 mm Hg; only 1-3 mm Hg is needed to run the cell's oxygen-using reactions, so there is a large safety factor.
Watch out

The caption of Fig 41-3 reads "in tissue cells, Pco2 = 23 mm Hg". This is a printing slip: 23 mm Hg is the average intracellular PO₂, as the text states. The cell PCO₂ is 46 mm Hg.

CO₂ diffusion

Virtually all O₂ used becomes CO₂. At every step CO₂ moves opposite to O₂, and about 20 times faster, so it needs much smaller gradients.

Tissue flow and metabolism

  • They act on PCO₂ opposite to their effect on PO₂.
  • Flow down to one-quarter of normal: interstitial PCO₂ rises from 45 to 60. Flow up to six times normal: it falls to 41, near arterial 40.
  • Metabolism up 10-fold greatly raises PCO₂ at all flows; down to one-quarter lowers it to about 41.

Hemoglobin and the dissociation curve

  • 97% of O₂ is carried combined with hemoglobin; 3% is dissolved.
  • O₂ binds the heme loosely and reversibly: bound at high PO₂ (lungs), released at low PO₂ (tissues).
  • The O₂-hemoglobin dissociation curve plots percent saturation against PO₂. Arterial blood (PO₂ about 95) is 97% saturated; venous blood (PO₂ 40) is 75%.

How much O₂ is carried

  1. Blood has about 15 g hemoglobin per 100 ml.
  2. Each gram binds a maximum of 1.34 ml O₂ (1.39 for pure hemoglobin; impurities such as methemoglobin lower it).
  3. 15 × 1.34 = about 20 ml per 100 ml at full saturation: 20 volume percent.
  4. At 97% saturation: 19.4 ml per 100 ml. Venous (75%): 14.4 ml.
  5. Difference: about 5 ml delivered to tissues per 100 ml of blood.

Exercise

  • Muscle interstitial PO₂ can fall from 40 to as low as 15 mm Hg; only 4.4 ml O₂ stays bound. Delivery is 19.4 - 4.4 = 15 ml per 100 ml: three times normal.
  • Cardiac output can rise 6-7 times in trained marathon runners, so O₂ transport rises 6-7 × 3, about 20-fold.

Utilization coefficient

The fraction of hemoglobin-bound O₂ given up to tissues: normally about 25%; in strenuous exercise 75-85% for the whole body; near 100% in local areas of slow flow or high metabolism.

Hemoglobin as a tissue O₂ buffer

  • Upper limit of tissue PO₂: tissues need 5 ml per 100 ml, which is released at a PO₂ of about 40. So tissue PO₂ cannot rise above about 40.
  • Heavy exercise: much more O₂ is released with a small PO₂ fall because the curve is steep and flow rises. Tissue PO₂ stays between about 15 and 40.
  • Low alveolar O₂: at alveolar PO₂ 60, arterial saturation is still 89% (8% below normal). Venous PO₂ falls only to 35 (5 below normal).
  • High alveolar O₂: even at 500 mm Hg, saturation cannot exceed 100%, only 3% above normal. Tissue PO₂ stays near normal.
  • So alveolar PO₂ can vary from about 60 to over 500 with little change in tissue PO₂.

Bohr effect

  • In tissues, CO₂ enters the blood, raising carbonic acid and H⁺. The curve shifts right and downward, forcing O₂ off hemoglobin to the tissues.
  • In lungs, CO₂ leaves; PCO₂ and H⁺ fall; the curve shifts left and upward, so more O₂ binds at any alveolar PO₂.

BPG

  • Normal BPG keeps the curve slightly right. In hypoxia lasting more than a few hours, BPG rises considerably and the curve shifts further right.
  • O₂ is then released at up to 10 mm Hg higher tissue pressure. This helps adaptation, especially to hypoxia from poor tissue blood flow.

Exercise

  • Muscles release CO₂ and acids (more H⁺) and warm by 2 to 3 °C. Together these shift the curve well to the right.
  • O₂ is released at PO₂ up to 40 even after 70% of the O₂ has left hemoglobin. In the lungs the shift reverses and extra O₂ is picked up.

Metabolic use of O₂, dissolved O₂

  • When intracellular PO₂ is above 1 mm Hg, O₂ is no longer limiting. The usage rate depends on the ADP concentration: ATP use makes ADP, which drives O₂ use. So cell O₂ use is set by energy expenditure.
  • Cells are seldom more than 50 µm from a capillary. Diffusion-limited O₂ use (cell too far away) occurs almost only in disease.
  • Blood flow-limited use: if flow is low enough that tissue PO₂ drops below 1 mm Hg. Neither state can last long, as cells then die.

Dissolved O₂

  • At PO₂ 95: 0.29 ml per 100 ml; at 40: 0.12 ml. Only 0.17 ml is delivered dissolved, compared with almost 5 ml by hemoglobin (about 3% of the total).
  • In strenuous exercise the dissolved share falls to about 1.5%.
  • Breathing O₂ at very high alveolar PO₂ raises the dissolved amount so much that O₂ poisoning can occur: brain convulsions and even death (as in deep-sea divers).

Carbon monoxide

  • CO binds at the same site on hemoglobin as O₂, so it displaces O₂ and lowers carrying capacity. It binds about 250 times as tightly.
  • The CO-hemoglobin curve is like the O₂ curve but at 1/250 of the pressures. A CO pressure of only 0.4 mm Hg competes equally with O₂ (alveolar PO₂ about 100) and binds 50% of hemoglobin. A pressure of 0.6 mm Hg (under 1 part in 1000 of air) can be lethal.
  • Blood O₂ content is low but PO₂ may be normal. The blood is bright red, there is no cyanosis, and the PO₂-driven stimulus to breathe is absent. The person may lose consciousness before noticing danger.

Treatment: pure O₂ at high alveolar pressure displaces CO. Adding 5% CO₂ strongly stimulates the respiratory center, raising ventilation and lowering alveolar CO. With both, CO leaves up to 10 times faster.

CO₂ transport

Resting blood carries about 4 ml of CO₂ per 100 ml from tissues to lungs. CO₂ transport is easier than O₂ transport but strongly affects acid-base balance.

Bicarbonate pathway

  1. Dissolved CO₂ enters the red cell.
  2. Carbonic anhydrase (in red cells) speeds CO₂ + H₂O to H₂CO₃ about 5000-fold; equilibrium in a fraction of a second.
  3. H₂CO₃ dissociates into H⁺ and HCO₃⁻. Most H⁺ is buffered by hemoglobin.
  4. HCO₃⁻ leaves the cell into plasma through a bicarbonate-chloride carrier, and Cl⁻ enters: the chloride shift. Venous red cells contain more chloride than arterial ones.
  • If carbonic anhydrase is blocked (acetazolamide), tissue PCO₂ may rise to 80 instead of 45.
  • Plasma proteins carry carbamino CO₂ too, but they are only one-fourth as abundant as hemoglobin, so their share is small. Carbamino binding is loose and reversible, so CO₂ is easily released in the lungs.
Watch out

The chapter gives the carbamino share three ways: about 30% (1.5 ml per 100 ml) in the text; "no more than 20%" because the reaction is slow, in the same paragraph; and 23% in Fig 41-13 (with dissolved 7% and bicarbonate 70%, which add to 100%). Use 7% / 70% / 23% as the standard split; know that the text itself doubts that carbamino carries more than 20%.

CO₂ curve and the Haldane effect

  • The CO₂ dissociation curve shows total blood CO₂ against PCO₂. Normal PCO₂ is 40 (arterial) to 45 (venous).
  • Total blood CO₂ is about 50 volume percent; only 4 is exchanged: about 52 after the tissues and 48 after the lungs.

Haldane effect

Binding of O₂ to hemoglobin displaces CO₂ from the blood. It is far more important for CO₂ transport than the Bohr effect is for O₂ transport.

  1. In the lungs, O₂ binding makes hemoglobin a stronger acid.
  2. It binds CO₂ less well, so carbamino CO₂ is released.
  3. It releases more H⁺, which combines with HCO₃⁻ to form H₂CO₃, which splits into water and CO₂, and the CO₂ leaves into the alveoli.
  • Without the shift, blood CO₂ would fall only from 52 to 50 (2 volume percent). With it, to 48 (another 2): the Haldane effect about doubles CO₂ release in the lungs and CO₂ pickup in the tissues.
  • It is shown by the two CO₂ curves at PO₂ 100 (lungs) and PO₂ 40 (tissues).

Blood pH and the respiratory exchange ratio

  • CO₂ entering the blood forms carbonic acid, but blood buffers limit the change. Arterial pH about 7.41, venous about 7.37: a change of 0.04.
  • In heavy exercise, high metabolism or sluggish flow, tissue pH can fall by as much as 0.50 (about 12 times normal), causing significant tissue acidosis.

Rest: about 5 ml O₂ delivered and about 4 ml CO₂ removed per 100 ml, so only about 82% as much CO₂ is expired as O₂ taken up.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • Aortic PO₂ (95) is lower than alveolar (104) because of 2% shunt (venous admixture).
  • Hemoglobin saturation at alveolar PO₂ 60 is still about 89%, not 60%.
  • Only about 5 ml of the 19.4 ml O₂ bound is released at rest; venous blood is still 75% saturated.
  • CO poisoning gives normal PO₂, bright red blood, no cyanosis and no extra drive to breathe.
  • A cell needs only 1-3 mm Hg PO₂; the cell average is 23, not 95.
  • Most CO₂ is carried as bicarbonate (70%), not dissolved.
  • The Bohr effect gives right shift with higher CO₂; do not reverse it.
  • R is 1.00 for carbohydrate and 0.7 for fat; the resting average is 0.825.
  • Fig 41-3 caption says "Pco2 = 23" but means PO₂.

  1. What is the initial O₂ pressure gradient across the lung membrane at the start of the pulmonary capillary?

    2. 64 mm Hg

    B. Alveolar 104 minus venous 40 equals 64 mm Hg.

  2. About what fraction of the blood entering the left atrium comes through the bronchial circulation as shunt flow?

    1. 2%
    2. 10%
    3. 25%
    4. 50%
    Show answer

    A. About 2%; the other 98% has passed the alveolar capillaries.

  3. Tissue blood flow rises to 400% of normal. Interstitial PO₂ rises from 40 to about:

    1. 50 mm Hg
    2. 66 mm Hg
    3. 95 mm Hg
    4. 104 mm Hg
    Show answer

    B. To 66 mm Hg; the upper limit is the arterial PO₂ of 95.

  4. The interstitial PCO₂ in a tissue at one-quarter of normal blood flow is about:

    1. 41 mm Hg
    2. 45 mm Hg
    3. 60 mm Hg
    4. 80 mm Hg
    Show answer

    C. It rises from 45 to about 60 mm Hg.

  5. How many ml of O₂ can 1 g of pure-enough hemoglobin carry at most (the value used for blood)?

    1. 0.29 ml
    2. 1.34 ml
    3. 5 ml
    4. 20 ml
    Show answer

    B. 1.34 ml per gram; 15 g gives about 20 ml per 100 ml.

  6. In heavy exercise muscle interstitial PO₂ falls to 15 mm Hg. How much O₂ does each 100 ml of blood then deliver?

    1. About 5 ml
    2. About 10 ml
    3. About 15 ml
    4. About 19 ml
    Show answer

    C. 19.4 minus 4.4 equals 15 ml, three times the resting value.

  7. Which statement about the utilization coefficient is correct?

    1. It is about 75% at rest
    2. It is about 25% at rest and can reach 75-85% in strenuous exercise
    3. It never exceeds 50%
    4. It is the same as hemoglobin saturation
    Show answer

    B. It is the share of hemoglobin-bound O₂ given up in the tissues.

  8. After a few hours of hypoxia, BPG rises. What is the main benefit?

    1. It increases hemoglobin saturation in the lungs
    2. It shifts the curve further right so O₂ is released at a higher tissue PO₂
    3. It increases CO₂ binding
    4. It reduces blood pH
    Show answer

    B. O₂ can be released at up to 10 mm Hg higher tissue PO₂.

  9. What carbon monoxide pressure in the alveoli binds 50% of hemoglobin?

    1. 0.04 mm Hg
    2. 0.4 mm Hg
    3. 4 mm Hg
    4. 40 mm Hg
    Show answer

    B. About 1/250 of the alveolar PO₂, so 0.4 mm Hg.

  10. The venous red cell contains more chloride than the arterial red cell. This is due to:

    1. The Haldane effect
    2. The Bohr effect
    3. The chloride shift, via the bicarbonate-chloride carrier
    4. Carbonic anhydrase in plasma
    Show answer

    C. HCO₃⁻ leaves the cell and Cl⁻ enters in exchange.

  11. In the lungs, how does O₂ binding to hemoglobin help CO₂ leave the blood?

    1. It makes hemoglobin a stronger acid, so it releases carbamino CO₂ and H⁺, and H⁺ turns HCO₃⁻ into CO₂
    2. It raises blood pH
    3. It blocks carbonic anhydrase
    4. It moves the curve to the right
    Show answer

    A. This is the Haldane effect; it doubles the CO₂ released in the lungs.

Key terms

Venous admixture: shunt blood mixing with oxygenated blood · Percent saturation: share of hemoglobin bound to O₂ · Volume percent: ml of gas per 100 ml of blood · Utilization coefficient: share of O₂ given up in tissues · Bohr effect: CO₂ and H⁺ shift the curve right · BPG: 2,3-biphosphoglycerate, shifts the curve right · Tissue O₂ buffer: hemoglobin holds tissue PO₂ near 15-40 mm Hg · Carbon monoxide: binds hemoglobin about 250 times more tightly than O₂ · Carbonic anhydrase: red cell enzyme that speeds CO₂ + H₂O · Chloride shift: Cl⁻ enters red cells as HCO₃⁻ leaves · Carbaminohemoglobin: CO₂ bound to hemoglobin amine groups · Haldane effect: O₂ binding displaces CO₂ · Respiratory exchange ratio (R): CO₂ output / O₂ uptake · Acetazolamide: carbonic anhydrase inhibitor.
