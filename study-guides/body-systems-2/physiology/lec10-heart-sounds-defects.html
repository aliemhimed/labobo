Heart sounds, valvular and congenital heart defects

What causes the four heart sounds and where to listen to them, how rheumatic fever and ageing damage the valves, the murmur of each valvular lesion, the circulatory changes in aortic and mitral disease, the congenital defects (patent ductus arteriosus and tetralogy of Fallot), and cardiac hypertrophy. Source: Guyton and Hall, 14th ed., chapter 23.

Labobo topic: Physio Lec 10: Heart Sounds, Valvular and Congenital Heart Defects

In 60 seconds

  • Valves make sound when they close, not when they open. S1 (lub) is closure of the A-V valves at the start of systole; S2 (dub) is closure of the aortic and pulmonary valves at the end of systole.
  • S1 lasts about 0.14 s and S2 about 0.11 s; S2 has the higher pitch. S3 comes in the middle third of diastole; S4 comes with atrial contraction and is almost never audible.
  • Rheumatic fever (group A streptococcus, autoimmune) damages valves; the mitral valve most often, then the aortic. In ageing, aortic stenosis is now the commonest valve disease.
  • Murmur timing: aortic stenosis and mitral regurgitation are systolic; aortic regurgitation and mitral stenosis are diastolic.
  • Aortic lesions cause left ventricular hypertrophy; mitral lesions cause a high left atrial pressure, atrial fibrillation and pulmonary edema.
  • Patent ductus arteriosus is a left-to-right shunt with a machinery murmur. Tetralogy of Fallot is a right-to-left shunt and the commonest cause of a blue baby.

Normal heart sounds

Closing of the valves causes audible sounds. Ordinarily, no sounds occur when the valves open. A normal heart sounds like "lub, dub".

SoundTiming and causeFeatures
First (S1, "lub")Closure of the A-V valves (tricuspid and mitral) at the start of systoleAbout 0.14 s; lower pitch
Second (S2, "dub")Closure of the aortic and pulmonary valves at the end of systoleAbout 0.11 s; higher pitch
Third (S3)Beginning of the middle third of diastole; weak, rumblingRecorded in only one-third to one-half of people; often too low to hear
Fourth (S4, atrial sound)Atrial contraction, inrush of blood into the ventriclesFrequency 20 cycles/s or less; recorded in perhaps one-fourth of people; almost never heard

How the first sound arises

  1. Ventricular contraction drives blood back against the A-V valves, which close and bulge toward the atria.
  2. The chordae tendineae stop the bulging abruptly.
  3. The taut valves make the blood bounce forward again into the ventricles.
  4. Blood, ventricular walls and valves vibrate and cause turbulence. The vibrations reach the chest wall and are heard.

The main cause is vibration of the taut valves right after closure, along with the adjacent heart and vessel walls.

How the second sound arises

The semilunar valves close and bulge backward toward the ventricles. Their elastic stretch recoils blood into the arteries. This causes a short reverberation of blood between the arterial walls and the valves, and between the valves and the ventricular walls. The vibrations travel mainly along the arteries.

Duration and pitch

  • S2 is shorter because the semilunar valves are tauter than the A-V valves, so they vibrate for a shorter time.
  • S2 has a higher frequency than S1 for two reasons: the semilunar valves are tauter, and the arterial walls (the vibrating chambers for S2) are more elastic than the looser ventricular chambers that vibrate for S1.
  • The audible range begins at about 40 cycles/s (lowest the ear can detect) and goes above 500. Electronic recording shows that more of the sound is below the audible range, from 3 to 4 cycles/s and peaking at about 20 cycles/s. So much can be recorded by phonocardiography but not heard with a stethoscope.

Third and fourth sounds in more detail

  • S3: a logical but unproved explanation is oscillation of blood back and forth between the ventricular walls after inrushing atrial blood, like water poured into a paper sack. It is absent in early diastole because the ventricles are not yet filled enough to create elastic tension.
  • S3 may be normal in children, adolescents and young adults, but generally indicates systolic heart failure in older adults.
  • S4 is common in people who rely on atrial contraction for ventricular filling because of reduced ventricular compliance, for example older patients with left ventricular hypertrophy.
In the clinic: a third heart sound

The same sound can be normal in a young adult and a sign of systolic heart failure in an older adult. Age decides how to read it.

Auscultation and the phonocardiogram

  • Auscultation is listening to body sounds, usually with a stethoscope.
  • The listening areas are not directly over the valves. Sounds from all valves can be heard in all areas; the clinician picks out each valve by moving the stethoscope and noting loudness.
AreaWhere
AorticUpward along the aorta, because sound travels up the aorta
PulmonicUpward along the pulmonary artery
TricuspidOver the right ventricle
MitralOver the apex of the left ventricle, the part nearest the chest surface (the heart is rotated so the rest of the left ventricle lies more posteriorly)

A phonocardiogram is a recording of heart sounds made with a microphone designed for low-frequency sound, amplified and recorded at high speed. Recording A in the book is normal and shows the first, second and third sounds and the very weak atrial sound.

Valve disease: rheumatic fever and ageing

Rheumatic fever

  1. An autoimmune disease usually started by a streptococcal toxin. It begins with an infection by group A hemolytic streptococci (sore throat, scarlet fever or middle ear infection).
  2. The streptococci release proteins; the person makes antibodies that react with the streptococcal protein and with other body tissues, causing immunologic damage for as long as the antibodies persist (1 year or more).
  3. Valve damage is directly related to the concentration and persistence of the antibodies.
  4. Large hemorrhagic, fibrinous, bulbous lesions grow along the inflamed valve edges.
  • The mitral valve is most often seriously damaged (it undergoes the most trauma), the aortic valve is second. The tricuspid and pulmonary valves are usually affected much less because of the low-pressure stresses on the right side.
  • Lesions often occur on adjacent leaflets, so the edges stick together. Later they become scar tissue that permanently fuses leaflets; the free edges become solid scarred masses.
  • Acute glomerular nephritis has a similar immunologic basis.

Stenosis and regurgitation

  • Stenosed valve: leaflets adhere so much that blood cannot flow normally.
  • Regurgitation (backflow): valve edges destroyed so the valve cannot close.
  • Stenosis rarely occurs without some regurgitation, and the reverse.

Aortic valve stenosis in ageing

  • With age the aortic valve thickens, calcifies and stiffens. Aortic valve stenosis is now the most common heart valve disease. Senile calcific aortic stenosis involves calcium deposition and ossification narrowing the orifice.
  • The left ventricle responds with concentric hypertrophy (thicker wall). The pressure gradient across the valve can reach 75 to 100 mm Hg in severe cases.
  • The hypertrophied ventricle becomes fibrotic and tends to be ischaemic. The ejection fraction may be normal and resting output adequate, but heart failure symptoms may appear with moderate exercise. Later systolic function falls and congestive heart failure appears.
  • It usually does not draw attention until after age 70. Symptoms: exertional angina, reduced exercise tolerance, congestive heart failure, and dyspnea. Untreated symptomatic stenosis is progressive and fatal.
  • Transcatheter aortic valve replacement offers treatment for older patients who cannot have conventional surgery.

Murmurs of valvular lesions

Abnormal heart sounds from valve abnormalities are heart murmurs.

LesionTimingQuality and causeWhere heard
Aortic stenosisSystolicLoud and harsh. Blood jets through the small opening ("nozzle effect") with severe turbulence in the aortic root; may be heard several feet away; a thrill can be feltThroughout the superior thoracic aorta and into the large neck arteries
Mitral regurgitationSystolicHigh-frequency blowing, swishing, from backflow into the left atriumTransmitted mainly through the left ventricle to the apex
Aortic regurgitationDiastolicBlowing, relatively high-pitched, swishing, from blood jetting back from the aorta into the left ventricleMaximally over the left ventricle
Mitral stenosisDiastolicWeak, very low-frequency rumble; left atrial pressure seldom exceeds 30 mm Hg so the pressure difference is smallMuch of the spectrum is below the audible range
Patent ductus arteriosusMostly systolic, waxing and waning with each beatMachinery murmur, harsh and blowingPulmonary artery area
  • Aortic stenosis gives the loudest murmur and mitral stenosis the weakest.
  • In aortic stenosis the left ventricular pressure sometimes rises as high as 300 mm Hg while the aortic pressure stays normal.
  • In mitral stenosis no murmur may be heard in the first third of diastole: the ventricle is nearly empty and flabby, so blood cannot reverberate. Once it is partly filled and stretched, the low rumbling murmur begins.
  • In aortic regurgitation there is no sound in systole.
Memory aid: which murmurs are systolic

Only aortic stenosis and mitral regurgitation are heard in systole. The other two (aortic regurgitation and mitral stenosis) are heard only in diastole.

Dynamics of aortic stenosis and regurgitation

In stenosis, the ventricle empties poorly. In regurgitation, blood flows back into the ventricle from the aorta after being pumped. In both, the net stroke volume is reduced. Compensations help.

Compensations

CompensationDetail
Left ventricular hypertrophyIn both lesions. In regurgitation the chamber also enlarges to hold the backflow: eccentric hypertrophy (muscle mass may increase four- to fivefold). In severe stenosis the ventricle can develop up to 400 mm Hg: concentric hypertrophy, with thick walls and a smaller cavity; also seen when afterload is raised, as in chronic hypertension
Larger stroke volume in regurgitationStroke volume as great as 250 ml, but as much as 75% returns in diastole and only 25% goes on to the body
Increased blood volumeFrom a slight pressure fall, reflexes and hormones that reduce urine output; red cell mass also rises with mild tissue hypoxia. More venous return gives the ventricle extra power

The two types of hypertrophy: eccentric (cavity enlarges, seen in aortic or mitral regurgitation, ventricular septal defect, systolic dysfunction) and concentric (wall thickens, seen in aortic stenosis and chronic hypertension).

Problems

  • Coronary flow can be inadequate. In stenosis, high wall tension cuts coronary flow in systole (especially in subendocardial vessels), and high diastolic pressure compresses the inner layers. Severe stenosis often causes ischaemia.
  • In regurgitation, diastolic ventricular pressure is also raised, and the aortic diastolic pressure falls, so coronary flow can fall too.
  • Early on, resting function is near normal. Considerable degrees of disease occur before the person knows. For example, resting left ventricular systolic pressure may be as high as 200 mm Hg in stenosis, or stroke volume as high as twice normal in regurgitation.
  • Failure: beyond a critical stage the left ventricle dilates and cardiac output falls. Blood dams up in the left atrium and lungs. At mean left atrial pressures above 25 to 40 mm Hg, serious pulmonary edema appears.
Watch out

The chapter gives three different left ventricular pressure figures for aortic stenosis. Pressure can rise as high as 300 mm Hg (murmur section), the hypertrophied ventricle may develop up to 400 mm Hg at systolic peak (hypertrophy section), and a resting systolic pressure as high as 200 mm Hg may go unnoticed (early stage). The values are given for different situations and are not corrected to each other. In a question, use the number tied to the situation stated, and do not treat them as one fixed value.

Dynamics of mitral stenosis and regurgitation

In stenosis, flow from the left atrium to the left ventricle is impeded. In regurgitation, much of the blood that entered the ventricle in diastole leaks back to the atrium in systole. Both reduce net movement of blood into the ventricle.

  1. Blood builds up in the left atrium, so left atrial pressure rises.
  2. Pulmonary edema develops, but usually lethal edema does not occur until the mean left atrial pressure exceeds 25 mm Hg (sometimes up to 40 mm Hg) because the lung lymphatics enlarge many times.
  3. The left atrium enlarges, lengthening the path for the electrical impulse. This predisposes to circus movements, so atrial fibrillation is common in late disease, especially mitral stenosis. It further reduces pumping.
  4. Blood dams back to the pulmonary artery; incipient lung edema causes pulmonary arteriolar constriction. Systolic pulmonary arterial and right ventricular pressures can rise to about 60 mm Hg (more than double normal), causing right heart hypertrophy.
  • Compensation: as in aortic disease and many congenital defects, blood volume rises mainly because the kidneys excrete less salt and water. Venous return rises, so cardiac output may fall only slightly until late in the disease.

Exercise with valve lesions

  • Exercise increases venous return, so all abnormalities worsen. Severe symptoms can appear in heavy exercise even when mild disease is silent at rest.
  • Aortic lesions: exercise can cause acute left ventricular failure and then acute pulmonary edema. Mitral disease: serious or even lethal pulmonary edema may follow in as little as 10 minutes.
  • Even in mild to moderate valve disease the cardiac reserve falls in proportion to severity, so output does not rise enough and muscles tire quickly.

Congenital heart defects

A congenital anomaly is a malformation of the heart or its vessels in fetal life. There are three major types:

TypeMeaningExample
StenosisNarrowing of a channel in the heart or a major vesselCongenital aortic valve stenosis; coarctation of the aorta
Left-to-right shuntBlood flows backward from the left heart or aorta to the right heart or pulmonary artery, so it does not go round the systemic circulationPatent ductus arteriosus
Right-to-left shuntBlood flows directly from the right heart to the left heart, bypassing the lungsTetralogy of Fallot
  • Congenital aortic valve stenosis has the same effects as other aortic stenosis: hypertrophy, ischaemia, reduced output, tendency to pulmonary edema.
  • Coarctation of the aorta, often above the level of the diaphragm: pressure above the coarctation is much higher than in the lower body, and some blood goes through small collateral arteries.

Causes and frequency

  • About 8 in 1000 live births.
  • A common cause is a viral infection in the mother in the first trimester, especially German measles (rubella).
  • Other risks: ACE inhibitors, acne medications such as isotretinoin, and alcohol or drug abuse in pregnancy.
  • Some are hereditary (seen in identical twins and in generations). Children of patients surgically treated for congenital heart disease have about 10 times the chance of congenital heart disease. They are often associated with other congenital defects.

Patent ductus arteriosus

The ductus before and after birth

  • Fetus: the lungs are collapsed, so lung vessel resistance is very high and pulmonary arterial pressure is high. The placenta has low resistance, so aortic pressure is lower than normal and lower than pulmonary artery pressure. Almost all pulmonary arterial blood flows through the ductus arteriosus (pulmonary artery to aorta), bypassing the lungs. This is harmless because the placenta oxygenates the blood.
  • Birth: the lungs inflate and lung resistance falls; pulmonary artery pressure falls while aortic pressure rises (placental flow stops). Flow through the ductus reverses (aorta to pulmonary artery).
  • The ductus closes within a few hours to a few days. It is believed to close because the oxygen concentration of the aortic blood now flowing through it is about twice as high as in fetal life; oxygen presumably constricts its muscle.
  • In about 1 of every 5500 babies, it stays open: patent ductus arteriosus (PDA), a left-to-right shunt.

Effects

  • In early months, usually not severe. With growth, the difference between aortic and pulmonary pressures increases, backflow increases and the ductus widens.
  • Recirculation: in an older child, one-half to two-thirds of aortic blood flows back through the ductus into the pulmonary artery, then through the lungs and left heart again, two or more times for every pass round the body.
  • No cyanosis until later in life (heart failure or lung congestion). Early on, arterial blood may be better oxygenated than normal.
  • Main effects: decreased cardiac and respiratory reserve. The left ventricle pumps two or more times the normal output; after hypertrophy the maximum is about four to seven times normal. In exercise, flow to the body cannot rise enough, and the person may weaken or faint.
  • High pulmonary pressures and excess lung flow cause pulmonary congestion and edema. Most untreated patients die of heart disease between ages 20 and 40.
  • Murmur: often no murmur in a newborn; at 1 to 3 years a harsh blowing machinery murmur is heard in the pulmonary artery area, much louder in systole than diastole.
In the clinic: closing a PDA

Surgery ligates the ductus or divides it and closes the ends, one of the first successful heart operations. Catheter-based devices, such as a small metal coil, can close the ductus in infants or children big enough for the procedure.

Tetralogy of Fallot

A right-to-left shunt and the most common cause of a blue baby. Most blood bypasses the lungs, so aortic blood is mainly unoxygenated venous blood.

#Abnormality
1Aorta arises from the right ventricle, or overrides a septal hole and receives blood from both ventricles
2Pulmonary artery stenosis: little blood reaches the lungs, most goes into the aorta
3Ventricular septal hole: left ventricular blood goes into the right ventricle and aorta, or directly into the overriding aorta
4Right ventricular hypertrophy, because the right side pumps against the high aortic pressure
  • As much as 75% of venous return passes straight from the right ventricle into the aorta unoxygenated, causing cyanosis.
  • Other signs: high right ventricular pressure, right ventricular enlargement, and a left-to-right shunt through the septum seen on echocardiography.
  • Surgery: open the pulmonary stenosis, close the septal defect and rebuild the flow pathway into the aorta. Average life expectancy rises from only 3 to 4 years to 50 or more years.

Hypertrophy and extracorporeal circulation

Heart-lung machines

  • It is almost impossible to repair intracardiac defects while the heart pumps. Extracorporeal circulation uses a pump (any type that does not cause hemolysis) and an oxygenating device.
  • Four ways to oxygenate: bubbling O₂ through the blood and removing bubbles; dripping blood over plastic sheets in O₂; passing blood over rotating discs; passing it between thin membranes or through thin tubes permeable to O₂ and CO₂.

Hypertrophy of the heart

  • Hypertrophy is a major adaptation to a higher pressure load or output load. The amount is roughly ventricular output multiplied by the pressure, with emphasis on pressure. The heart may weigh as much as 800 g instead of the normal 300 g.
  • The most common cause is hypertension, but nearly all heart diseases can enlarge the heart.
  • It is usually beneficial, but extreme hypertrophy can cause failure for two reasons: coronary vessels do not grow in proportion, and fibrosis develops, especially in the poorly perfused subendocardial muscle.
  • Result: relative ischaemia, anginal pain, a higher risk of arrhythmias and sudden death from fibrillation.

Commonly confused

PairHow to tell them apart
S1 vs S2S1: A-V valves close, start of systole, longer and lower. S2: semilunar valves close, end of systole, shorter and higher
S3 vs S4S3: middle third of diastole, from ventricular filling. S4: atrial contraction
Stenosis vs regurgitationStenosis: valve will not open fully. Regurgitation: valve will not close fully
Eccentric vs concentric hypertrophyEccentric: chamber enlarges (regurgitation). Concentric: wall thickens, cavity small (stenosis, hypertension)
Mitral vs aortic disease effectsMitral: left atrial pressure, lung edema, atrial fibrillation, right heart load. Aortic: left ventricular hypertrophy and ischaemia
Left-to-right vs right-to-left shuntLeft-to-right (PDA): extra lung flow, no early cyanosis. Right-to-left (Fallot): bypasses lungs, cyanosis

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • Heart sounds come from valve closure; opening makes no audible sound.
  • The listening area is not over the valve itself (mitral is at the apex; aortic is up along the aorta).
  • The mitral valve is damaged most in rheumatic fever; right-sided valves are usually spared.
  • Mitral stenosis gives the weakest murmur; aortic stenosis the loudest.
  • Mitral regurgitation is systolic; mitral stenosis is diastolic.
  • Early PDA patients are not cyanotic, and the newborn may have no murmur.
  • The ductus arteriosus is a fetal channel from the pulmonary artery to the aorta; in the patent state flow goes aorta to pulmonary artery.
  • In tetralogy of Fallot the right ventricle hypertrophies, not the left.
  • Aortic regurgitation lowers aortic diastolic pressure, which can reduce coronary flow.

    3. The chest wall absorbs all sound
    4. It occurs only in diastole

    1. First heart sound
    2. Second heart sound
    3. Third heart sound
    4. Fourth heart sound
    Show answer

    D. A fourth sound is common when atrial contraction aids filling of a stiff ventricle.

  4. After a streptococcal sore throat, a patient develops rheumatic heart disease. Which valve is most likely to be seriously damaged?

    1. Tricuspid
    2. Pulmonary
    3. Aortic
    4. Mitral
    Show answer

    D. The mitral valve, then the aortic.

  5. A loud, harsh murmur during systole with a thrill felt at the lower neck suggests:

    1. Mitral stenosis
    2. Aortic stenosis
    3. Aortic regurgitation
    4. Mitral regurgitation
    Show answer

    B. Blood jets through a narrow aortic opening and the vibrations reach the neck arteries.

  6. A high-pitched blowing murmur is heard only in diastole over the left ventricle. Which lesion is it?

    1. Aortic regurgitation
    2. Mitral regurgitation
    3. Aortic stenosis
    4. Patent ductus arteriosus
    Show answer

    A. Blood jets backward from the aorta into the low-pressure diastolic left ventricle.

  7. Which change is typical of late mitral stenosis?

    1. Falling left atrial pressure
    2. Left atrial enlargement with a tendency to atrial fibrillation
    3. Left ventricular concentric hypertrophy as the main change
    4. Reduced pulmonary arterial pressure
    Show answer

    B. The atrium enlarges and a longer pathway predisposes to circus movements.

  8. In aortic regurgitation, what happens to the aortic diastolic pressure, and what is the consequence for the heart?

    1. It falls, so coronary flow can fall too
    2. It rises, so the myocardium is over-perfused
    3. It is unchanged, so coronary flow is normal
    4. It rises, so the murmur is heard in systole
    Show answer

    A. Aortic diastolic pressure falls and diastolic ventricular pressure is raised, so coronary flow can fall.

  9. In a fetus, blood flows through the ductus arteriosus from the:

    1. Aorta to the pulmonary artery because aortic pressure is higher
    2. Pulmonary artery to the aorta because aortic pressure is lower
    3. Left atrium to the right atrium
    4. Right ventricle to the left ventricle
    Show answer

    B. Fetal lung resistance is high and placental resistance is low.

  10. An 18-month-old with a patent ductus is not cyanotic. The best explanation is that:

    1. The shunt is right-to-left
    2. Blood passes through the lungs extra times, so arterial blood is well oxygenated
    3. The ductus has closed
    4. Hemoglobin is raised
    Show answer

    B. Left-to-right shunting recirculates aortic blood through the lungs.

  11. Which combination is part of tetralogy of Fallot?

    1. Pulmonary stenosis and right ventricular hypertrophy
    2. Aortic stenosis and left ventricular hypertrophy
    3. Patent ductus and coarctation
    4. Mitral stenosis and atrial fibrillation
    Show answer

    A. The four abnormalities also include an overriding aorta and a ventricular septal hole.
