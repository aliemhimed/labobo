Pulmonary circulation, pulmonary edema and pleural fluid

The lung has two circulations. This lecture covers the low-pressure pulmonary vessels, their pressures and blood volume, how blood flow is spread through the lung (hypoxia, gravity, zones 1-3), what happens in exercise and left heart failure, how the alveoli stay dry, why pulmonary edema develops, and how fluid is handled in the pleural cavity. Written from Guyton & Hall 14e, chapter 39 (no deck for this week).

Labobo topic: Physio Lec 12: Pulmonary Circulation, Pulmonary Edema and Pleural Fluid

In 60 seconds

  • Two circulations: bronchial (high pressure, low flow, 1-2% of cardiac output) and pulmonary (low pressure, high flow, equals the whole cardiac output).
  • Pulmonary artery pressure: 25/8, mean 15 mm Hg. Capillary mean 7. Left atrium about 2. Right ventricle 25/0-1.
  • Low alveolar O₂ makes local vessels constrict (the opposite of systemic vessels). This sends blood to well-ventilated alveoli.
  • Standing: flow is least at the apex (zone 2) and greatest at the base (zone 3). Zone 1 (no flow) is abnormal.
  • Net filtration pressure across the capillary is only +1 mm Hg; lymphatics and a negative interstitial pressure keep the alveoli dry.
  • Edema needs capillary pressure above plasma colloid osmotic pressure (28 mm Hg): safety factor 21 mm Hg.
  • Pleural fluid pressure is about -7 mm Hg; lymphatics keep the pleural space nearly empty.

Anatomy of the pulmonary circulation

FeatureBronchial circulationPulmonary circulation
Pressure and flowHigh pressure, low flowLow pressure, high flow
BloodSystemic arterial (oxygenated), from bronchial arteries off the thoracic aorta; pressure only slightly below aorticVenous blood from the whole body, from the right ventricle via the pulmonary artery
SuppliesTrachea, bronchial tree to the terminal bronchioles, supporting tissue, septa, outer coats of pulmonary vesselsThe alveolar capillaries, for gas exchange
Share of output1-2% of cardiac outputAll of cardiac output
Drains toPulmonary veins, then the left atriumPulmonary veins, then the left atrium
  • Because bronchial blood joins the pulmonary veins, left ventricular output is 1-2% greater than right ventricular output.
  • The pulmonary artery runs only 5 cm beyond the apex of the right ventricle before it divides into right and left branches. Its wall is one-third as thick as the aorta.
  • Pulmonary arteries are short, wide and distensible, so their compliance is large: almost 7 ml/mm Hg, similar to the whole systemic arterial tree. This lets them accept the right ventricular stroke volume.
  • Pulmonary veins are also short and empty straight into the left atrium.
  • Lymphatics start in the connective tissue around the terminal bronchioles, run to the hilum and mostly into the right thoracic lymph duct. They remove particles and leaked plasma protein, which helps prevent edema.

Pressures in the pulmonary system

SitePressure (mm Hg)
Right ventricleSystolic about 25; diastolic about 0-1 (one-fifth of the left ventricle)
Pulmonary arterySystolic about 25; diastolic about 8; mean 15
Pulmonary capillaryMean about 7
Left atrium and pulmonary veinsMean about 2 lying down (range 1 to 5)
Wedge pressureAbout 5; usually 2-3 above left atrial pressure
  • In systole the pulmonary artery pressure equals the right ventricular pressure. After the pulmonary valve closes, ventricular pressure falls sharply but arterial pressure falls slowly.
  • Left atrial pressure is hard to measure directly. A catheter is passed through a peripheral vein, the right heart and the pulmonary artery until it wedges in a small branch. Flow there stops, so the pressure reflects the capillaries and left atrium.
  • Wedge pressure rises when left atrial pressure rises, so it is used to follow congestive heart failure.

Blood volume of the lungs

  • About 450 ml, about 9% of total blood volume. About 70 ml is in the capillaries; the rest is split about equally between arteries and veins.
  • The lungs are a blood reservoir: volume can vary from half-normal to twice normal. Blowing hard (a trumpet) can push up to 250 ml into the systemic circulation. After a hemorrhage, blood shifts from lungs to systemic vessels.
  • In left heart failure or mitral stenosis or regurgitation, blood dams up in the lungs and pulmonary blood volume can rise by as much as 100%.
  • The systemic circulation is about nine times larger than the pulmonary, so the same shift greatly affects the lungs but only mildly affects the systemic circulation.

Blood flow and its distribution

  • Lung blood flow is essentially equal to cardiac output, so it is controlled by the same mainly peripheral factors. The vessels behave as distensible tubes: they widen as pressure rises and narrow as it falls.
  • Blood must go to the best-ventilated alveoli. The control is hypoxic vasoconstriction.
  1. Alveolar O₂ falls below normal, especially below 70% of normal (under 73 mm Hg).
  2. Nearby arterioles constrict; resistance can rise more than fivefold at very low O₂.
  3. Blood is diverted to better-aerated alveoli, matching flow to ventilation.
  • This is opposite to systemic vessels, which dilate in low O₂.
  • Mechanisms are not fully understood. Proposed: more vasoconstrictor (endothelin, reactive oxygen species) or less vasodilator (nitric oxide); or low O₂ blocks O₂-sensitive K⁺ channels in smooth muscle, which depolarises the cell, opens Ca²⁺ channels and lets Ca²⁺ in, causing constriction.

Hydrostatic gradients and zones 1-3

  • In an upright adult the lung base is about 30 cm below the apex: a 23 mm Hg difference, about 15 above the heart and 8 below.
  • At rest standing, flow at the base is about five times that at the top.
  • Alveolar capillaries are distended by blood pressure inside and compressed by alveolar air pressure outside. If alveolar pressure exceeds capillary pressure, they close.
ZoneFlowWhyWhere
Zone 1None at any timeCapillary pressure never exceeds alveolar pressureAbnormal only
Zone 2Intermittent, in systoleSystolic pressure above alveolar, diastolic belowApices, from about 10 cm above heart level
Zone 3ContinuousPressure above alveolar throughout the cycleAll lower lung; the whole lung lying down
  • At the apex standing: systolic 25 - 15 = 10 mm Hg (above alveolar 0) so systolic flow; diastolic 8 mm Hg cannot beat the 15 mm Hg gradient so no diastolic flow.
  • Zone 1 occurs when alveolar pressure is high (breathing against a positive pressure at least 10 mm Hg above normal) or pulmonary systolic pressure is very low (severe blood loss).
  • Exercise raises flow everywhere, partly because rising pressures turn the apices from zone 2 into zone 3.

Exercise and left heart failure

Heavy exercise

  • Lung flow may rise four- to sevenfold. Three adaptations: (1) up to threefold more open capillaries; (2) distended capillaries with more than twofold faster flow in each; (3) a rise in arterial pressure.
  • The first two lower resistance so much that arterial pressure barely rises. This saves right heart energy and prevents a rise in capillary pressure and edema.

Left heart failure

  • In health, left atrial pressure almost never exceeds +6 mm Hg, even in hard exercise, with no effect on circulation.
  • In failure it can climb from 1-5 to 40-50 mm Hg. Up to about 7 mm Hg little happens.
  • Above 7-8 mm Hg, each rise in left atrial pressure raises pulmonary arterial pressure almost as much, loading the right heart. Capillary pressure rises equally.
  • Above 30 mm Hg, pulmonary edema is likely.

Pulmonary capillary dynamics

  • Capillaries almost touch side by side in the alveolar walls, so blood flows as a "sheet of flow".
  • Transit time at normal cardiac output is about 0.8 s; it can shorten to 0.3 s when output rises (much less because extra collapsed capillaries open).

  • This slight outward leak is pumped back by lymphatics, apart from a little that evaporates in the alveoli.

Pulmonary edema

It forms like edema elsewhere: more filtration, or poor lymph drainage, so that interstitial pressure moves from negative to positive and fluid fills the interstitium and alveoli.

Common causeMechanism
Left heart failure or mitral valve diseaseLarge rise in pulmonary venous and capillary pressure floods interstitium and alveoli
Damaged capillary membranesInfections such as pneumonia, or noxious gases (chlorine, sulfur dioxide); rapid leak of plasma protein and fluid

Safety factor

  • Edema becomes significant only when capillary pressure reaches the plasma colloid osmotic pressure. In dogs, fluid began to collect when left atrial pressure passed 23 mm Hg (capillary pressure above 25), which matched the dogs' plasma colloid osmotic pressure of 25.
  • In a person, capillary pressure must rise from 7 to above 28 mm Hg: an acute safety factor of 21 mm Hg.
  • Chronic (at least 2 weeks): lymph vessels expand and can carry away up to about 10-fold more fluid. In chronic mitral stenosis, capillary pressures of 40-45 mm Hg have been measured without lethal edema.
  • Just above the safety level, lethal edema can occur in hours; with a rise of 25-30 mm Hg above it, in 20-30 minutes. In acute left heart failure with capillary pressure near 50 mm Hg, death may follow in under 30 minutes.
Watch out

The chapter gives two "edema" levels. Capillary pressure must pass the plasma colloid osmotic pressure, 28 mm Hg, for substantial edema (the safety factor is 28 - 7 = 21). Separately, the text says edema is likely once left atrial pressure passes 30 mm Hg. They are compatible: capillary pressure runs 1-2 mm Hg above left atrial pressure. In the dog experiment the threshold was left atrial 23 (capillary above 25), because the dogs' plasma colloid pressure was 25, not 28.

Fluid in the pleural cavity

  • A thin layer of mucoid fluid lies between the parietal and visceral pleurae so the lungs slide easily. The pleura is a porous serous membrane; interstitial fluid and protein transude into the space, which makes the fluid mucoid.
  • Each pleural cavity holds only a few ml. Excess is pumped away by lymphatics opening directly into the mediastinum, the superior surface of the diaphragm and the lateral parietal pleura. The pleural space is a potential space.
  • The lungs need a negative force outside them to stay expanded. It comes from the lymphatic pumping of fluid out of the space, as in most tissue spaces.
  • The lung collapse tendency is about -4 mm Hg, so pleural pressure must be at least that negative. It is usually about -7 mm Hg.

Pleural effusion

Large amounts of free fluid in the pleural space: edema of the pleural cavity. Causes:

  1. Blocked lymphatic drainage from the pleural cavity.
  2. Cardiac failure: high peripheral and pulmonary capillary pressures push too much fluid into the cavity.
  3. Greatly reduced plasma colloid osmotic pressure.
  4. Infection or inflammation of the pleural surfaces: higher capillary permeability dumps protein and fluid into the cavity.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • Pulmonary hypoxia constricts vessels; do not apply the systemic rule.
  • Normal upright lungs have zones 2 and 3 only; zone 1 is abnormal.
  • Lying down, the whole lung is zone 3, including the apices.
  • Heavy exercise barely raises pulmonary arterial pressure because resistance falls (more open and distended capillaries).
  • The pulmonary capillary pressure (7) is lower than peripheral (17), and the interstitial pressure is more negative.
  • The net filtration pressure is +1, outward, not zero.
  • The safety factor is 28 - 7 = 21, not 28.
  • Bronchial venous blood drains to the left atrium, so left output is slightly larger.

    2. 100 ml
    3. 250 ml
    4. 450 ml

    C. As much as 250 ml is pushed into the systemic circulation.

  5. Which ion channel change is proposed to explain hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction?

    1. Opening of voltage-gated Na⁺ channels
    2. Blockade of O₂-sensitive K⁺ channels, leading to depolarisation and Ca²⁺ entry
    3. Opening of Cl⁻ channels
    4. Closing of Ca²⁺ channels
    Show answer

    B. Blocked K⁺ channels depolarise the smooth muscle cell, Ca²⁺ channels open and the vessel constricts.

  6. At the apex of an upright lung the systolic arterial pressure is about 10 mm Hg. Why is flow there only intermittent?

    1. Diastolic pressure there cannot overcome the hydrostatic gradient of about 15 mm Hg
    2. Alveolar pressure is far above 10 mm Hg
    3. The apical capillaries are absent
    4. Hypoxia closes them in diastole
    Show answer

    A. Systole (10 mm Hg) exceeds alveolar pressure (0); diastole (8 mm Hg) cannot beat the 15 mm Hg gradient.

  7. During heavy exercise the number of open pulmonary capillaries can rise by up to:

    1. Fifty percent
    2. Twofold
    3. Threefold
    4. Tenfold
    Show answer

    C. Up to threefold; each capillary also flows more than twice as fast.

  8. Which is the correct sum of the forces tending to move fluid out of a pulmonary capillary?

    1. 7 + 28 = 35 mm Hg
    2. 7 + 14 + 8 = 29 mm Hg
    3. 14 + 28 = 42 mm Hg
    4. 7 + 14 = 21 mm Hg
    Show answer

    B. Capillary pressure 7, interstitial colloid osmotic pressure 14 and negative interstitial pressure 8 give 29 mm Hg.

  9. After a pulmonary capillary pressure rise has lasted at least 2 weeks, why is edema less likely?

    1. Plasma proteins increase
    2. The alveolar epithelium thickens
    3. Capillary pressure falls back to 7
    4. Lymph vessels expand and carry away up to about 10 times more fluid
    Show answer

    D. This is the chronic safety factor, seen in chronic mitral stenosis.

  10. The pleural lymphatics open directly into all of the following EXCEPT:

    1. The mediastinum
    2. The superior surface of the diaphragm
    3. The lateral surfaces of the parietal pleura
    4. The pulmonary capillaries
    Show answer

    D. Lymphatics open into the mediastinum, the upper surface of the diaphragm and the lateral parietal pleura.

  11. Which change would favour a pleural effusion?

    1. Raised plasma colloid osmotic pressure
    2. Blocked lymphatic drainage from the pleural cavity
    3. Lower pulmonary capillary pressure
    4. More negative pleural pressure from stronger lymph pumping
    Show answer

    B. Blocked pleural lymph drainage is one of the four causes, with cardiac failure, low plasma colloid pressure and inflammation.

Key terms

Bronchial arteries: systemic supply of the lung's own tissue · Wedge pressure: pressure measured with a catheter wedged in a small pulmonary artery; estimates left atrial pressure · Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction: local vessel narrowing in low alveolar O₂ · Zone 1, 2, 3: no, intermittent and continuous capillary flow · Sheet of flow: blood spread through the alveolar wall capillaries · Mean filtration pressure: net outward force at the capillary (+1 mm Hg) · Safety factor: rise in capillary pressure needed before edema (21 mm Hg acute) · Pulmonary edema: fluid in the lung interstitium and alveoli · Pleural space: potential space with a few ml of mucoid fluid · Pleural effusion: large amount of free fluid in the pleural space · Mitral stenosis: narrowed mitral valve that dams blood in the lungs.
