Passive transport (diffusion by Fick's law, permeability, osmosis and the van 't Hoff law, aquaporins, the Donnan effect, facilitated diffusion and gated ion channels including the K⁺ selectivity filter) and active transport (chemical potential, P-type ATPases such as Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, and secondary co-transport).

In 60 seconds

  • Fick's law: J = −DA·dc/dx. Einstein: D = kT/(6πrη). Membrane permeability P = Dβ/Δx (β = partition coefficient), so J = −PA(Co − Ci).
  • Aquaporins (Agre, Nobel 2003): tetramers; each subunit ~270 aa, six helices, NPA motifs; pass water but not protons. AQP2 is ADH-regulated (diabetes insipidus, SIADH).

Types of transport

The membrane is a selective barrier keeping inside and outside different. Passive transport moves molecules down their gradient until equilibrium; active transport moves them against a gradient and needs cellular energy.

  1. Diffusion down a concentration gradient (O₂, N₂, ethanol, urea, small uncharged molecules).
  2. Diffusion of charged or hydrophilic particles down an electrochemical gradient.
  3. Osmosis: water moves because of a gradient of a non-permeating solute.
  4. Facilitated diffusion.
  5. Active transport against a gradient.

Endocytosis and exocytosis move bulk material.

Diffusion and permeability

Fick's law: J = −D·A·dc/dx ≈ −D·A·(Co − Ci)/(xo − xi). The net rate J is proportional to the concentration difference and area and inversely proportional to distance.

Einstein's formula (valid for small uncharged molecules): D = kT/(6πrη), so D rises with temperature and falls with molecular radius r and viscosity η.

Treating the membrane as a third compartment: J = −D·A·β·ΔC/Δx = −P·A·ΔC, with permeability P = Dβ/Δx, where β is the oil–water partition coefficient and Δx the membrane thickness. Lipid-soluble molecules (high β) cross easily.

Aquaporins

Discovered by Peter Agre (Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2003, shared with Roderick MacKinnon for K⁺ channels). Aquaporins are water channels controlling cell water content, found in kidney, red cells and elsewhere; more than 10 types exist.

  • Each subunit: one peptide of about 270 amino acids, six membrane-spanning domains, N- and C-termini inside. Loops B and D carry asparagine–proline–alanine (NPA) motifs that line the water path.
  • They assemble as tetramers.
  • They are impermeable to charged species, even protons, preserving the membrane's electrochemical potential.
In the clinic: AQP2 and ADH

Aquaporin-2 in the collecting duct is regulated by ADH (vasopressin). Too little ADH or action → diabetes insipidus (polyuria, thirst). Too much → SIADH (water retention, hyponatraemia).

Osmosis and the Donnan effect

Osmotic pressure depends on the number of particles (a colligative property). Van 't Hoff's law: π = i·R·T·c, corrected by the osmotic coefficient φ: π = φ·i·R·T·c.

For 0.154 M NaCl at 37 °C (i = 2, φ = 0.93, R = 0.082 L·atm/(K·mol), T = 310 K): osmolarity φ·i·c ≈ 0.286 Osm/L, π ≈ 7.28 atm.

Gibbs–Donnan effect: when one ion (e.g. intracellular protein anions) cannot cross the membrane, the permeant ions redistribute unequally across it.

Facilitated diffusion and ion channels

Facilitated diffusion uses a carrier, still moving down the gradient without energy. The glucose transporter (permease) is a multipass protein with 12 α-helical segments. Plasma glucose (~5 mM) exceeds red-cell glucose, and uptake is faster than Fick's law predicts but saturates, following Michaelis–Menten kinetics (Km measures affinity).

The K⁺ selectivity filter

Four subunits surround a central pore. The filter has the conserved sequence TVGYG (Thr-Val-Gly-Tyr-Gly); its carbonyl oxygens replace the water shell of K⁺ exactly, which they cannot do for the smaller Na⁺. There are four K⁺ binding sites; ions hop between sites and are pushed through by electrostatic repulsion from incoming ions.

Active transport

Active transport always uses energy, because it moves solutes against their gradient and hydrophilic molecules face an energy barrier in the hydrophobic core. Cells need it to concentrate substances and to move certain molecules across the bilayer.

Thermodynamics

Three kinds of potential energy are involved: concentration gradients, chemical reactions (ATP hydrolysis) and charge moving in an electric field. The chemical potential is Gibbs energy per mole: μ = μ° + RT ln C (J/mol). ATP hydrolysis at about 4 mM releases about 69 kJ/mol in the cell.

Primary active transport

ATP hydrolysis by an ATPase changes the protein's conformation, pushing ions across. P-type ATPases are phosphorylated on a conserved aspartate.

  • Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase (isolated by Jens Skou, 1957): binds ATP and 3 Na⁺ inside; phosphorylation flips it to lower Na⁺ and raise K⁺ affinity; releases Na⁺ outside, binds 2 K⁺, dephosphorylates and returns. Inhibited by ouabain (outer side). Na⁺ is 10–20× higher outside; K⁺ 20–40× higher inside.
  • Ca²⁺-ATPase: removes Ca²⁺ after contraction (relaxation), and in nerve, kidney and gut.
  • H⁺/K⁺-ATPase: gastric proton pump, acidifies the stomach and activates pepsin.
  • H⁺-ATPase in plants and fungi; flippase moves phosphatidylserine to the inner leaflet; CPx-ATPases move heavy metals (Cu⁺, Zn²⁺, Cd²⁺, Ag⁺).

Secondary active transport

Uses the energy stored in an ion gradient made by a primary pump, so it is always co-transport: symport (same direction) or antiport (opposite).

  • Na⁺–glucose symport (and amino acids) on the apical membrane of intestinal cells, driven by the Na⁺ gradient from Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase; glucose then leaves basolaterally by facilitated diffusion.
  • Cl⁻/HCO₃⁻ exchanger (band 3, 12-pass) in red cells: CO₂ entering is converted by carbonic anhydrase to HCO₃⁻, which is exchanged for Cl⁻ and carried to the lungs; the exchanger raises membrane HCO₃⁻ permeability over a million-fold.
