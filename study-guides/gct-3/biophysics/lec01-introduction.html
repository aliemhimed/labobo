Introduction to Biophysics

What biophysics is, the milestones in its history, its main branches by size of subject, and the techniques it uses to separate, measure and image biological molecules and cells.

Labobo topic: Biophys Lec 1: Introduction to Biophysics

In 60 seconds

  • Biophysics is interdisciplinary: physics, biology, chemistry and mathematics. Its goal: simplified models, quantitative predictions, experimental tests.
  • History: 1892 Karl Pearson coins "biophysics"; 1943 Schrödinger, What is Life; 1946 King's College hires physicists (Wilkins, Franklin, X-ray diffraction of DNA); 1953 Watson and Crick; 1957 Biophysical Society.
  • Branches by size: molecular and subcellular, physiological and anatomical, environmental.
  • Separation: ultracentrifugation and size-exclusion chromatography (size), electrophoresis (charge/mass).
  • Structure: X-ray crystallography, NMR, mass spectrometry, spectroscopy, electron and atomic force microscopy.
  • Cells and forces: optical tweezers, voltage/current/patch clamp, calorimetry.

What biophysics is and its history

Biophysics applies the methods of physics, chemistry and mathematics to biological systems. Its aims are to create simplified models of biological systems, make quantitative predictions and test them experimentally.

YearMilestone
1892Karl Pearson introduces the term "biophysics"
1943Erwin Schrödinger (Nobel Prize 1933) writes What is Life
1946King's College London hires physicists for biological problems: Maurice Wilkins and Rosalind Franklin use X-ray diffraction of DNA
1953Crick (particle physicist) and Watson (biologist) describe the DNA double helix
1957The Biophysical Society is founded

Branches of biophysics

Topics are grouped by the relative size of the subject:

BranchTopics
Molecular and subcellularStructure and conformation of biomolecules, structure–function, conformational transitions, ligand binding, diffusion and transport, membrane biophysics, DNA and protein biophysics, bioenergetics (thermodynamics, statistical mechanics, kinetics), molecular machines, allostery
Physiological and anatomicalBiomechanics (animal, system and cell mechanics), electrophysiology (electrical properties of tissues: muscle contraction, nerve impulses), sensory biophysics (vision, hearing, taste, balance)
EnvironmentalInteractions between organisms and their physical environment, especially energy and mass exchange

General techniques, imaging biophysics and medical biophysics apply across all branches.

Biophysical techniques

TechniquePrinciple / what it gives
UltracentrifugationSeparates molecules by size
ElectrophoresisSeparates by charge-to-mass ratio
Size-exclusion chromatographySeparates by size through a densely packed gel
Absorption spectroscopyConcentration from absorption of EM radiation (visible, IR, UV)
Fluorescence spectroscopyAbsorbs one wavelength and emits a longer one; characterises molecules and conformational change
Mass spectrometryMass of charged particles moving in a magnetic field
X-ray crystallographyPositions of atoms in a crystal from X-ray diffraction and scattering
NMR spectroscopyRadio-frequency EM interacting with nuclear spins in a strong magnetic field; structural organisation
Electron microscopyA beam of electrons instead of light; far higher resolution
Atomic force microscopyA mechanical probe scans the surface; 3D images at molecular resolution
Optical tweezersLaser light holds and moves single molecules and measures molecular forces
Voltage, current and patch clampFine electrodes measure voltage and current across cell membranes
CalorimetryHeat capacity versus temperature; thermal properties of macromolecules
Watch out

Electrophoresis separates by charge/mass, while ultracentrifugation and size-exclusion separate by size. In fluorescence, the emitted light always has a longer wavelength (lower energy) than the absorbed light.

Key terms

Biophysics: physics applied to biology · Biomechanics: mechanics of organisms and cells · Electrophysiology: electrical properties of tissues · Bioenergetics: energy flow in living systems · Spectroscopy: interaction of matter with EM radiation · Fluorescence: emission at a longer wavelength · Crystallography: atomic structure from diffraction · NMR: nuclear magnetic resonance · AFM: atomic force microscopy · Patch clamp: electrode recording of membrane current · Calorimetry: measurement of heat capacity.
