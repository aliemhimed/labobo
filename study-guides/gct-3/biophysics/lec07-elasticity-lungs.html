Elastic Properties of Biomaterials and Lung Biomechanics

Mechanical properties of muscle and the Hill model, the cytoskeleton and structural proteins (intermediate filaments, collagen, elastin, resilin), stress, strain, Hooke's law and Young's modulus, bone fracture, levers in the body, and the physics of breathing: Boyle's law, pleural pressures, Poiseuille resistance, compliance, Laplace's law and surfactant, and lung volumes.

Labobo topic: Biophys Lec 7: Elastic Properties and Lung Biomechanics

In 60 seconds

  • Muscle: irritability, contractility, extensibility, elasticity. Hill model: contractile component + parallel elastic (membrane, fascia) + series elastic (tendon).
  • Intermediate filaments (keratin, vimentin, neurofilaments, lamins) give strength and have no ATP site. Collagen = triple helix, tensile strength; elastin = cross-linked, recoils.
  • Stress = F/A; strain = ΔL/L; Hooke's law stress = Y × strain (elastic region). Elastic = recoverable; plastic = permanent; brittle = fracture.
  • Max muscle force ≈ 70 N/cm² of cross-section. Lever: load × load arm = effort × effort arm. Most body levers are class 3 (MA < 1).
  • Breathing: Boyle's law P₁V₁ = P₂V₂. Intrapleural pressure ≈ −4 mmHg; pneumothorax collapses the lung. Resistance R = 8ηl/(πr⁴) (r⁴!). Compliance C = ΔV/ΔP (low in fibrosis, high in emphysema).
  • Laplace: P = 2γ/r; surfactant (type II cells, DPPC + SP-A to SP-D) lowers γ; its lack causes neonatal RDS. TLC ≈ 5900 mL; VC ≈ 4700 mL; TV ≈ 500 mL.

Mechanical properties of muscle

  • Irritability: responds to a stimulus.
  • Contractility: shortens or develops tension.
  • Extensibility: stretches under force.
  • Elasticity: recoils to resting length when the force is removed.

Muscle is anisotropic, nearly incompressible, non-linearly elastic and viscoelastic. The Hill model (Hill 1922 added speed and viscosity; Levin and Wyman 1927 added elasticity; Hill and Shorten 1938) has three parts:

ComponentRepresents
Contractile component (CC)Muscle fibres (actin–myosin), with damping
Parallel elastic component (PEC)Membrane and fascia; matters at large stretch
Series elastic component (SEC)Tendon

Muscle structure (endomysium, perimysium, epimysium; sarcomere bands) is as in Lecture 6.

Cytoskeleton and structural proteins

The cytoskeleton is a dynamic network of actin filaments, microtubules and intermediate filaments, plus accessory proteins, responsible for cell shape, movement, organelle transport and contraction. Actin–myosin microfilaments have tensile strength about 2.2 MPa.

Intermediate filaments

Strong, mechanically and chemically stable, with no ATP-binding site (unlike actin). Each has N- and C-terminal heads and an α-helical rod; two form a coiled-coil dimer, dimers form tetramers, and eight tetramers form a rope-like filament. Types: keratin (epithelia), vimentin (connective tissue, muscle, glia), neurofilaments (neurons), nuclear lamins (nuclear envelope). They let epithelial sheets withstand stretching, a principle copied in fibreglass and steel-reinforced concrete.

Extracellular proteins

  • Collagen: a long, stiff triple helix made by fibroblasts as procollagen; cross-linked into fibrils with tensile strength comparable to steel. About 20 genes; about 25% of total protein in mammals (bone, tendon, skin).
  • Elastin: cross-linked chains that uncoil when stretched and recoil spontaneously, like rubber.
  • Resilin (arthropods): 50–60% water, stretches to about three times its length, Young's modulus about 1.8 MPa, shear modulus 0.6 MPa. Abductin (scallops) opens the shell; its modulus is similar to elastin.

Stress, strain and Young's modulus

  • Stress = force per unit area (F/A). Types: tensile (stretching), compressive (squeezing), shear (sliding displacement).
  • Strain = the resulting deformation, ΔL/L (dimensionless).
  • Hooke's law: in the elastic range, stress = Y × strain, where Y is Young's modulus (stiffness, units of pressure). For a spring F = kx, stored energy = ½kx².
DeformationFeatures
ElasticSmall stress; fully recoverable (like a rubber band); stress applied briefly
Plastic (ductile)Permanent; via movement along small structural defects; stress applied for long periods
BrittleFracture; stress applied rapidly

Units: 1 N = 10⁵ dyn; 1 m² = 10⁴ cm².

Bone, muscle force and levers

Bone fracture can be analysed by energy: a bone (e.g. 60 cm long, Young's modulus 14 × 10⁵ N/cm², breaking stress 10⁴ N/cm²) breaks when the impact energy exceeds the elastic energy it can store before reaching breaking stress, as when a 70 kg person lands stiff-legged from a jump.

"It is the function of muscles to pull and not to push" (Leonardo da Vinci). The maximum force of a muscle is proportional to its cross-section, about 70 N/cm².

Lever law: FL × LR = FE × LE (load × load arm = effort × effort arm); mechanical advantage M = load/effort. At equilibrium the load, effort and fulcrum forces sum to zero.

Mechanics of breathing

Gas exchange depends on pressure gradients, diffusion and mechanical forces. The airways branch dichotomously over 22 generations (trachea n = 0, main bronchi n = 1; 2ⁿ airways per generation). Generations 0–15 are cartilage-supported conducting airways; 17–19 are respiratory bronchioles; 20–22 are alveolar ducts. Alveoli are about 200 µm across.

  • Inspiration is active: the diaphragm contracts and descends, external intercostals swing the ribs out, increasing thoracic diameters.
  • Quiet expiration is passive: driven by elastic recoil (collagen and elastin) and alveolar surface tension. Forced expiration uses internal intercostals and abdominal muscles.
  • Rest: about 15 breaths/min (4 s cycle), inspiration : expiration ≈ 1 : 2.

Boyle's law: at constant temperature P₁V₁ = P₂V₂. Increasing thoracic volume lowers alveolar pressure (about −1 cmH₂O) so air flows in until Palv = Patm; recoil raises Palv above Patm and air flows out.

Pressures

  • Intrapleural pressure is always below atmospheric and alveolar pressure, about −4 mmHg: the outward pull of the chest wall slightly exceeds the inward recoil of the lung.
  • Transpulmonary pressure = Palv − Pip, always positive in health, keeping the lungs open.
In the clinic: pneumothorax

A chest-wall wound or lung tear lets air into the pleural space; intrapleural pressure rises to atmospheric, the negative pressure is lost and the lung collapses under its own elastic recoil.

Resistance, compliance and surfactant

Airflow Q = ΔP/R. By Poiseuille's law, R = 8ηl/(πr⁴): halving the radius raises resistance 16-fold. Poiseuille assumes laminar flow, true in small airways; flow becomes turbulent in large airways.

Compliance C = ΔV/ΔP (inverse of elastance, stiffness).

  • Low compliance (pulmonary fibrosis, restrictive disease): stiff lung, hard to inflate but recoils strongly.
  • High compliance (emphysema, COPD): alveolar walls and elastic tissue destroyed; lung stretches easily but fails to recoil, so exhaling is hard.

The pressure–volume curve shows hysteresis because of the air–water interface.

Laplace's law and surfactant

P = 2γ/r: with equal surface tension γ, small alveoli would have higher pressure and empty into larger ones and collapse. Surfactant, made by type II pneumocytes, is mainly dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine (DPPC) with proteins SP-A, SP-B, SP-C and SP-D. It:

  • lowers surface tension and increases compliance;
  • reduces the work of breathing;
  • stabilises alveoli so small ones do not collapse;
  • draws fluid out, keeping alveoli dry.
In the clinic: neonatal respiratory distress syndrome

Premature babies lacking surfactant develop atelectasis; compliance falls sharply and oxygenation becomes dangerously low.

Lung volumes

Volume / capacityDefinitionTypical value
Tidal volume (TV)Air per quiet breath~500 mL
Inspiratory reserve (IRV)Extra air inhaled beyond TV—
Expiratory reserve (ERV)Extra air exhaled beyond TVup to ~1200 mL (men)
Residual volume (RV)Air left after maximal expiration; keeps alveoli open—
Inspiratory capacity (IC)TV + IRV~3500 mL
Functional residual capacity (FRC)ERV + RV~2400 mL (men)
Vital capacity (VC)TV + IRV + ERV~4700 mL
Total lung capacity (TLC)All four volumes~5900 mL

Values vary with age, sex, body size and fitness. RV, FRC and TLC cannot be measured by simple spirometry because they include RV.

Watch out: slide wording

One slide calls FRC "ERV + tidal volume"; FRC is ERV + RV (the air left after a normal breath out), which is why it cannot be measured by spirometry. Another slide defines transpulmonary pressure as intrapleural minus alveolar; it is alveolar minus intrapleural, which is positive.

Decompression sickness (caisson or divers' disease) follows from gas laws: gas dissolved at high pressure comes out of solution as bubbles if pressure falls too quickly.
