Membrane Potential and Muscle Contraction

The membrane as a capacitor, the resting potential and the Nernst and Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz equations, driving force, the phases of the action potential and its conduction, then the three muscle types, the sarcomere, actin, myosin and accessory proteins, the cross-bridge cycle and Ca²⁺ control in skeletal, cardiac and smooth muscle.

In 60 seconds

  • The bilayer is a poor conductor with charge on both faces: a capacitor, field ~200 000 V/cm. Neurons and cardiac cells rest at about −70 mV, non-excitable cells −60 to −80 mV, photoreceptors about −20 mV.
  • Nernst: Veq = (RT/zF) ln([X]out/[X]in). GHK combines K⁺, Na⁺ and Cl⁻ weighted by permeability (pK : pNa : pCl = 1 : 0.05 : 0.45 at rest).

  • Action potential: all-or-nothing; Na⁺ channels open (overshoot), then inactivate as K⁺ channels open (repolarisation, hyperpolarisation). Bulk concentrations do not change. Myelin (Schwann cells, oligodendrocytes) gives conduction at nodes of Ranvier.
  • Sarcomere: Z–Z; A band (myosin + overlap), H band (myosin only), I band (actin only), M line. Sliding filament model (1954).

Resting membrane potential

Electrostatics underlies membrane potentials (Coulomb's law, electric field, work moving a charge). The hydrophobic bilayer is a poor conductor with charges on both sides, so it acts as a capacitor; the voltage gradient is about 200 000 V/cm. Cells are slightly negative inside.

The potential arises from unequal ion concentrations (Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻, anions A⁻) plus selective ion channels, and is measured with fine glass microelectrodes filled with 3 M KCl.

CellTypical Vm
Neuron, cardiac muscleabout −70 mV
Non-excitable cells−60 to −80 mV
Photoreceptorsabout −20 mV

In non-excitable cells Vm stays at rest; excitable cells (neurons, muscle) fire rapid changes. Terms: depolarisation (less negative), hyperpolarisation (more negative).

Nernst, GHK and driving force

If a membrane is permeable to K⁺ only (e.g. 150 mM inside, 5 mM outside), K⁺ diffuses out until the electrical pull balances the concentration gradient: the equilibrium (Nernst) potential.

Veq = (RT / zF) · ln([X]out / [X]in), with R = 8.314 J/(K·mol), F = 96 485 C/mol, z = valence.

Real cells are permeable to several ions, so the Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz equation weights each by permeability:

Vm = (RT/F) · ln[(pK[K⁺]o + pNa[Na⁺]o + pCl[Cl⁻]i) / (pK[K⁺]i + pNa[Na⁺]i + pCl[Cl⁻]o)]

At rest pK : pNa : pCl ≈ 1 : 0.05 : 0.45, so Vm lies close to EK. Raising the permeability to an ion moves Vm toward that ion's Nernst potential.

Driving force: VDF = Vm − Veq. Its sign gives the direction of ion flow. In a resting neuron K⁺ flows out, Na⁺ and Cl⁻ flow in; the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase restores the gradients, and the kidneys keep extracellular Na⁺ and K⁺ constant. The Na⁺ gradient also powers secondary active transport (e.g. Na⁺–glucose).

Watch out

For Cl⁻ (z = −1) the inside and outside concentrations swap places in the GHK equation. Also, Na⁺ and K⁺ concentrations do not measurably change during one action potential; only tiny amounts of ions cross.

The action potential

An action potential is all-or-nothing: once threshold is reached it fires at full size.

  1. Rest.
  2. Depolarisation to threshold: voltage-gated Na⁺ channels activate.
  3. Overshoot: more Na⁺ channels open, Na⁺ permeability soars and Vm becomes positive (positive feedback, the Hodgkin cycle).
  4. Peak: Na⁺ channels inactivate; K⁺ channels activate.
  5. Repolarisation: K⁺ permeability high, Na⁺ low.
  6. Hyperpolarisation (undershoot) until K⁺ channels close.

The voltage-gated Na⁺ channel has three states: closed (resting), open, and inactivated.

Conduction

Squids speed conduction with very wide axons (lower resistance). Vertebrates use myelin: Na⁺ and K⁺ channels cluster at the nodes of Ranvier, so impulses jump node to node. Myelin is made by Schwann cells (one axon each, peripheral) and oligodendrocytes (up to about 40 axons, CNS).

Skeletal muscle organisation: each fibre is wrapped in endomysium (with capillaries), fibres group into fascicles wrapped in perimysium, and the whole muscle is wrapped in epimysium; these connective tissues contain collagen and elastin.

Actin (thin filaments)

Six isoforms (α-skeletal, α-cardiac, α-vascular, γ-enteric, β- and γ-cytoplasmic), about 80% identical. Globular G-actin (~43 kDa, with an ATP site) polymerises into F-actin, about 7–8 nm wide, with a helical repeat of about 37 nm (14 monomers). Monomers add at the plus end (critical concentration ~1 µM) and leave the minus end (~8 µM): treadmilling between those concentrations.

  • Tropomyosin: rigid coiled-coil dimer (~41 nm) lying along 7 actin monomers.
  • Troponin: TnT binds tropomyosin, TnI inhibits actin–myosin interaction, TnC binds Ca²⁺.
  • Tropomodulin caps and regulates filament length.

Myosin II (thick filaments)

Two heavy chains (~200 kDa) forming a coiled-coil tail (heptad repeat with hydrophobic residues at positions a and d) and two globular motor heads, each with two light chains (~18–20 kDa). Tails pack into the thick filament with heads projecting.

Accessory proteins

  • M line: myomesin and C-protein cross-link thick filaments and titin; creatine kinase regenerates ATP from phosphocreatine.
  • Z disc: CapZ caps actin; α-actinin cross-links actin.
  • Titin (~3.5 MDa) links myosin to the Z disc and acts as a spring; nebulin sets the number of actin monomers in thin filaments.

Cross-bridge cycle and Ca²⁺ control

The sliding filament model (1954): filaments slide past each other without shortening, so the I and H bands narrow while the A band stays the same. Energy is required.

  1. ATP hydrolysis cocks the myosin head, which contacts actin.
  2. Release of Pi and a conformational change produce the power stroke (about 10 nm).
  3. ADP is released; the head stays bound until a new ATP binds and detaches it. With no ATP, heads stay attached: rigor mortis.

Regulation by Ca²⁺

In relaxed muscle, tropomyosin and TnI block myosin-binding sites on actin. When Ca²⁺ binds TnC, the troponin–tropomyosin complex shifts and myosin heads can bind.

Excitation–contraction coupling

  • The action potential travels down T-tubules.
  • The voltage-sensing dihydropyridine receptor (DHPR) signals the SR Ca²⁺ release channel, the ryanodine receptor.
  • Ca²⁺ is pumped back into the SR (1–5% of muscle volume) by Ca²⁺-ATPase, 2 Ca²⁺ per ATP, causing relaxation.
  • Calsequestrin binds 40–43 Ca²⁺ per molecule inside the SR, storing Ca²⁺ while keeping free SR Ca²⁺ low so the pump can work.

Cardiac and smooth muscle

Cardiac

Similar to skeletal muscle but cells are joined by intercalated discs with desmosomes (maculae adherentes), fasciae adherentes and gap junctions; Purkinje fibres conduct impulses. Cardiac myosin II has lower ATPase activity than skeletal myosin. Thin filaments use cardiac isoforms of actin, tropomyosin and troponin, with six thin filaments around each thick filament, and Ca²⁺–troponin regulation.

Smooth

  • No sarcomeres; individual fusiform cells (20 µm in small vessels to 200–300 µm in the gut), linked by gap junctions; they also make connective-tissue matrix.
  • Dense bodies containing α-actinin replace Z discs.
  • No troponin; about twice as much actin–tropomyosin; 12 thin filaments per thick (versus 6 in striated muscle).
  • Smooth myosin has two heavy and four light chains; isolated, it does not form filaments and its ATPase is blocked until regulated (light-chain phosphorylation).
