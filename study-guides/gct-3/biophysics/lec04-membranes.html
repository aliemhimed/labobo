Biological Membranes

How the membrane model developed from Pfeffer to the fluid mosaic model, the amphipathic phospholipids that form bilayers, what controls fluidity (saturation and cholesterol), how lipids move, micelles versus liposomes, lipid phases and melting, and how proteins are built and sit in the bilayer.

Labobo topic: Biophys Lec 4: Biological Membranes

In 60 seconds

  • History: Pfeffer 1877 (plasma membrane), Overton 1895 (permeation follies oil–water partition: membranes are oily), Langmuir 1917 (monolayers), Gorter and Grendel 1925 (lipid area = 2 × RBC area → bilayer), Singer–Nicolson 1972 (fluid mosaic), Racker–Stoeckenius 1974 (light-driven ATP in artificial membranes), Mouritsen–Bloom 1984 (mattress model).
  • Phospholipids are amphipathic; phosphatidylcholine is the most common. Hydrophobic tails inside, hydrophilic heads outside.
  • Saturated tails pack tightly (less fluid); unsaturated tails lower packing (more fluid).
  • Cholesterol restricts phospholipid movement, disrupts regular packing, lowers permeability to small water-soluble ions and mixes lipids.
  • Lipids diffuse laterally fast, rotate, flex and rarely flip-flop. Single-chain lipids form micelles (above the CMC); two-chain lipids form bilayers/liposomes.
  • Phases: crystalline → gel → ripple → fluid; melting shows as a heat-capacity peak. Proteins are integral or peripheral.

History of membrane models

The membrane is the "skin" of the cell and of organelles; the nuclear envelope is a double membrane with pore complexes.

YearWhoFinding
1877Wilhelm PfefferOsmotic pressure of plant cells → protoplasm surrounded by a plasma membrane
1895Charles OvertonPermeation follows the oil–water partition coefficient → membranes behave like oils
1917Irving LangmuirSpread lipid monolayers at an air–water interface
1925Gorter and GrendelLipids from erythrocytes (extracted with acetone) covered twice the cell surface area → bilayer

1984Mouritsen and BloomMattress model: hydrophobic matching between protein and bilayer thickness

Phospholipids and the bilayer

Phospholipids are amphipathic: a hydrophilic (water-attracting) head and hydrophobic (water-avoiding) fatty-acid tails. In water they form a bilayer with tails in the centre and heads on both surfaces. Phosphatidylcholine (PC) is the most common; others are phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), phosphatidylserine (PS), phosphatidylinositol (PI), sphingomyelin (SM) and cardiolipin (CL). Head-group composition differs between membranes.

In the fluid mosaic model proteins float in a fluid lipid bilayer.

Fluidity and cholesterol

Fluidity depends on lipid composition. Bilayers of saturated lipids pack much more closely than those with unsaturated (kinked) lipids or mixtures, so they are less fluid.

Cholesterol (polar head, rigid steroid ring, hydrocarbon chain):

  • restricts movement of phospholipids → reduces fluidity;
  • disrupts the regular packing of hydrocarbon chains → increases flexibility;
  • reduces permeability to small water-soluble molecules and ions (Na⁺, H⁺);
  • reduces segregation of different phospholipids, keeping composition more mixed.

Lipid motion, micelles and liposomes

The bilayer is a two-dimensional fluid. Phospholipids:

  • move rapidly sideways in the plane (lateral diffusion);
  • rotate rapidly about their long axis;
  • flex their fatty-acyl tails constantly;
  • occasionally move across the bilayer (flip-flop), which is slow.

Single-chain lipids self-assemble into micelles once their concentration exceeds the critical micellar concentration (CMC). Two-chain phospholipids cannot pack well in a micelle, so they form bilayers and liposomes, which cost less energy. Liposomes can fuse with each other or with a larger membrane, which is how membranes grow.

Lipid phases and melting

PhaseOrder
CrystallineThree-dimensional order
GelBilayer with ordered, tilted chains (tilt depends on head group)
RipplePartially melted
FluidDisordered chains; head ordering also lost

Lipid melting (gel → fluid) appears as a peak in the heat-capacity profile measured by calorimetry (e.g. dimyristoyl-PC). Longer chains melt at higher temperatures. Bacteria such as E. coli adjust membrane composition so their membranes melt just below the growth temperature, keeping them fluid.

Membrane proteins

Proteins are chains of amino acids, whose side chains are hydrophobic, charged or polar uncharged (with special cases such as glycine, proline and cysteine).

LevelDescription
PrimaryAmino-acid sequence joined by peptide bonds
Secondaryα-helix: H-bond between C=O of one residue and N–H four residues on; β-pleated sheet: H-bonds between more distant parts of the backbone
TertiaryOverall 3D fold of one chain
QuaternarySeveral subunits, e.g. haemoglobin

Water-soluble proteins have hydrophobic residues inside and hydrophilic ones outside; membrane-spanning segments have hydrophobic residues facing the lipid tails. Integral proteins are embedded in or span the bilayer; peripheral proteins attach to the surface. Functions include transport, enzymes, receptors, anchoring and cell recognition.

Key terms
