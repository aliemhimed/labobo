Kidney Metabolism, Acid–Base Balance and Minerals

What the kidney does for glucose and acid–base balance, how bicarbonate is reclaimed and acid is excreted, how to read compensation, and the roles, regulation and diseases of the major minerals and trace elements.

Labobo topic: Biochem Lec 2: Kidney Metabolism and Minerals

In 60 seconds

  • Kidney: excretion, erythropoietin, calcitriol, and 20–40% of gluconeogenesis after absorption, mostly from glutamine in the proximal tubule.
  • Blood pH 7.36–7.44. Lungs correct in minutes, kidneys in hours to days.
  • Proximal tubule reclaims ~80% of filtered HCO₃⁻: NHE3 secretes H⁺, CA IV (lumen) and CA II (cell), NBCe1 out to blood. Acetazolamide → mild metabolic acidosis.
  • α-Intercalated cells secrete H⁺; β-intercalated cells secrete HCO₃⁻ via pendrin. Aldosterone excess → hypokalaemia + metabolic alkalosis.
  • Ca: 98% in bone; ionised fraction is active. PTH ↑Ca ↓P; calcitriol ↑ both; calcitonin ↓ both. Refeeding → hypophosphataemia.
  • Copper: Menkes (ATP7A) deficiency vs Wilson (ATP7B) excess with low ceruloplasmin. Iron: DMT1 in, ferroportin out, transferrin in blood; high TIBC = iron deficiency.
  • Zinc → acrodermatitis enteropathica; selenium → glutathione peroxidase, deiodinases, Keshan disease.

Renal gluconeogenesis

  • After absorption the kidneys make about 20–40% of new glucose, mainly in the proximal convoluted tubules of the cortex; their share grows in prolonged fasting as liver glycogen runs out.
  • Glutamine is the preferred substrate: its carbons make glucose, its nitrogen makes NH₃ to buffer H⁺. In metabolic acidosis this gives both acid excretion and glucose.
  • Glucose 6-phosphatase removes the last phosphate so free glucose can leave the cell.

pH and the bicarbonate buffer

pH = −log[H⁺]. Blood pH is held at 7.36–7.44; intracellular pH is about 7.1 (6.9–7.4); survival limits are roughly 6.8–7.8.

CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻. The lungs change CO₂ removal within seconds to minutes. The kidneys reabsorb filtered HCO₃⁻, make new HCO₃⁻ and excrete H⁺; full compensation takes hours to days.

Bicarbonate reabsorption in the proximal tubule

About 80% of filtered HCO₃⁻ is reclaimed here. HCO₃⁻ cannot cross the luminal membrane, so it is rebuilt inside the cell.

  1. NHE3 moves Na⁺ in and H⁺ out into the lumen (plus some H⁺-ATPase).
  2. H⁺ + filtered HCO₃⁻ → H₂CO₃; luminal carbonic anhydrase IV splits it to CO₂ + H₂O, which diffuse in.
  3. Cytosolic carbonic anhydrase II rebuilds H⁺ + HCO₃⁻; the H⁺ is recycled to the lumen.
  4. HCO₃⁻ leaves to blood mainly by NBCe1 (Na⁺/HCO₃⁻ cotransport), also by Cl⁻/HCO₃⁻ exchange.
In the clinic: angiotensin II and acetazolamide

Angiotensin II stimulates NHE3 and H⁺-ATPase, so volume depletion raises HCO₃⁻ reabsorption and can cause metabolic alkalosis. Acetazolamide blocks proximal carbonic anhydrase: more Na⁺, HCO₃⁻ and water in urine and a mild metabolic acidosis.

Aldosterone raises Na⁺ reabsorption and K⁺ secretion in principal cells and H⁺ secretion in α-intercalated cells (with HCO₃⁻ gain). Excess gives hypokalaemia and metabolic alkalosis.

Secreted H⁺ is buffered in urine as NH₄⁺ (ammonia) and H₂PO₄⁻ (phosphate), so about 50 mmol of H⁺ can be excreted daily without dangerously acidic urine.

Calcium, phosphate and magnesium

Minerals are inorganic elements needed in small amounts for bones and teeth, fluid balance, nerve conduction, contraction, signalling and catalysis.

Calcium and phosphate

  • Calcium: most abundant mineral, ~98% in bone. About 40% of serum Ca is protein-bound (inactive); the ionised fraction is active.
  • Phosphate: second most abundant; the main intracellular anion; ~85% in hydroxyapatite. Needed for nucleic acids, ATP, creatine phosphate, enzyme and B-vitamin activation, and pH balance.
HormoneSourceCa²⁺Phosphate

In the clinic: refeeding syndrome

Sudden carbohydrate after starvation → insulin surge drives phosphate (and K⁺) into cells for ATP → low serum phosphate, weakness, arrhythmias.

Magnesium

  • ~99% in bone or inside cells; cofactor for 300+ enzymes; stabilises ATP for kinases; needed by DNA/RNA polymerases.
  • Natural Ca²⁺ antagonist. Low Mg → more Ca²⁺ enters nerve terminals → irritability, arrhythmias. High Mg → hypotension, weak reflexes, cardiac depression.
  • Magnesium sulfate treats preeclampsia: relaxes vessels and prevents seizures.

Copper: Menkes and Wilson

Copper is part of ferroxidases (ceruloplasmin, hephaestin) that oxidise Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺ for transport and storage, so copper lack causes functional iron-deficiency anaemia. It is also a factor V cofactor.

Iron

Adults hold 3–4 g of iron. Heme Fe²⁺ (animal foods) is less abundant but better absorbed than non-heme Fe³⁺ (plants). Iron is in hemoglobin, myoglobin, Fe–S proteins of the ETC, cytochromes c and P450, peroxidases and metalloproteases.

Absorption (duodenum and upper jejunum)

  1. Ferrireductase (helped by vitamin C) reduces Fe³⁺ to Fe²⁺, which enters by DMT1.
  2. Heme enters by HCP1; heme oxygenase releases Fe²⁺, CO and biliverdin.
  3. Paraferritin: a DMT1-independent route for Fe³⁺.
  4. Iron is stored as ferritin or carried by mobilferrin to the basolateral side, exported by ferroportin, oxidised by hephaestin (and ceruloplasmin) and carried by transferrin. Hepcidin blocks absorption.

TIBC measures empty transferrin sites: high in iron deficiency, low in overload or chronic inflammation. Iron deficiency gives a microcytic hypochromic anaemia, the most common anaemia.

Watch out

Slide 23 calls hepcidin “the enzyme”. Lippincott describes it as a liver peptide hormone that makes ferroportin be internalised and degraded, which is how it stops iron absorption. If asked, hepcidin acts on ferroportin.

In the clinic: hemochromatosis

“Bronze diabetes”: cirrhosis, diabetes, skin pigmentation, plus hypogonadism, arthropathy, cardiomyopathy; slower in women because of menstruation. High serum iron and transferrin saturation. Treat with phlebotomy or deferoxamine. Primary = absorption gene mutations; secondary = transfusions (β-thalassaemia), liver disease, excess intake.

Zinc and iodine

Zinc

Structural (zinc fingers bind DNA) and catalytic: alcohol dehydrogenase, carbonic anhydrase, porphobilinogen synthase (inhibited by lead), cytosolic SOD.

In the clinic: acrodermatitis enteropathica

Autosomal recessive ZIP zinc transporter defect: alopecia, diarrhoea, periorificial dermatitis, starting at weaning (breast milk has a zinc-binding ligand). Also growth delay, poor immunity, hypogonadism, taste and smell loss, poor wound healing.

Iodine

Iodide is concentrated in thyroid follicular cells; thyroperoxidase oxidises it to I₂ and iodinates thyroglobulin tyrosines to MIT and DIT. DIT + DIT = T4; MIT + DIT = T3.

Selenium and other trace elements

Selenium works as selenocysteine in glutathione peroxidases, thioredoxin reductases and iodothyronine deiodinases (T4 → T3); it spares vitamin E. Deficiency + Coxsackievirus = Keshan cardiomyopathy. Toxicity: acute diarrhoea, tachycardia, tetany; chronic alopecia, nail dystrophy, cachexia.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • Filtered HCO₃⁻ itself never crosses the cell; its equivalent is rebuilt inside.
  • Acetazolamide causes acidosis; excess aldosterone causes alkalosis.
  • In Wilson disease urine copper is high but ceruloplasmin is low.
  • High TIBC means low iron.
  • Glutathione peroxidase needs selenium; carbonic anhydrase needs zinc; arginase needs manganese.
  • MIT + DIT = T3, not T4.

Key terms

NHE3: luminal Na⁺/H⁺ exchanger · NBCe1: basolateral Na⁺/HCO₃⁻ cotransporter · Carbonic anhydrase: zinc enzyme, CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ · Pendrin: Cl⁻/HCO₃⁻ exchanger of β-intercalated cells · ENaC: epithelial Na⁺ channel · Titratable acid: H⁺ buffered by phosphate · Hydroxyapatite: Ca–phosphate bone mineral · Refeeding syndrome: hypophosphataemia after feeding the starved · Ceruloplasmin: copper ferroxidase of plasma · ATP7A/ATP7B: copper ATPases of Menkes/Wilson · DMT1: Fe²⁺ importer · Ferroportin: iron exporter · Hephaestin: enterocyte ferroxidase · Transferrin: plasma Fe³⁺ carrier · TIBC: total iron-binding capacity · Hepcidin: hormone that blocks iron absorption · Zinc finger: DNA-binding motif · Thyroperoxidase: iodinates thyroglobulin · Selenocysteine: catalytic Se amino acid · Keshan disease: Se-deficiency cardiomyopathy.
