Brain Metabolism and Neurotransmission

How the blood–brain barrier protects and feeds the brain, what fuels neurons use, and how the main neurotransmitters (catecholamines, serotonin, acetylcholine, histamine, glutamate and GABA) are made, stored and broken down, with the diseases that follow when a step fails.

Labobo topic: Biochem Lec 1: Neurotransmitters

In 60 seconds

  • The blood–brain barrier = endothelium with tight junctions + basement membrane + astrocyte foot processes; P-glycoprotein pumps drugs back out. GLUT-1 brings glucose in, MCT1 ketones and lactate, LAT1 large neutral amino acids.
  • Brain fuel: glucose normally, ketone bodies in prolonged fasting, lactate in exercise; not fatty acids.
  • Catecholamines: Tyr →(tyrosine hydroxylase, BH4, rate-limiting) L-DOPA →(PLP) dopamine →(vitamin C, Cu²⁺) norepinephrine →(PNMT, SAM, induced by cortisol) epinephrine.
  • Signal ends by reuptake first, then MAO (FAD, outer mitochondrial membrane) and COMT (Mg²⁺, SAM). HVA = dopamine breakdown; 5-HIAA = serotonin breakdown.
  • Serotonin: Trp →(tryptophan hydroxylase, BH4) 5-HTP →(PLP) 5-HT → melatonin (acetyl-CoA + SAM). Carcinoid → high urinary 5-HIAA, pellagra, right-heart valve disease.
  • ACh = choline + acetyl-CoA (choline acetyltransferase); CHT1 choline uptake is rate-limiting. Alzheimer: Aβ42 plaques, tau tangles, loss of the nucleus basalis of Meynert.
  • GABA = glutamate decarboxylated by GAD (PLP); broken down by GABA-T (PLP). B6 deficiency → neonatal seizures.

The blood–brain barrier

Neurons are permanent cells that do not divide in adulthood, so the brain is protected by a barrier around its capillaries. It keeps out microorganisms, cells, proteins and many drugs.

LayerWhat it is
InnerCapillary endothelial cells joined by tight junctions
MedialCapillary basement membrane
OuterAstrocyte foot processes, linked by gap junctions

The endothelium also defends actively: P-glycoprotein uses ATP to pump lipid-soluble foreign compounds back into the blood, and liver-like drug-metabolising enzymes form an enzymatic barrier against transmitters and toxins.

Transporters that let nutrients in

TransporterCarries
GLUT-1Glucose (endothelium, glia)

In the clinic: leaky spots and PKU

The area postrema (vomiting centre) and hypothalamus have a leakier barrier, which is why chemotherapy causes vomiting. In phenylketonuria, huge amounts of phenylalanine saturate LAT1, so less tyrosine and tryptophan reach the brain, dopamine and serotonin fall, and intellectual disability follows.

Brain fuels

The brain needs a lot of energy all the time and relies on oxidative metabolism.

  • Glucose: the main fuel when fed. It crosses the barrier on GLUT-1 and enters neurons on GLUT-3. It feeds glycolysis and the pentose phosphate pathway; glycogen is made only in astrocytes, and stores are small.
  • Ketone bodies (β-hydroxybutyrate, acetoacetate): important in prolonged fasting.
  • Lactate: used in intense exercise or starvation.
  • Fatty acids: not significant; they cross the barrier poorly and the brain oxidises them poorly.
In the clinic: GLUT-1 deficiency syndrome

Infants with seizures, microcephaly and developmental delay; CSF glucose is low while blood glucose is normal.

Catecholamine synthesis

Catecholamines (dopamine, norepinephrine, epinephrine) are made from tyrosine. Dopamine controls movement, motivation and reward; norepinephrine raises alertness and constricts vessels; epinephrine, mainly from the adrenal medulla, drives “fight or flight”.

Regulation

  • High catecholamines feedback-inhibit tyrosine hydroxylase by reducing its use of BH4.
  • Neuronal activity phosphorylates tyrosine hydroxylase, raising activity and lowering feedback sensitivity.
  • Cortisol from the adrenal cortex induces PNMT, so the medulla makes epinephrine.
  • Metyrosine inhibits tyrosine hydroxylase and is used in pheochromocytoma.

Storage and termination of the signal

Storage

V-ATPase pumps H⁺ into vesicles and VMAT2 swaps H⁺ for catecholamine (secondary active transport). In noradrenergic neurons, dopamine β-hydroxylase works inside the vesicle. Vesicles store catecholamines with ATP and chromogranins. Norepinephrine neurons are concentrated in the locus coeruleus; NE is the main transmitter of the sympathetic system.

Termination

  1. Reuptake into the presynaptic neuron by NET and DAT (main route).
  2. Enzymes: MAO on the outer mitochondrial membrane (uses FAD; MAO-A prefers NE and serotonin, MAO-B phenylethylamines) and COMT in many cells including erythrocytes (uses Mg²⁺, methyl from SAM).

Homovanillic acid (HVA) in CSF reflects dopamine breakdown and is low in Parkinson disease.

Pheochromocytoma, tyramine and Parkinson disease

Tyramine and MAO inhibitors

Gut MAO normally destroys dietary tyramine. With MAO inhibited, tyramine (aged cheese, wine) enters the blood and releases norepinephrine from sympathetic terminals: vasoconstriction, very high blood pressure, headache, palpitations.

Pheochromocytoma

In the clonidine suppression test, clonidine (central α₂ agonist) normally lowers plasma catecholamines; a tumour keeps them high. High catecholamines can also come from MAO-A deficiency (Brunner syndrome).

Parkinson disease

  • Loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta upsets the dopamine–acetylcholine balance.
  • Oxidised catecholamines and MAO aldehydes react with α-synuclein, which misfolds into Lewy bodies.
  • Drug-induced parkinsonism: D2 blockers (haloperidol, metoclopramide) and VMAT2 inhibitors (reserpine).
  • Treatment: levodopa crosses the barrier (dopamine cannot) and is turned into dopamine by AADC; carbidopa blocks this conversion outside the brain.

Serotonin, melatonin and carcinoid syndrome

StepEnzyme / needs
Trp → 5-hydroxytryptophanTryptophan hydroxylase, BH4
5-HTP → serotoninDecarboxylase, PLP
Serotonin → 5-HIAAMAO-A, then oxidation; 5-HIAA is excreted in urine
Serotonin → melatonin (pineal)Acetylation (acetyl-CoA) and methylation (SAM); rises in darkness

Serotonin regulates mood, appetite, pain, gut motility, vascular and platelet function. The serotonin release assay diagnoses heparin-induced thrombocytopenia.

In the clinic: carcinoid syndrome

Gut tumour serotonin is cleared by the liver, so symptoms appear when the tumour metastasises to the liver: flushing, diarrhoea, bronchospasm and right-sided valve fibrosis (the lungs clear serotonin, sparing the left heart). Urinary 5-HIAA is high, and diverting about 60% of tryptophan to serotonin leaves too little for niacin, causing pellagra.

Acetylcholine

ACh is made from choline + acetyl-CoA by choline acetyltransferase. Because acetyl-CoA comes from glucose oxidation, hypoxia or hypoglycaemia lowers ACh synthesis.

  • Nicotinic receptors: ligand-gated ion channels (ganglia, neuromuscular junction).
  • Muscarinic receptors: G-protein-coupled (brain, smooth and cardiac muscle, glands); M2 slows the heart.
  • ACh contracts skeletal muscle, slows the heart, constricts pupils and bronchi, increases secretions and gut motility, and supports attention and memory.

Breakdown and choline supply

Acetylcholinesterase splits ACh into choline and acetate. Choline is taken back by the Na⁺-dependent CHT1 transporter, the rate-limiting step for resynthesis. Neurons cannot make choline: they get it by recycling (about 50–60%), from the diet (crossing the barrier on CTL1), or by breaking down membrane phosphatidylcholine and sphingomyelin. Only the liver makes choline, by methylating phosphatidylethanolamine with PEMT and SAM (folate and B12 matter).

In the clinic: myasthenia gravis and organophosphates

Myasthenia gravis: autoantibodies against ACh receptors → ptosis, diplopia, weakness that worsens with use; treat with pyridostigmine (AChE inhibitor). Organophosphates irreversibly inhibit AChE → ACh floods both muscarinic and nicotinic receptors.

Alzheimer disease

The commonest dementia; early anterograde amnesia (hippocampus), later retrograde amnesia, language and behaviour changes.

APP pathwayFirst cutResult
Non-amyloidogenic (normal)α-secretase → sAPPα + C83γ-secretase → harmless p3 + AICD
Amyloidogenic (disease)β-secretase → sAPPβ + C99γ-secretase → Aβ42 → extracellular plaques
  • Tau normally stabilises microtubules; hyperphosphorylated tau detaches and forms intracellular neurofibrillary tangles.
  • Cholinergic hypothesis: loss of neurons in the nucleus basalis of Meynert lowers ACh in cortex and hippocampus; treated with AChE inhibitors donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine.

Histamine

Made from histidine in one step by histidine decarboxylase (PLP). In the CNS it keeps us awake; in the lungs it constricts bronchi.

WhereBreakdown
CNSHistamine N-methyltransferase (SAM) → N-methylhistamine; then MAO-B and ALDH → N-methylimidazoleacetic acid (urine)
PeripheryDiamine oxidase → imidazole-4-acetaldehyde; ALDH → imidazole-4-acetic acid
In the clinic: sleepy antihistamines

First-generation antihistamines cross the barrier and block central H1 receptors fed by the tuberomammillary nucleus; they also block M1, α1 and 5-HT2A receptors, adding to drowsiness.

Glutamate and GABA

Glutamate, the main excitatory transmitter

Made from α-ketoglutarate by glutamate dehydrogenase or by transamination, or from glutamine by glutaminase. Acts on ionotropic (NMDA, AMPA) and metabotropic receptors. In the glutamate–glutamine cycle, astrocytes take up glutamate and make glutamine, which neurons turn back into glutamate.

GABA, the main inhibitory transmitter

  • Made by glutamate decarboxylase (GAD), which needs PLP; packed into vesicles by VGAT.
  • Cleared by GAT-1 and broken down by GABA transaminase (PLP) to succinic semialdehyde, then SSADH.
  • Vigabatrin irreversibly inhibits GABA-T; tiagabine blocks reuptake.

Low vitamin B6 impairs GAD, lowers GABA and can cause intractable neonatal seizures.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • Rate-limiting step is tyrosine hydroxylase, not phenylalanine hydroxylase or DOPA decarboxylase.
  • Dopamine β-hydroxylase needs vitamin C and copper (copper deficiency in Menkes affects it too).
  • Carbidopa does not enter the brain; it acts peripherally.
  • Main termination is reuptake, not enzymes.
  • Carcinoid valve disease is right-sided because the lungs clear serotonin.
  • The rate-limiting step for ACh resynthesis is choline uptake (CHT1), not choline acetyltransferase.
  • Glycogen in the brain is only in astrocytes.

Key terms

Blood–brain barrier: endothelium with tight junctions, basement membrane and astrocyte feet · P-glycoprotein: ATP-driven efflux pump of the endothelium · LAT1: carrier for large neutral amino acids · BH4: tetrahydrobiopterin, cofactor of aromatic amino acid hydroxylases · PNMT: methylates norepinephrine to epinephrine · VMAT2: vesicular H⁺/monoamine exchanger · MAO: FAD enzyme that deaminates monoamines · COMT: SAM-dependent methyltransferase · HVA: dopamine end product · 5-HIAA: serotonin end product · Lewy body: α-synuclein aggregate · Clonidine suppression test: catecholamines fail to fall in pheochromocytoma · CHT1: high-affinity choline transporter · PEMT: liver enzyme making choline · Aβ42: plaque-forming amyloid peptide · Tau: microtubule protein forming tangles · GAD: glutamate decarboxylase · GABA-T: GABA transaminase · Vigabatrin: irreversible GABA-T inhibitor · Tuberomammillary nucleus: histamine wake centre.
