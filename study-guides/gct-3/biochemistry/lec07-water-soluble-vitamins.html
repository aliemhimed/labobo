Water-Soluble Vitamins

For each B vitamin and vitamin C: its coenzyme form, the enzymes that need it, who becomes deficient, what the deficiency looks like, how it is diagnosed and treated, and when it becomes toxic.

Labobo topic: Biochem Lec 7: Water-Soluble Vitamins

In 60 seconds

  • B1 (TPP): PDH, α-KGDH, BCKDH, transketolase. Beriberi (dry = neuropathy, wet = cardiomyopathy), Wernicke–Korsakoff. Give thiamine before glucose.
  • B2 (FAD, FMN): redox; glossitis, cheilitis, photophobia; test RBC glutathione reductase + FAD.
  • B3 (NAD, NADP): made from tryptophan (needs B2, B6). Pellagra 4 Ds (corn, Hartnup, carcinoid, isoniazid). Toxicity: flushing, gout, hyperglycaemia.
  • B5 (CoA): burning feet, adrenal insufficiency. B6 (PLP): transaminases, decarboxylases, ALA synthase; sideroblastic anaemia, seizures, isoniazid; toxic >500 mg/day.
  • B7 (biotin): carboxylases; raw egg white (avidin); biotinidase and holocarboxylase synthetase defects.
  • B9 (folate): one-carbon transfer; megaloblastic anaemia, high homocysteine, normal MMA, NTDs (0.4 mg/day).
  • B12: cobalt; MMA and homocysteine both high; nerve damage; IF absorbed in terminal ileum (cubilin); pernicious anaemia; folate trap.
  • C: prolyl/lysyl hydroxylases (collagen), iron absorption, dopamine β-hydroxylase, carnitine, PAM; scurvy.

Water- vs fat-soluble

Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) are stored in liver and fat and can be toxic. Water-soluble vitamins travel in blood, are stored in small amounts and lost in urine, so deficiency develops faster and toxicity is rare. Exceptions: B12 is stored in the liver for years (2–5 mg); B6 and niacin are toxic at drug doses. Malnourished patients (alcohol use, eating disorders, bariatric surgery, unsupplemented TPN) usually have several deficiencies.

Vitamin B1 (thiamine)

  • Active form thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP). Absorbed in the jejunum (active transport at low levels, diffusion at high). Body stores only ~30 mg, mostly in skeletal muscle. High carbohydrate intake raises the need.
  • TPP enzymes: pyruvate dehydrogenase, α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, branched-chain α-ketoacid dehydrogenase, transketolase.
  • Test: low RBC transketolase that rises >25% with added TPP.
  • Risk: polished rice, thiaminase-rich raw fish, alcohol use disorder.
SyndromeFeatures
Dry beriberiPeripheral neuropathy, especially legs
Wet beriberiOedema from dilated cardiomyopathy
Wernicke encephalopathyConfusion, ataxia, nystagmus, ophthalmoplegia
Korsakoff syndromeMemory loss with confabulation; memory recovery incomplete
Watch out: glucose first?

Glucose raises the demand on thiamine-dependent PDH; without TPP, pyruvate piles up and becomes lactate (lactic acidosis), and Wernicke can be precipitated. Give thiamine before glucose. (Beriberi and Wernicke–Korsakoff details are from Lippincott p. 1054; the slides show them as pictures.)

Vitamin B2 (riboflavin)

Forms FMN and FAD for redox reactions.

  • FAD: succinate dehydrogenase, acyl-CoA dehydrogenase, xanthine oxidase, glutathione reductase. FMN: complex I.
  • Test: low RBC glutathione reductase that rises with added FAD.
  • RDA 1.1–1.3 mg/day (dairy, eggs, liver, greens); destroyed by light. 400 mg/day reduces migraines and turns urine bright yellow.
  • Deficiency: angular cheilitis, glossitis, seborrhoeic dermatitis, photophobia, corneal vascularisation, anaemia. Worsens B6 and niacin deficiency (FMN activates B6; FAD in Trp → niacin).

Vitamin B3 (niacin)

Forms NAD⁺/NADH (catabolic redox) and NADP⁺/NADPH (synthesis of fatty acids and steroids, respiratory burst, P450, glutathione reductase). Can be made from tryptophan (needs B2 and B6).

Cause of pellagraWhy
Corn (maize) dietNo usable niacin
Hartnup diseaseTryptophan lost in gut and urine
Malignant carcinoidTryptophan diverted to serotonin
IsoniazidLowers B6

Pellagra = dermatitis (sun-exposed), diarrhoea, dementia, death.

Toxicity and use in dyslipidaemia

  • Flushing: GPR109A → PGD₂, PGE₂ → vasodilation; prevented by aspirin (COX).
  • Hyperuricaemia/gout: less renal urate excretion. Hyperglycaemia: insulin resistance.
  • Lipids: ↓ cAMP inhibits HSL (fewer FFA); inhibits hepatic DGAT2 (less VLDL, LDL); slows HDL-apoA-I breakdown (↑ HDL).

Vitamin B5 (pantothenic acid)

Part of coenzyme A: needed for fatty acid, cholesterol and acetylcholine synthesis. Deficiency is very rare: burning feet (distal paresthesias), alopecia, dermatitis, enteritis and adrenal insufficiency, because steroidogenesis needs CoA.

Vitamin B6 (pyridoxine)

Active form pyridoxal phosphate (PLP): transaminases (AST, ALT), decarboxylases (dopamine, GABA, serotonin), ALA synthase, cystathionine synthase, glycogen phosphorylase.

  • Deficiency: cheilosis, glossitis, stomatitis; microcytic sideroblastic anaemia (ringed sideroblasts, high ferritin); irritability, depression, confusion, seizures (GAD cannot make GABA), neuropathy.
  • Isoniazid binds B6 into an inactive complex; give pyridoxine 25–50 mg/day with INH.
  • Toxic: >500 mg/day for months → sensory neuropathy.

Vitamin B7 (biotin)

Cofactor of carboxylases, bound to a lysine: acetyl-CoA carboxylase, pyruvate carboxylase, propionyl-CoA carboxylase, 3-methylcrotonyl-CoA carboxylase.

  • Deficiency is rare: raw egg whites (avidin), anticonvulsants (carbamazepine, phenytoin, primidone).
  • Features: periorificial rash, alopecia, depression, hallucinations, paresthesias, GI upset, myalgia, metabolic acidosis.
  • Recycling: proteolysis gives biocytin; biotinidase frees biotin. Biotinidase deficiency blocks recycling; holocarboxylase synthetase deficiency presents earlier (neonatal acidosis, rash) and needs higher doses.

Vitamin B9 (folate)

Folic acid = pteridine + PABA (pteroic acid) + glutamates. It must be reduced to be active (THF, N5-methyl-THF, N10-formyl-THF). Folates carry one-carbon units for methylation, purine and thymine synthesis, and methionine and choline synthesis.

DrugTarget
MethotrexateHuman DHFR
TrimethoprimBacterial DHFR
PyrimethamineProtozoal DHFR
SulfonamidesDihydropteroate synthase (PABA analogues)

Deficiency (the most common vitamin deficiency)

  • Causes: alcohol use disorder, pregnancy, haemolysis, coeliac disease, methotrexate, TMP-SMX.
  • Effects: megaloblastic anaemia with hypersegmented neutrophils; ↑ homocysteine (endothelial damage, thrombosis); neural tube defects.
  • Labs: ↓ RBC folate, ↑ homocysteine, normal MMA; no neurological signs.
  • NTD prevention: 0.4 mg/day from ≥1 month before conception to week 12; 4–5 mg/day after a previous NTD pregnancy or on valproate/carbamazepine. Treatment: 1–5 mg/day.
Watch out

Always check B12 before giving folic acid alone: folate can correct the anaemia of B12 deficiency while nerve damage progresses.

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin)

  • Cobalt in a corrin ring; coenzymes 5′-deoxyadenosylcobalamin and methylcobalamin; cyanocobalamin is the commercial form. Only in animal products (or made by gut bacteria).
  • Needed for odd-chain fatty acid metabolism: deficiency raises methylmalonic acid, which (with abnormal fatty acids in CNS membranes) damages myelin → neuropathy.

Absorption

  1. Acid and pepsin free B12 from food; it binds haptocorrin (protects from acid).
  2. In the duodenum trypsin releases it; it binds intrinsic factor.
  3. B12–IF is absorbed in the terminal ileum by cubilin.
  4. In blood it travels on transcobalamin II.

Folate trap and pernicious anaemia

B12 converts N5-methyl-THF back to THF; without it, folate is trapped in the methyl form and nucleotide synthesis fails in marrow and gut. Pernicious anaemia: autoimmune destruction of parietal cells → no intrinsic factor; linked to gastric adenocarcinoma.

In the clinic: scurvy

Perifollicular haemorrhage, corkscrew hairs, swollen bleeding gums, loose teeth, poor wound healing, haemarthrosis, subperiosteal haemorrhage, anaemia. At risk: elderly, alcohol use, restrictive diets, smokers, dialysis.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • B6 is the water-soluble vitamin with significant toxicity (sensory neuropathy).
  • B6 deficiency anaemia is microcytic; folate and B12 deficiency are macrocytic.
  • The B12 enzyme in odd-chain metabolism is the mutase; the carboxylase uses biotin.
  • Pancreatic disease can cause B12 deficiency: trypsin is needed to free B12 from haptocorrin.
  • Aspirin before niacin prevents flushing via COX.

Key terms

TPP: thiamine pyrophosphate · Transketolase test: B1 status · Beriberi: B1 deficiency (dry neuropathy, wet cardiomyopathy) · Wernicke–Korsakoff: B1 deficiency brain syndrome · Confabulation: unintended false memories · FAD/FMN: riboflavin coenzymes · Pellagra: niacin deficiency · Hartnup disease: neutral amino acid transport defect · GPR109A: niacin receptor causing flushing · Coenzyme A: B5-derived acyl carrier · PLP: B6 coenzyme · Sideroblastic anaemia: ringed sideroblasts, failed heme synthesis · Avidin: egg white biotin binder · Biotinidase: frees biotin from biocytin · THF: tetrahydrofolate · DHFR: dihydrofolate reductase · MMA: methylmalonic acid · Intrinsic factor: gastric B12 carrier · Folate trap: folate stuck as N5-methyl-THF · Scurvy: vitamin C deficiency.
