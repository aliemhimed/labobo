Secondary Lymphoid Tissue and Leukocyte Migration

How lymphatics carry antigen to lymph nodes, the zones of a lymph node and the chemokines that keep B and T cells apart, the structure and filtering role of the spleen, mucosal lymphoid tissue, and the multistep process by which leukocytes leave the blood and lymphocytes recirculate.

Labobo topic: Immuno Lec 7: Secondary Lymphoid Tissue and Leukocyte Migration

In 60 seconds

  • Secondary lymphoid organs (lymph nodes, spleen, MALT) are where naive lymphocytes meet antigen and become effector and memory cells.
  • Lymph node: afferent lymphatics → subcapsular sinus → cortex (B-cell follicles) → paracortex (T cells, DCs) → medulla (medullary cords with plasma cells and macrophages) → efferent lymphatic.
  • Naive T and B cells enter nodes through high endothelial venules (specialised postcapillary venules). CCL19/CCL21 (FRCs) attract CCR7⁺ T cells and DCs; CXCL13 (FDCs) attracts B cells.
  • Primary follicles = naive B cells; secondary follicles have germinal centres (proliferation, high-affinity selection, memory and long-lived plasma cells).
  • Spleen: PALS (T cells), follicles (B cells), marginal zone (traps blood-borne antigen); red pulp filters blood. Asplenia → sepsis with encapsulated bacteria.
  • Leukocyte recruitment: selectin rolling → chemokine-raised integrin affinity → firm adhesion → transmigration (PECAM-1) → migration.

Lymphatics and the route of antigen

Interstitial fluid forms constantly from plasma filtered out of capillaries, and much faster when tissue is injured or infected. Lymphatic vessels drain this fluid and carry microbial antigens from their portals of entry (skin, gut, airways) to lymph nodes.

Epithelial barriers contain DCs that capture antigen and enter lymphatics through gaps in the basement membrane. Lymph nodes along the vessels act as filters, sampling lymph for soluble and DC-carried antigen. Lymph node development depends on lymphoid tissue-inducer cells and cytokines, chemokines and transcription factors.

In the clinic: reactive lymphadenitis

Lymph nodes swell during infection or inflammation as lymphocytes proliferate: mainly B cells in germinal centres and T cells in the paracortex.

Efferent lymphatic (hilum)Lymph leaves

The artery enters at the hilum and branches into capillaries; some postcapillary venules become high endothelial venules (HEVs), through which naive T and B cells leave the blood. HEVs are also in Peyer's patches but not in the spleen.

Follicles

  • Primary follicles: no germinal centre; mostly mature naive B cells.
  • Secondary follicles: germinal centres with activated B cells. Germinal centres are sites of B-cell proliferation, selection of high-affinity antibody, and generation of memory B cells and long-lived plasma cells.

B and T zones and chemokines

B and T cells are kept apart by chemokines made by specialised stromal cells:

ZoneStromal cellChemokine → receptor
T-cell zoneFibroblastic reticular cells (FRCs)CCL19, CCL21 → CCR7 (naive T cells and activated DCs)
B-cell follicleFollicular dendritic cells (FDCs), B-zone FRCsCXCL13 → CXCR5 (B cells)

DCs that meet microbes upregulate CCR7, migrate into lymphatics and gather in the T-cell zone with naive T cells, which also express CCR7, so rare antigen-specific T cells find their antigen. After a B cell binds antigen, it decreases CXCR5 and increases CCR7, moving to the T–B border to meet helper T cells.

The spleen

About 150 g in the left upper quadrant. It removes aged and damaged blood cells and particles (immune complexes, opsonised microbes) from the blood and starts adaptive responses to blood-borne antigens. It also stores platelets.

  • The splenic artery enters at the hilum; arterioles are surrounded by the periarteriolar lymphoid sheath (PALS): T cells.
  • Follicles in the white pulp: B cells.
  • Marginal zone between white and red pulp: macrophages and specialised B cells that capture blood-borne antigens.
  • Red pulp: blood-filled sinusoids with a fenestrated "barrel hoop" basement membrane; macrophages in the splenic cords filter microbes, damaged and opsonised cells (e.g. spherocytes).
  • Blood leaves through the splenic vein into the portal circulation.
In the clinic: asplenia

Without a spleen, patients are prone to overwhelming infection with encapsulated bacteria (pneumococci, meningococci, H. influenzae), because opsonisation, phagocytosis and antibody production against them all depend on the spleen. They need vaccination against these organisms.

Mucosal lymphoid tissue

Each epithelial barrier has its own lymph nodes, non-encapsulated lymphoid structures and scattered immune cells. MALT includes lymphoid tissue of the gut (GALT: tonsils, Peyer's patches, appendix), genitourinary tract, tracheobronchial tree and mammary glands. It is non-encapsulated and contains T and B cells, with B cells predominating.

Peyer's patches lie in the submucosa of the small intestine; their follicles are rich in B cells that become IgA-producing plasma cells. MALT lymphoma is a slow-growing non-Hodgkin B-cell lymphoma.

Leukocyte migration

Immune cells constantly move between blood and tissues. Migration delivers (1) neutrophils and monocytes to sites of infection, (2) naive lymphocytes from the marrow and thymus to secondary lymphoid organs, and (3) effector lymphocytes to infected tissues. This is why immunity is both local and systemic. Movement of a leukocyte into a particular tissue is called homing.

The multistep recruitment process

  1. Cytokines (TNF, IL-1) at the site activate endothelium.
  2. Selectin-mediated rolling: low-affinity binding of selectins to carbohydrate ligands (e.g. sialyl-Lewis X) on postcapillary venules.
  3. Chemokines increase integrin affinity on the leukocyte.
  4. Firm adhesion of integrins (e.g. LFA-1) to ligands such as ICAM-1.
  5. Transmigration between endothelial cells, involving PECAM-1 (CD31).
  6. Migration through tissue toward the infection along chemokine gradients.

Chemokines also build lymphoid organs, direct lymphocytes within them, and are needed for DC migration to draining nodes.

Lymphocyte recirculation

A naive T cell leaving the thymus homes to lymph nodes, spleen or MALT. If it does not meet its antigen, it leaves through lymphatics, returns to the blood and repeats the cycle. This recirculation maximises the chance that the few cells specific for an antigen will find it.

Key terms
