Complement, Inflammation and the Antiviral Response

The three pathways of complement and what C3b, C3a, C5a and the MAC do; acute inflammation and leukocyte recruitment; the cytokines TNF, IL-1 and IL-6 and their systemic effects; how phagocytes kill; and how type I interferons block viruses.

Labobo topic: Immuno Lec 5: Complement, Inflammation and Antiviral Response

In 60 seconds

  • Complement starts by three routes: classical (C1 binds antibody), alternative (C3 binds microbial surfaces such as LPS) and lectin (MBL binds mannose). All make C3 convertase → C5 convertase → MAC (C5b–C9).
  • C3b opsonises; C3a, C5a (and C4a) cause inflammation (anaphylatoxins); the MAC lyses. MAC lysis matters most against Neisseria.
  • Acute inflammation: vasodilation, increased permeability, leukocyte emigration. Neutrophils dominate the first 24–48 h, then monocytes. IL-8 attracts neutrophils.
  • TNF, IL-1, IL-6: fever (hypothalamus), leukocytosis and the acute-phase response (CRP, SAP, fibrinogen; IL-6 is the main driver). High TNF causes septic shock.
  • Killing: NADPH oxidase respiratory burst, NO (peroxynitrite), lysosomal enzymes, MPO (hypochlorite), NETs.
  • Type I IFNs (α, β) act on neighbouring cells to create an antiviral state and raise MHC I for CTL killing.

The complement system

Complement is a set of plasma proteins, mostly made by the liver, activated by proteolytic cascades: an inactive zymogen becomes a protease that activates the next protein. Activation begins with recognition of molecules on microbes but not on host cells.

PathwayTriggerNotes
ClassicalC1 binds antibody (IgG, IgM) on microbesDiscovered first; links to adaptive immunity
AlternativeC3 binds microbial surfaces directly (e.g. LPS)Discovered later but phylogenetically older
LectinMannose-binding lectin (MBL) binds terminal mannoseNo antibody needed

Every pathway builds a C3 convertase, which splits C3 into C3b and C3a. C3b joins other proteins to form a C5 convertase, which splits C5 into C5a and C5b. C5b starts assembly of C6, C7, C8 and C9 into the membrane attack complex, a pore that lyses the cell.

ProductFunction
C3bOpsonin: promotes phagocytosis
C3a, C5a (less C4a)Anaphylatoxins: attract neutrophils, degranulate mast cells, increase vascular permeability
MAC (C5b–9)Lysis; most important against thin-walled organisms such as Neisseria
In the clinic

Deficiency of C5–C9 predisposes to recurrent Neisseria infections. Loss of the regulators C1 inhibitor (hereditary angioedema) or DAF/CD55 and CD59 (paroxysmal nocturnal haemoglobinuria) lets complement attack the host.

Acute inflammation

Inflammation brings leukocytes and plasma proteins from the blood to sites of infection or injury. Acute inflammation develops within minutes to hours and is short; chronic inflammation lasts weeks to months, with injury and repair coexisting, and may follow acute inflammation or start insidiously.

It begins when sentinels (macrophages, DCs, mast cells) recognise PAMPs or DAMPs. Mast cells release histamine and prostaglandins. The three components are:

  1. Dilation of small vessels, increasing blood flow.
  2. Increased microvascular permeability, letting plasma proteins and leukocytes out.
  3. Emigration, accumulation and activation of leukocytes.

Neutrophils arrive first and dominate within 24–48 h (most numerous in blood, fastest to respond to chemokines); monocytes/macrophages follow and secrete more cytokines, amplifying the response. Recruitment depends on selectins, integrins and chemokines. IL-8 from macrophages and epithelium attracts neutrophils and promotes their phagocytosis. Pus is protein-rich fluid with dead leukocytes, mainly neutrophils.

TNF, IL-1 and IL-6

Mainly made by tissue macrophages and DCs (also mast cells, endothelium, epithelium). Most act locally (paracrine); in severe infection they enter the blood and act at a distance (endocrine).

CytokineMain sourcesKey actions
TNFMacrophages, DCs, mast cellsActivates endothelium (adhesion molecules), local inflammation; fever; at high levels septic shock
IL-1Mononuclear phagocytes, neutrophils, DCsSimilar to TNF; fever; induced by TNF
IL-6Mononuclear phagocytes, DCs, endothelium, fibroblastsHepatic acute-phase proteins; Th17 differentiation

TNF, IL-1 and IL-6 also reach the bone marrow and, with CSFs, increase neutrophil production.

Phagocytosis and killing

Phagocytosis is an active, energy-dependent engulfment of large particles. Microbes bind phagocyte receptors, including receptors for opsonins (antibody, complement, plasma lectins). The high-affinity IgG receptor FcγRI links adaptive antibody to innate effector cells. The phagosome fuses with lysosomes, isolating killing from the rest of the cell.

  • Reactive oxygen species: phagocyte NADPH oxidase reduces O₂ to superoxide, giving H₂O₂ and hydroxyl radicals (the respiratory burst).
  • Nitric oxide: combines with superoxide or H₂O₂ to form peroxynitrite.
  • Lysosomal enzymes: oxygen-independent, such as elastase and cathepsin G.
  • Myeloperoxidase: converts chloride and H₂O₂ to hypochlorite; MPO deficiency increases fungal and bacterial infections.
  • NETs: neutrophils extrude DNA, histones and granule enzymes that trap and kill bacteria and fungi.
In the clinic: chronic granulomatous disease

Defective NADPH oxidase means no respiratory burst; patients get recurrent infections with catalase-positive organisms such as S. aureus and Aspergillus.

Systemic effects and sepsis

  • Fever: TNF and IL-1 act on the hypothalamus.
  • Leukocytosis: TNF, IL-1 and IL-6 release neutrophils and monocytes from the marrow.
  • Acute-phase response: hepatocytes make CRP, serum amyloid P and fibrinogen, used clinically as markers of inflammation.

The same killing mechanisms can damage host tissue. Sepsis (usually bacterial or fungal) causes fever, tachycardia, tachypnoea, metabolic and mental disturbances. In septic shock, high TNF causes vascular collapse and disseminated intravascular coagulation; serum TNF level may predict outcome.

The antiviral response

The main innate antiviral mechanism is type I interferon (IFN-α and IFN-β), made by infected cells (and DCs) after intracellular TLRs and other sensors detect viral RNA.

  • Acts mainly paracrine: binds receptors on neighbouring uninfected cells and induces genes that block viral replication (an antiviral state).
  • On infected cells it raises MHC class I, making them easier targets for CD8⁺ CTLs.
  • Increases cytotoxicity of NK cells and CTLs and promotes Th1 differentiation.

Key terms

Zymogen: inactive protease precursor · C3 / C5 convertase: enzyme complexes cleaving C3 and C5 · MAC: C5b–C9 pore · Anaphylatoxin: C3a, C4a, C5a · MBL: mannose-binding lectin · Opsonin: coating that promotes phagocytosis · Respiratory burst: ROS production by NADPH oxidase · MPO: makes hypochlorite · NETs: neutrophil extracellular traps · Acute-phase proteins: CRP, SAP, fibrinogen · Type I IFN: IFN-α and IFN-β.
