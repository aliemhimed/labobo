Ontogeny and Function of Immune Cells

Where immune cells come from, how haematopoiesis moves from yolk sac to bone marrow, the primary lymphoid organs (bone marrow and thymus), and what each cell type does: neutrophils, monocytes and macrophages, mast cells, basophils, eosinophils, dendritic cells, lymphocytes and NK cells.

Labobo topic: Immuno Lec 2: Ontogeny and Function of Immune Cells

In 60 seconds

  • Almost all immune cells come from haematopoietic stem cells via a common myeloid or common lymphoid progenitor.
  • Haematopoiesis: yolk sac → liver (and spleen) from the 3rd–4th month → bone marrow; by puberty mainly sternum, vertebrae, iliac bones and ribs.
  • GM-CSF, G-CSF, IL-3 drive the myeloid line; IL-7 drives the lymphoid line; M-CSF drives monocytes.
  • Primary lymphoid organs: bone marrow (B cells, partly) and thymus (T cells). B cells finish maturing in the spleen.
  • Neutrophils: most abundant WBC, first to arrive, live 1–2 days in tissue. Macrophages: live long, divide at sites, dominate later stages.
  • Naive B cells carry IgM and IgD; naive T cells carry one TCR. CD27 marks memory B cells; CD45RO (180-kD CD45) marks activated and memory T cells.

Lineages and haematopoiesis

Common lymphoid progenitorCommon myeloid progenitor
B lymphocytes, T lymphocytes, NK cellsErythrocytes, megakaryocytes/platelets, mast cells, eosinophils, basophils, neutrophils, monocytes/macrophages, dendritic cells

Haematopoiesis starts in the blood islands of the yolk sac and para-aortic mesenchyme, shifts to the liver (and spleen) between the 3rd and 4th months of gestation, and finally to the bone marrow. At birth it occurs throughout the skeleton; by puberty mainly in the sternum, vertebrae, iliac bones and ribs. When the marrow fails or demand is high, the liver and spleen resume extramedullary haematopoiesis.

Precursors are driven by cytokines, many called colony-stimulating factors, made by marrow stromal cells and macrophages (and by activated T cells and macrophages when more leukocytes are needed):

  • GM-CSF, G-CSF, IL-3 → myeloid lineage (GM-CSF also drives dendritic cells).
  • IL-7 → lymphoid lineage.
  • M-CSF → monocyte–macrophage lineage.

Bone marrow

The bone marrow generates red cells, granulocytes and monocytes and is where B cells (partially) mature. It is a primary (generative, central) lymphoid organ, where lymphocytes first express antigen receptors. It also houses long-lived plasma cells (made in secondary organs, then migrating back) and some long-lived memory T cells.

B cells leave the marrow immature and complete maturation in the spleen; T cells leave the thymus fully mature. Both then populate secondary lymphoid organs (lymph nodes, spleen, mucosal tissues), which are organised to bring antigen, APCs and naive lymphocytes together.

Thymus

A bilobed organ in the anterior mediastinum that involutes after puberty. Each lobule has an outer cortex, densely packed with immature thymocytes, and a paler medulla with mature T cells, macrophages and DCs. Immature cells enter from the blood, begin maturing in the cortex and move toward the medulla; only mature naive T cells leave.

Medullary thymic epithelial cells (mTECs) present self antigens to developing T cells and eliminate self-reactive ones, a key mechanism of tolerance.

In the clinic: DiGeorge syndrome

A chromosomal deletion (22q11.2) removes genes needed for thymus development, so T cells are deficient while B cells are relatively preserved.

Phagocytes: neutrophils and macrophages

Phagocytes ingest and destroy microbes and remove damaged tissue. Their steps: recruitment, recognition and activation, phagocytosis, and killing. They also signal to other cells by contact and cytokines.

Macrophage functions

  • Phagocytose and kill microbes; clear up after infection or sterile injury.
  • Act as sentinels, secreting cytokines that start and amplify responses.
  • Serve as antigen-presenting cells.
  • Promote repair: angiogenesis and fibrosis.

They are activated by microbial and damage molecules and by opsonins (substances that coat microbes and tag them for phagocytosis). Healthy cells are protected by CD47, a "don't eat me" signal that binds the inhibitory receptor SIRPα on macrophages.

Watch out

Neutrophils dominate the first hours to day; macrophages are the dominant effectors several days into an innate response because they survive and divide.

Mast cells, basophils, eosinophils and dendritic cells

Mast cells, basophils and eosinophils all have granules of inflammatory and antimicrobial mediators, and all take part in defence against helminths and in allergic disease.

  • Mast cells: bone-marrow derived, most abundant in skin and mucosal epithelia; release potent mediators when activated.
  • Basophils: blood granulocytes resembling mast cells; express IgE receptors; normally absent from tissues and few in number, so their role is uncertain.
  • Eosinophils: granules with enzymes toxic to parasite walls (and host tissue); normally present in the mucosa of respiratory, GI and genitourinary tracts.
  • Dendritic cells: detect microbes, start innate reactions and capture microbial proteins to present to T cells, forming the bridge between innate and adaptive immunity.

Lymphocytes and NK cells

  • A mature naive B cell carries two antibody isotypes, IgM and IgD, as receptors.
  • A mature naive T cell carries one type of receptor, the TCR.
  • CD4⁺ helper T cells secrete cytokines; CD8⁺ CTLs kill infected and cancer cells; regulatory T cells inhibit responses.
StageFeatures
NaiveNever met antigen; resting in G0; small cell with dense nucleus and thin cytoplasm; live 1–3 months; survival needs antigen-receptor signals and cytokines.
EffectorEnter G1, enlarge into lymphoblasts; helper T cells, CTLs, plasmablasts and plasma cells; mostly short-lived.
MemorySurvive months to years; activated and memory T cells express the 180-kD isoform of CD45 (CD45RO); memory B cells may carry IgG, IgA or IgE after switching, and CD27 marks them in humans.

Plasma cells have an eccentric nucleus with "cartwheel" chromatin, abundant rough ER and a prominent perinuclear Golgi; one cell can secrete thousands of antibody molecules per second.

NK cells and innate lymphoid cells are lymphoid-looking innate cells without T-cell receptors. NK cells kill like CD8⁺ CTLs; ILCs secrete cytokines.

Key terms
