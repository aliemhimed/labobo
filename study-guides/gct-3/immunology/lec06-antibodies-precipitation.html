Antibodies, Antigens and Antigen–Antibody Precipitation

The structure of antibodies (Fab, Fc, V and C regions, hinge, CDRs), the isotypes and their half-lives, what makes an antigen or immunogen (haptens, epitopes, valency), affinity versus avidity, serology and titres, and how precipitation depends on the zone of equivalence, illustrated by hepatitis B serology.

Labobo topic: Immuno Lec 6: Antibodies, Antigens and Precipitation

In 60 seconds

  • Antibodies are made only by B-lineage cells, as membrane receptors or secreted protein. IgM leads the primary response; IgG dominates later.
  • Two identical heavy + two identical light chains. VH + VL form each binding site; Fab binds antigen, Fc carries effector functions; the hinge lies between CH1 and CH2. Light chains are κ or λ.
  • Isotype (IgA, IgD, IgE, IgG, IgM) is set by the heavy-chain C region. Half-lives: IgE ~2 days, IgA ~3, IgM ~4, IgG 21–28 days.
  • Immunogens provoke responses; a hapten needs a carrier. Epitope = determinant; paratope = antibody contact site.
  • Affinity = one site; avidity = total binding. Pentameric IgM has up to 10 sites, so high avidity despite low affinity.
  • Precipitation needs the zone of equivalence. Antibody excess = prozone; antigen excess = postzone. Hepatitis B "window period" = equivalence, where anti-HBc IgM is the marker.

What antibodies are

Antibodies are circulating proteins made in response to foreign structures (antigens); they mediate humoral immunity against all classes of microbes. They are synthesised only by B-lineage cells and exist as membrane-bound receptors on B cells and as secreted antibody in plasma, mucosal secretions and interstitial fluid.

When a naive B cell's membrane antibody binds antigen, the cell is activated and becomes a plasma cell secreting antibody of the same specificity. Each B-cell clone makes antibody with its own binding site. IgM is the main antibody of the primary response; later responses are dominated by other isotypes, mostly IgG. IgM is always made by cells meeting the antigen for the first time.

Antibody structure

A symmetrical, Y-shaped molecule of two identical heavy and two identical light chains, joined by disulfide bonds. Every antibody therefore has at least two antigen-binding sites.

PartWhat it is and does
V regionsAmino-terminal; one VH + one VL form each antigen-binding site
C regionsCarboxy-terminal; heavy-chain C regions mediate effector functions; light-chain C regions do not, and are not attached to membranes
FabAntigen-binding fragment; formed by light chain plus part of heavy chain
FcC-terminal fragment of heavy chains only; effector functions (Fc of IgG and IgM fixes complement)
HingeBetween CH1 and CH2 in certain isotypes; gives flexibility
CDRs (hypervariable regions)Three loops in VH and three in VL that contact antigen and set specificity
Framework regionsConserved parts of V regions supporting the CDRs

Light chains come in two classes, κ and λ. The heavy chain contributes to both Fab and Fc; the light chain only to Fab. On B cells the receptor signals through Igα and Igβ, with a co-receptor of CD19, CD21 and CD81. Membrane and secreted forms differ only at the carboxy-terminal end of the heavy chain, and have the same specificity.

Isotypes and half-lives

Classes (isotypes) are defined by differences in the heavy-chain constant regions: IgA, IgD, IgE, IgG and IgM.

IsotypeHalf-life in circulationNotable feature
IgE~2 days (much longer when bound to mast cells)Binds mast-cell Fc receptors, triggers degranulation
IgA~3 daysMucosal
IgM~4 daysPentamer; primary response; fixes complement
IgG21–28 daysOpsonises for phagocytes; crosses placenta; fixes complement

Antigens, immunogens and epitopes

An antigen is anything that can be bound specifically by an antibody or TCR. Antibodies can recognise almost any biological molecule (metabolites, sugars, lipids, hormones, nucleic acids, proteins), whereas most T cells recognise only peptides.

  • Immunogen: an antigen that actually stimulates a response. Macromolecules are effective immunogens.
  • Hapten: a small chemical that is antigenic but not immunogenic on its own; it becomes immunogenic when attached to a carrier protein or polysaccharide.
  • Epitope (determinant): the part of a macromolecule an antibody binds. Epitopes may be conformational.
  • Paratope: the part of the antibody that contacts the epitope. Idiotype: the unique structure of the binding site.
  • Polyvalency (multivalency): multiple identical determinants on one antigen.

Affinity and avidity

Binding is noncovalent and reversible. Affinity is the strength between one combining site and one epitope. Avidity is the overall strength when several sites bind a multivalent antigen, and is much greater.

Thanks to the hinge, IgG or IgE can use at most two sites; pentameric IgM can use up to ten, so a low-affinity IgM can bind a polyvalent antigen with high avidity.

Watch out

Effector functions are carried out by the Fc region but are triggered by antigen binding to the V regions.

Serology and laboratory methods

Serum is the fluid left after blood clots: it lacks coagulation factors but has all other plasma proteins, including antibodies. The study of antibodies and their reactions is serology.

The titre is found by titration: testing serial dilutions of serum until binding can no longer be detected. More dilutions mean a higher titre.

Serological reactions: precipitation, agglutination, neutralisation, complement fixation, enzyme immunoassay (ELISA) and immunofluorescence. Antibody-based laboratory methods include quantifying antigen (RIA, ELISA), identifying and purifying proteins, immunoprecipitation and immuno-affinity chromatography.

Monoclonal versus polyclonal

  • Monoclonal: identical antibodies from one B-cell clone, same V region and epitope. Used to identify cell markers, in diagnosis and to identify tumours.
  • Polyclonal: a mixture from many clones against many epitopes, produced naturally in infection or by immunising animals.

Precipitation and the zones

In precipitation, two soluble reactants (antigen and antibody) form one insoluble product, the precipitate, by building a lattice of cross-links. The lattice forms best when antigen and antibody are in optimal proportions.

ZoneWhat happens
Antibody excess (prozone)Too much antibody; little cross-linking
EquivalenceMaximum lattice and precipitate; no free antigen or antibody
Antigen excess (postzone)Too much antigen; little cross-linking

Tests must be read in the zone of equivalence. In vivo, precipitation traps pathogens in lymphoid organs but also starts immune-complex disease.

During an infection

  1. Early: antigen excess; the pathogen grows and antibody has not yet formed.
  2. Adequate response: equivalence; all antigen is complexed and neither free antigen nor free antibody is detectable.
  3. Recovery: antibody excess; more antibody than needed.
In the clinic: the hepatitis B window period

HBsAg appears first (antigen excess). In the window period (equivalence) HBsAg and anti-HBs are both undetectable because they are precipitating each other. Then anti-HBs rises (antibody excess) as the infection resolves. During the window, anti-HBc IgM is the only reliable marker of acute infection; HBcAg itself stays inside hepatocytes.
