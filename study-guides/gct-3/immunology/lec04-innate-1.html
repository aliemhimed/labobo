Innate Immune Response I: Recognition, Barriers and Cells

How the innate system recognises danger through PAMPs and DAMPs, the pattern recognition receptors that sense them (TLRs and cytosolic sensors), epithelial barriers and antimicrobial peptides, and the innate cells, with a focus on NK cells and mast cells.

Labobo topic: Immuno Lec 4: Innate Immune Response I

In 60 seconds

  • Innate immunity has limited diversity and reacts with the same vigour on repeated exposure; its two protective reactions are inflammation and antiviral defence.
  • PAMPs are shared by classes of microbes (dsRNA, unmethylated CpG DNA, LPS, lipoteichoic acid, flagellin, mannose-rich glycans). DAMPs come from damaged or necrotic cells (HSPs, urate crystals, HMGB1, extracellular ATP).
  • PRRs: TLRs (cell surface and endosomes), lectin receptors, and cytosolic NLRs, RLRs and CDSs. TLRs activate NF-κB, IRF3 and IRF7. Humans have 10 TLRs.
  • Barriers: skin and mucosae, with defensins and cathelicidin.
  • NK cells: CD56⁺CD3⁻, most CD16⁺; kill via perforin and granzymes; ADCC via CD16; inhibited by self MHC class I; secrete IFN-γ.
  • Mast cells: sentinels near vessels; histamine, proteases, leukotrienes, prostaglandins, TNF.

Components and functions

Innate defences are all present at birth, have very limited antigen diversity and attack microbes with the same vigour however often they have met them. Often they clear the infection alone; if not, they keep the host alive while the adaptive response develops.

  • Barrier epithelia block entry.
  • Tissue-resident sentinels (macrophages, mast cells, DCs) detect microbes that breach the epithelium.
  • Plasma proteins (complement and others) fight microbes in and outside the circulation.
  • Leukocytes recruited from blood (neutrophils, monocytes, NK cells) eliminate microbes and remove damaged cells.

Innate immunity also removes damaged cells, starts tissue repair, and stimulates and shapes adaptive responses to suit the type of microbe.

PAMPs and DAMPs

The innate system detects that infection is present, not the precise species. It targets structures shared by broad classes of microbes and often essential for their survival (for example viral dsRNA, bacterial LPS), so microbes cannot easily lose them.

PAMP classExamples and source
Nucleic acidsssRNA and dsRNA (viruses), unmethylated CpG DNA (bacteria, viruses)
ProteinsPilin and flagellin (bacteria)
Cell wall lipidsLPS (Gram-negative), lipoteichoic acid (Gram-positive)
CarbohydratesMannan, terminal-mannose oligosaccharides and glucans (fungi, bacteria)

DAMPs are released from damaged or dying cells, whether from infection or sterile injury (toxins, burns, trauma, ischaemia). Examples: heat shock proteins, monosodium urate crystals, HMGB1 and extracellular ATP. Molecules from healthy cells that are released only on damage are called alarmins.

Watch out

DAMPs are generally not released by cells dying by apoptosis; they come from necrosis and damage. That is why apoptosis is immunologically quiet.

Pattern recognition receptors

PRRs bind PAMPs and DAMPs and trigger signalling that leads to inflammatory and antimicrobial responses. Most cells have some; macrophages and DCs have the widest range. Soluble PRRs in blood and secretions help clear microbes by promoting phagocytosis or activating extracellular killing.

LocationReceptors
Cell surfaceSome TLRs; lectin-like receptors
EndosomesTLRs that recognise nucleic acids of ingested microbes
CytosolNLRs, RLRs and cytosolic DNA sensors (CDSs)

Toll-like receptors are an evolutionarily conserved family; humans have ten (TLR1–TLR10). Ligand binding activates the transcription factors NF-κB (inflammatory genes) and IRF3 and IRF7 (type I interferons). Cytosolic sensors also lead to inflammation or type I IFN; they matter because viral translation and assembly happen in the cytosol.

Epithelial barriers

The skin and the mucosae of the gastrointestinal, respiratory and genitourinary tracts are lined by continuous epithelia that physically block microbes. Epithelial cells and some leukocytes make antimicrobial peptides:

  • Defensins: insert into and disrupt microbial membranes.
  • Cathelicidin: kills microbes and neutralises LPS.

Intraepithelial lymphocytes (including γδ T cells) also guard the epithelium.

Natural killer cells

NK cells are large granular lymphocytes that defend mainly against viruses and intracellular bacteria. They have no clonal antigen receptors; their receptors are germline-encoded. In blood they are CD56⁺ and CD3⁻, and most are CD16⁺.

Recognition: a balance of signals

  • Inhibitory receptors (e.g. KIRs) recognise MHC class I, present on all healthy nucleated cells. Healthy cells therefore switch NK cells off.
  • Infected, stressed or tumour cells often lose MHC I ("missing self") and express ligands for activating receptors, tipping the balance toward killing.
  • CD16 (FcγRIIIA) binds IgG on antibody-coated cells, triggering antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC).

Effector functions

Granule exocytosis releases perforin, which lets granzymes enter the target cytosol and trigger apoptosis. NK cells also secrete IFN-γ, which activates macrophages to kill ingested microbes.

Mast cells

Sentinels in the skin, mucosal epithelium and connective tissue, usually beside blood vessels. When activated (including by TLR ligands) they release:

  • Preformed granule contents: histamine (vasodilation, increased permeability) and proteases that kill bacteria or inactivate toxins.
  • Newly made lipid mediators: leukotrienes and prostaglandins.
  • Cytokines such as TNF.

Their products drive acute inflammation, defend against helminths and cause allergic symptoms.

In the clinic: gout

Monosodium urate crystals are a DAMP sensed by the cytosolic NLRP3 inflammasome, releasing IL-1 and causing intense joint inflammation.

Key terms

PAMP: pathogen-associated molecular pattern · DAMP: damage-associated molecular pattern · Alarmin: molecule released only when cells are damaged · PRR: pattern recognition receptor · TLR: Toll-like receptor · NLR / RLR / CDS: cytosolic sensors · NF-κB, IRF3, IRF7: transcription factors activated by PRRs · Defensins, cathelicidin: antimicrobial peptides · KIR: killer inhibitory receptor · ADCC: antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity · Perforin / granzymes: lytic granule proteins.
