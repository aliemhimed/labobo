The Immune System: Innate and Adaptive Immunity

What the immune system responds to, how the fast innate defences and the slower, specific adaptive responses differ and help each other, the cardinal features of adaptive immunity, humoral versus cell-mediated immunity, and active versus passive protection.

Labobo topic: Immuno Lec 1: Innate and Adaptive Immunity

In 60 seconds

  • Innate immunity is present before infection, acts within hours, and uses barriers, phagocytes, DCs, NK cells, mast cells and blood proteins (complement).
  • Adaptive immunity is mediated by lymphocytes (B and T cells), takes days, and is stronger and more specialised.
  • Cardinal features of adaptive immunity: specificity and diversity (10⁷–10⁹ determinants), memory and self-tolerance.
  • Clonal selection: antigen selects a pre-existing clone that already carries a receptor for it.
  • Humoral immunity (B cells → antibodies) handles extracellular microbes and toxins; cell-mediated immunity (T cells) handles intracellular microbes.
  • Active immunity comes from the host's own response and gives memory; passive immunity is transferred antibody (placental IgG, antivenom) and gives no memory.

What the immune system responds to

Historically, immunity meant protection from infectious disease. The cells and molecules responsible make up the immune system, and their coordinated reaction to foreign substances is the immune response. Immunology is the study of these responses.

Its physiological job is defence against infectious microbes, but responses also occur against noninfectious foreign substances, products of damaged cells and malignant (tumour) cells. Any substance that can be recognised is an antigen. Sometimes even self molecules provoke a response: an autoimmune response.

Innate immunity: the early defence

Innate (native, natural) immunity is present in every normal person from birth. Its mechanisms are in place before infection, so it responds almost immediately, in the first hours or days, before adaptive responses develop.

Innate immunity fights microbes in two main ways: inflammation (recruiting phagocytes and other leukocytes that destroy microbes) and antiviral defence (blocking viral replication or killing infected cells). Many innate cells, such as DCs, some macrophages and mast cells, are tissue-resident sentinels watching for invading microbes.

Watch out: the two systems talk to each other

Innate responses provide the early danger signals that stimulate adaptive immunity. In return, adaptive responses often work by enhancing innate mechanisms (for example antibodies coating microbes for phagocytes). Neither suppresses the other.

Adaptive immunity and its cardinal features

Adaptive (specific, acquired) immunity is mediated by lymphocytes and their products. Lymphocytes carry highly diverse receptors able to recognise a vast number of antigens. The two major populations are B and T lymphocytes. Many pathogens resist innate defences, but the stronger adaptive response can eradicate them.

FeatureWhat it means
SpecificityResponses are directed at distinct antigens, and even at different parts (epitopes or determinants) of one molecule.
DiversityThe lymphocyte repertoire can distinguish about 10⁷–10⁹ determinants, because the antigen-binding sites of receptors vary between clones.
MemorySecond and later (secondary) responses are faster, larger and often qualitatively different from the primary response.
Self-toleranceNo harmful reaction to self antigens; failure leads to autoimmune disease.

Clonal selection

Clones of lymphocytes with different specificities exist before any exposure to antigen. An introduced antigen binds to (selects) the matching pre-existing clone and activates it, causing proliferation and differentiation into effector cells. Antigen does not create new receptors.

Why secondary responses are stronger

Each exposure creates long-lived memory cells. Memory cells are more numerous than the original naive cells for that antigen, and they respond more rapidly and vigorously.

How self-tolerance is maintained

By eliminating lymphocytes with receptors for some self antigens, inactivating self-reactive lymphocytes, or suppressing them with regulatory T cells. Unresponsiveness to an antigen is called tolerance.

Other properties

  • Systemic: lymphocytes circulate, so a response started in one site protects distant sites. This is why a vaccine injected in the arm protects any tissue.
  • Positive feedback amplifies the few specific lymphocytes into a large response, while control mechanisms prevent excessive activation and collateral damage.

Classes of lymphocytes

  • B lymphocytes recognise many kinds of antigen and become antibody-secreting cells.
  • Helper T lymphocytes recognise antigen on antigen-presenting cells and secrete cytokines.
  • Cytotoxic T lymphocytes recognise antigen in infected cells and kill them.
  • Regulatory T cells suppress immune responses, for example to self antigens.

Lymphocyte classes are told apart by membrane proteins named with CD numbers, which also take part in their functions.

Active and passive immunity

Active immunity is induced by the host's own response to an antigen. Individuals and lymphocytes that have never met an antigen are naive; those protected after responding are immune.

Passive immunity is conferred by transferring antibodies (or T cells) from an immunised individual. Protection is immediate but there is no memory.

  • Physiological example: maternal IgG crosses the placenta and protects the newborn for several months.
  • Medical examples: antibodies against toxins in rabies and snake bites; pooled immunoglobulin for some genetic immunodeficiencies.
Watch out

Active and passive immunity are both forms of adaptive (specific) immunity, not innate immunity. Only active immunity generates memory.

Phases of the adaptive response

  1. Antigen capture: most microbes enter through epithelia; dendritic cells, the most specialised antigen-presenting cells, carry antigens to secondary lymphoid organs (lymph nodes, spleen) and present them to naive T cells.
  2. Activation and clonal expansion: naive lymphocytes recognising the antigen proliferate.
  3. Differentiation into effector cells that eliminate the antigen (helped by macrophages and neutrophils) and memory cells. This takes a few days, which is why innate immunity must protect first.
  4. Decline: once the infection is cleared, most effector cells die; memory cells remain.

Cells communicate through cytokines, secreted proteins that regulate innate and adaptive responses. Chemokines are the subset that control cell migration. Some of the most effective drugs for immune diseases target cytokines.

How antibodies and T cells protect

Each B-cell clone carries a membrane-bound antibody as its receptor. Proteins, polysaccharides, lipids and small molecules can all elicit antibodies. B cells respond to many non-protein antigens without T-cell help, but responses to protein antigens need help from CD4⁺ T cells (hence "helper").

  • Neutralisation: antibodies block microbes from infecting cells. It is the only adaptive mechanism that stops infection before it is established, which is why vaccines aim for neutralising antibodies.
  • IgG opsonisation: phagocytes carry receptors for IgG.
  • IgG and IgM activate complement.
  • IgA is secreted across mucosal epithelia and neutralises microbes in the airway and gut lumen.
  • Maternal IgG crosses the placenta. IgG has a half-life of about 3 weeks; other classes last a few days.

T cells do not make antibodies. They recognise peptides bound to MHC molecules on other cells, so they respond to cell-associated, not soluble, antigens. Helper T cells act through cytokines and membrane molecules; CTLs produce molecules that directly kill infected cells.

In the clinic

A newborn is protected by maternal IgG for several months, which is why infants become more prone to infection as it wanes. A person bitten by a snake or exposed to rabies is given preformed antibody because there is no time to wait for an active response.

Key terms

Antigen: any substance recognised by the immune system · Epitope (determinant): the part of an antigen a lymphocyte recognises · Repertoire: all the specificities of an individual's lymphocytes · Clonal selection: antigen activates a pre-existing specific clone · Clonal expansion: proliferation of the activated clone · Effector cell: cell that eliminates antigen · Memory cell: long-lived cell for faster later responses · Self-tolerance: unresponsiveness to self · Naive: never met the antigen · Active / passive immunity: own response versus transferred antibody · Cytokine: secreted messenger protein · Chemokine: cytokine controlling migration · CD: cluster of differentiation surface marker.
