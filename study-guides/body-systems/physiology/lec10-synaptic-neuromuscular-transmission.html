Synaptic and neuromuscular transmission

Chemical and electrical synapses; how calcium releases transmitter; ionotropic and G-protein receptors; the main neurotransmitters; EPSPs, IPSPs and summation; synaptic fatigue. Then the neuromuscular junction: acetylcholine release, the nicotinic receptor, the end plate potential, and the drugs and disease that act there. Written from Guyton & Hall (no deck for this week).

Labobo topic: Lec 10: Synaptic & Neuromuscular Transmission

In 60 seconds

  • Most synapses are chemical and conduct one way; electrical synapses (gap junctions) are two-way, e.g. heart and visceral smooth muscle.
  • The action potential opens presynaptic voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels; Ca²⁺ entry triggers exocytosis of vesicles.
  • Receptors: ionotropic (open a channel directly, fast) or metabotropic (G protein, second messenger, slow and long).
  • Opening cation (Na⁺) channels excites (EPSP); opening Cl⁻ or K⁺ channels inhibits (IPSP).
  • One terminal gives only 0.5–1 mV: firing needs spatial and temporal summation. The action potential starts at the axon initial segment.
  • NMJ: ACh binds two α subunits of the nicotinic receptor → Na⁺ in → end plate potential (50–75 mV, about 3× what is needed) → muscle action potential. Acetylcholinesterase ends it in milliseconds.
  • Myasthenia gravis: antibodies against ACh receptors; improved by neostigmine.

WhereMost CNS synapsesVisceral smooth muscle, cardiac muscle

A synapse can block an impulse, turn it into a repeated discharge or combine it with others. One motor neuron receives 10,000 to 200,000 terminals (boutons), 80–95% on dendrites and 5–20% on the soma. The synaptic cleft is about 200–300 Å wide.

Transmitter release

  1. The action potential depolarizes the presynaptic terminal.
  2. Voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open and Ca²⁺ enters.
  3. Ca²⁺ binds release sites; vesicles fuse and release transmitter by exocytosis. The amount released is directly related to the Ca²⁺ that enters.
  4. Transmitter diffuses across the cleft and binds postsynaptic receptors.

The terminal also holds mitochondria to supply ATP for making transmitter.

Postsynaptic receptors

IonotropicMetabotropic
MechanismThe receptor is an ion channelActivates a G protein (second messenger)
SpeedOpens in a fraction of a millisecond, closes as fastSlow, prolonged (seconds to months, e.g. memory)
  • Cation channels (mostly Na⁺) are lined with negative charges that repel anions: opening them excites.
  • Anion channels (mostly Cl⁻): opening them inhibits.
  • G protein: α, β, γ subunits with GDP at rest. The activated receptor makes α swap GDP for GTP and separate. It can (1) open an ion channel such as a K⁺ channel, (2) activate cAMP or cGMP, (3) activate intracellular enzymes, or (4) activate gene transcription. Hydrolysis of GTP to GDP switches it off.

Neurotransmitters

More than 50 are known, in two groups. Small-molecule transmitters act fast, are made in the terminal and their vesicles are recycled in seconds to minutes. Neuropeptides are made on ribosomes in the cell body, carried down the axon, act slowly and last hours or longer, and are more than 1000× as potent.

Acetylcholine is made from acetyl-CoA and choline by choline acetyltransferase, split by acetylcholinesterase into acetate and choline, and the choline is taken back up.

EPSP, IPSP and summation

  • The neuron soma rests at about −65 mV, so its potential can be pushed either way.
  • EPSP: opening Na⁺ channels raises the potential, e.g. from −65 to −45 mV (+20 mV). One terminal gives only 0.5–1 mV, so about 40–80 terminals must fire together.
  • IPSP: opening Cl⁻ channels (ECl about −70 mV) or K⁺ channels makes the inside more negative (hyperpolarization).
  • The action potential starts at the initial segment of the axon, which has about 7× the density of voltage-gated Na⁺ channels of the soma.
  • Spatial summation: many terminals at once. Temporal summation: rapid repeated firing; each opening lasts 1–2 ms but the potential lasts up to 15 ms. EPSPs and IPSPs add algebraically; a subthreshold sum leaves the neuron facilitated.
  • Presynaptic inhibition: GABA on the presynaptic terminal opens anion channels and reduces transmitter release (common in sensory pathways).
  • Dendrites have few Na⁺ channels and conduct with decrement, so synapses near the soma, and inhibitory synapses on the axon hillock, have the strongest effect.

Fatigue, pH, hypoxia and drugs

  • Synaptic fatigue: rapid repetitive firing depletes transmitter within seconds to minutes; this protects against overexcitation, for example ending an epileptic seizure.
  • Alkalosis raises excitability (pH 7.8–8.0 can cause seizures); acidosis depresses it (below pH 7.0 can cause coma).
  • Hypoxia: consciousness is lost within 3–7 seconds of stopping cerebral blood flow.
  • Caffeine, theophylline, theobromine raise excitability; strychnine blocks inhibitory glycine in the cord, causing convulsions; most anaesthetics raise the threshold.

The neuromuscular junction

Skeletal muscle fibres are supplied by large myelinated fibres from anterior horn motor neurons. Each nerve fibre branches to supply from 3 to several hundred muscle fibres; each muscle fibre has one junction, near its middle, so the action potential spreads both ways.

  • Motor end plate: the terminals lie in a synaptic gutter, covered by Schwann cells; the cleft is 20–30 nm; subneural clefts enlarge the surface.
  • The terminal holds about 300,000 vesicles, each with about 10,000 ACh molecules; acetylcholinesterase sits in the cleft.
  1. The action potential opens voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels beside the dense bars of the terminal.
  2. Ca²⁺ activates Ca²⁺-calmodulin kinase, which phosphorylates synapsin, freeing vesicles to dock and release ACh; about 125 vesicles per impulse.
  3. ACh binds the nicotinic receptor: five subunits (adult 2α, β, δ, ε; fetal γ instead of ε). The channel opens only when two ACh molecules bind the two α subunits.
  4. The channel passes cations, mainly Na⁺ inward, because the inside is negative (−80 to −90 mV); negative charges at its mouth keep Cl⁻ out.
  5. The end plate potential rises 50–75 mV; a rise of 20–30 mV is enough to fire a muscle action potential. The safety factor is about 3.
  6. Acetylcholinesterase destroys ACh within a few milliseconds, preventing re-excitation; choline is reabsorbed and vesicles are recycled (coated pits, about 20 s).

Stimulating faster than about 100 times per second for several minutes depletes the vesicles: fatigue of the junction.

In the clinic

Myasthenia gravis (about 1 in 20,000): autoantibodies against ACh receptors make end plate potentials too weak, so muscles weaken with use; severe cases die of respiratory failure. Neostigmine or another anticholinesterase improves strength within minutes, for hours.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • The trigger for release is Ca²⁺ entry, not Na⁺.
  • The nicotinic channel needs two ACh molecules on the α subunits.
  • Neuron action potentials start at the axon initial segment, not on the dendrites.
  • Strychnine blocks glycine (inhibition) and so causes convulsions.
  • Myasthenia is antibodies to the receptor, not to the transmitter.

Key terms

Synapse: junction between excitable cells · Gap junction: electrical synapse · Synaptic cleft: gap between pre- and postsynaptic membranes · Exocytosis: vesicle release · Ionotropic receptor: ligand-gated channel · Metabotropic receptor: G-protein receptor · EPSP / IPSP: excitatory / inhibitory postsynaptic potential · Summation: adding potentials · Facilitation: neuron near threshold · Presynaptic inhibition: reduced release from a terminal · Motor end plate: muscle side of the NMJ · Subneural clefts: folds of the end plate · Nicotinic receptor: ACh-gated cation channel · End plate potential: local depolarization of the end plate · Acetylcholinesterase: enzyme that splits ACh · Safety factor: EPP size above what is needed.
