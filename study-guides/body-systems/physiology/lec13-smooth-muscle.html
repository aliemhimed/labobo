Excitation and contraction of smooth muscle

Multi-unit and unitary smooth muscle; dense bodies and side-polar myosin; why smooth muscle contracts slowly, cheaply and for a long time (latch, stress-relaxation); the calcium-calmodulin-MLCK pathway and myosin phosphatase; autonomic innervation; spike, plateau and slow-wave potentials; and contraction without action potentials. Written from Guyton & Hall chapter 8 (no deck for this week).

Labobo topic: Lec 13: Smooth Muscle

In 60 seconds

  • Multi-unit: separate fibres, each nerve-controlled (iris, ciliary muscle, piloerectors). Unitary: sheets joined by gap junctions that contract together (gut, ureters, uterus, vessels).
  • No troponin, no striations: actin attaches to dense bodies (like Z discs); side-polar myosin lets it shorten up to 80%.
  • Slow cross-bridge cycling → slow onset, long contraction, 1/10 to 1/300 of the energy, and higher maximum force (4–6 kg/cm²). The latch holds force cheaply.
  • Ca²⁺ (mostly from outside the cell) binds calmodulin → activates myosin light chain kinase → phosphorylates myosin → cycling. Myosin phosphatase relaxes it.
  • Resting potential −50 to −60 mV; action potentials are carried mainly by Ca²⁺ channels; gut muscle has slow waves.
  • Nerves form diffuse junctions with varicosities releasing ACh or noradrenaline; the receptor decides excitation or inhibition. About half of contractions start without any action potential.

Multi-unit and unitary

Smooth muscle fibres are small: 1–5 µm wide and 20–500 µm long.

The contractile apparatus

  • Smooth muscle has actin and myosin but no troponin and no striations.
  • Actin filaments attach to dense bodies, the equivalent of Z discs; some lie on the membrane and are bonded to those of neighbouring cells, so force passes from cell to cell.
  • There are 5–10 times as many actin as myosin filaments; myosin filaments are more than twice as thick.
  • Myosin has side-polar cross-bridges: bridges on one side hinge one way and those on the other side the opposite way, so one myosin filament pulls actin in both directions. Smooth muscle can shorten by up to 80% of its length (skeletal less than 30%).

  • Latch mechanism: after full contraction, excitation can fall far below its starting level while force is kept, using as little as 1/300 of the energy of skeletal muscle. Possibly, when both kinase and phosphatase activity fall, heads stay attached longer.
  • Stress-relaxation: when a hollow organ such as the bladder is suddenly stretched, its pressure jumps and then falls back almost to the original level within about 15–60 s. Reverse stress-relaxation follows a sudden fall in volume. This keeps pressure nearly constant despite big volume changes.

Calcium regulation

  1. A stimulus (nerve, hormone, stretch, chemical change) raises cytosolic Ca²⁺, mostly by influx from the extracellular fluid, some from the small SR.
  2. Ca²⁺ binds calmodulin (instead of troponin).
  3. Ca²⁺-calmodulin activates myosin light chain kinase (MLCK).
  4. MLCK phosphorylates the regulatory light chain of the myosin head; only then can the head bind actin and cycle: contraction.
  5. Relaxation: a slow ATP-driven Ca²⁺ pump removes Ca²⁺, and myosin phosphatase removes the phosphate, stopping cycling. Phosphatase activity largely sets the relaxation time.
  • Extracellular Ca²⁺ is above 10⁻³ M, intracellular below 10⁻⁷ M. Diffusion in takes 200–300 ms, about 50× the latent period of skeletal muscle.
  • Caveolae, small membrane pits beside the SR, are a rudimentary version of T tubules.
  • Because smooth muscle depends on outside Ca²⁺, its contraction usually stops when extracellular Ca²⁺ falls to 1/3 to 1/10 of normal; skeletal muscle is hardly affected.

Nerves and transmitters

  • There is no organised neuromuscular junction. Autonomic fibres branch over sheets of muscle and form diffuse junctions, releasing transmitter from varicosities a few nanometres to a few micrometres from the fibres; often only the outer layer is reached and excitation spreads inward.
  • Contact junctions (20–30 nm gap) exist in some multi-unit muscle and give faster responses.
  • Transmitters: acetylcholine and noradrenaline, never from the same fibre. Each excites some smooth muscles and inhibits others; where one excites, the other usually inhibits. The receptor decides the effect.

Membrane potentials

  • Resting potential about −50 to −60 mV, about 30 mV less negative than skeletal muscle.
  • Spike potentials (10–50 ms) in most unitary muscle, triggered by electrical stimulation, hormones, transmitters, stretch or spontaneously.
  • Action potentials with a plateau (repolarization delayed several hundred ms up to about 1 s) in the ureter, uterus and some vascular muscle; they explain prolonged contraction.
  • The action potential is carried mainly by voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels (few Na⁺ channels). They open and close slowly, giving the plateau, and the Ca²⁺ that enters also starts contraction.
  • Slow waves: spontaneous rhythmic swings of membrane potential in self-excitatory muscle such as the gut. They are not action potentials, but when a peak rises from about −60 to about −35 mV (threshold) action potentials fire, giving rhythmic contraction: pacemaker waves.
  • Stretch of visceral muscle fires action potentials, so an overfilled gut contracts and starts peristalsis.
  • In multi-unit muscle the fibres are too small for action potentials; the local junctional potential spreads electrotonically and is enough (in unitary muscle 30–40 fibres must depolarize together to fire a propagated action potential).

Contraction without action potentials

About half of all smooth muscle contraction probably starts without any action potential.

  • Local tissue factors relax arterioles, metarterioles and precapillary sphincters: lack of O₂, excess CO₂, more H⁺, adenosine, lactic acid, more K⁺, nitric oxide, higher temperature, and lower blood pressure (less stretch).
  • Hormones: noradrenaline, adrenaline, angiotensin II, endothelin, vasopressin, oxytocin, serotonin, histamine. Each contracts muscle with excitatory receptors and relaxes muscle with inhibitory receptors.
  • Excitation: receptors open Na⁺ or Ca²⁺ channels. Inhibition: closing them or opening K⁺ channels (hyperpolarization). Some act through second messengers: Ca²⁺ release from the SR (contraction), or cAMP/cGMP that pump Ca²⁺ away (relaxation).
  • The same hormone can do opposite things: noradrenaline relaxes gut smooth muscle but contracts vascular smooth muscle.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • Smooth muscle has no troponin; regulation is on myosin.
  • Most activating Ca²⁺ comes from outside the cell.
  • Smooth muscle is stronger per area than skeletal, though slower.
  • The smooth muscle action potential depends mainly on Ca²⁺, not Na⁺.
  • The iris and ciliary muscle are multi-unit.

Key terms

Multi-unit smooth muscle: separate, nerve-driven fibres · Unitary (visceral) smooth muscle: gap-junction sheets · Dense body: actin anchor · Side-polar myosin: pulls both ways · Calmodulin: Ca²⁺-binding regulator · MLCK: myosin light chain kinase · Myosin phosphatase: relaxing enzyme · Latch: cheap sustained force · Stress-relaxation: pressure falls back after stretch · Caveolae: membrane pits near the SR · Varicosity: transmitter-releasing swelling · Diffuse junction: loose nerve-muscle contact · Plateau potential: prolonged action potential · Slow wave: rhythmic pacemaker oscillation.
