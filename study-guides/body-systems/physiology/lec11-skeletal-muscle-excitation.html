Excitation of skeletal muscle

How a nerve impulse becomes a muscle contraction: the neuromuscular junction in brief, the muscle action potential and how it differs from a nerve's, the T tubule–sarcoplasmic reticulum system, the DHP and ryanodine receptors that release calcium, the calcium pulse and its removal by SERCA, and malignant hyperthermia. Written from Guyton & Hall chapter 7 (no deck for this week).

Labobo topic: Lec 11: Excitation of Skeletal Muscle

In 60 seconds

  • ACh at the end plate fires a muscle action potential that spreads both ways from the middle of the fibre.
  • Muscle vs large nerve: resting potential −80 to −90 mV; action potential 1–5 ms (about 5× longer); conduction 3–5 m/s (about 1/13 as fast).
  • T tubules carry the action potential deep into the fibre; each lies between two terminal cisternae of the SR: a triad. Mammalian skeletal muscle has two T tubules per sarcomere, at the A-I junctions.
  • DHP receptors (voltage sensors) in the T tubule open ryanodine receptor channels in the SR → Ca²⁺ floods the sarcoplasm.
  • Cytosolic Ca²⁺ rises from <10⁻⁷ M to about 2 × 10⁻⁴ M (500-fold) for about 1/20 s; SERCA pumps it back (ATP), and calsequestrin stores it.
  • Malignant hyperthermia: RyR or DHP mutations + halothane or succinylcholine → uncontrolled Ca²⁺ release; treat with dantrolene and cooling.

From nerve to muscle

Covered fully in lecture 10; the points that start excitation:

  1. A large myelinated fibre from an anterior horn motor neuron branches to 3 to several hundred muscle fibres; each fibre has one junction near its middle.
  2. The nerve action potential opens Ca²⁺ channels; about 125 vesicles of ACh are released.
  3. ACh opens the nicotinic channels (two ACh on two α subunits); Na⁺ enters and the end plate potential rises 50–75 mV, about 3× the 20–30 mV needed.
  4. Neighbouring voltage-gated Na⁺ channels fire the muscle action potential, which spreads toward both ends of the fibre. Acetylcholinesterase removes ACh within milliseconds.

T tubules and sarcoplasmic reticulum

A muscle fibre is too wide for a surface action potential to reach the deep myofibrils. The transverse (T) tubules solve this.

  • T tubules start at the cell membrane and run transversely through the fibre, branching among all the myofibrils. They are internal extensions of the cell membrane, open to the outside and filled with extracellular fluid.
  • The sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) has large terminal cisternae pressed against the T tubules and longitudinal tubules around the myofibrils. It stores Ca²⁺.
  • A T tubule with the two cisternae on either side is a triad.
  • Mammalian skeletal muscle has two T tubules per sarcomere, at the junctions of the A and I bands; frog muscle and the heart have one, at the Z disc.

Excitation-contraction coupling

  1. The action potential travels along the sarcolemma and down the T tubules.
  2. Dihydropyridine (DHP) receptors, voltage sensors in the T-tubule membrane, change shape.
  3. They open the linked ryanodine receptor (RyR) calcium release channels in the SR cisternae.
  4. Ca²⁺ pours into the sarcoplasm for a few milliseconds and binds troponin C, lifting the troponin-tropomyosin block on actin: contraction (lecture 12).
  5. During repolarization the DHP receptors close the RyR channels.
  6. The SERCA pump (SR Ca²⁺-ATPase), always active, pumps Ca²⁺ back into the SR using ATP, concentrating it about 10,000-fold. Inside, calsequestrin binds up to 40 Ca²⁺ per molecule. The muscle relaxes.

The calcium pulse

  • At rest, sarcoplasmic Ca²⁺ is below 10⁻⁷ M: too low to remove the troponin-tropomyosin inhibition, so the muscle is relaxed.
  • Full excitation raises it to about 2 × 10⁻⁴ M, a 500-fold rise and about 10× the level needed for maximal contraction.
  • In a usual skeletal fibre the pulse lasts about 1/20 s (cardiac muscle about 1/3 s). Sustained contraction therefore needs a series of action potentials and Ca²⁺ pulses.

Malignant hyperthermia

In the clinic

In susceptible people (at least six mutations, especially in the RyR or DHP receptor genes), anaesthetic gases such as halothane and isoflurane, or succinylcholine, cause uncontrolled Ca²⁺ release from the SR. The result is sustained contraction, a very high metabolic rate, heat, acidosis and ATP depletion: muscle rigidity, high fever and tachycardia; severe cases bring rhabdomyolysis and high plasma K⁺. Treatment: rapid cooling and dantrolene, which blocks ryanodine receptors.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • In skeletal muscle the Ca²⁺ for contraction comes from the SR, not from outside the cell.
  • T tubules contain extracellular fluid.
  • Relaxation needs ATP (for SERCA).
  • In mammals the T tubules lie at the A-I junctions, not at the Z disc.
  • Dantrolene acts on the ryanodine receptor.

Key terms

Sarcolemma: muscle cell membrane · T tubule: inward extension of the sarcolemma · Sarcoplasmic reticulum: Ca²⁺ store · Terminal cisterna: SR chamber beside a T tubule · Triad: T tubule + two cisternae · Excitation-contraction coupling: from action potential to contraction · DHP receptor: voltage sensor · Ryanodine receptor: SR Ca²⁺ release channel · SERCA: SR Ca²⁺-ATPase · Calsequestrin: Ca²⁺-binding protein in the SR · Calcium pulse: brief rise of sarcoplasmic Ca²⁺ · Malignant hyperthermia: drug-triggered uncontrolled Ca²⁺ release · Dantrolene: RyR blocker.
