Organization and cells of the nervous system

How the nervous system is divided by structure and function; the neuron and its structural and functional types; the glial cells of the CNS and PNS and how each system makes myelin; the three levels of CNS function; and the diseases that follow when glia fail.

Labobo topic: Lec 8: Organization of the Nervous System

In 60 seconds

  • Structure: CNS (brain, spinal cord) and PNS (nerves, ganglia). Function: afferent (sensory) → integration → efferent (motor).
  • The neuron is the functional unit: dendrites and cell body receive, the single axon sends.
  • Structural types: multipolar (most common; motor neurons, interneurons), bipolar (retina, olfactory, vestibulocochlear), pseudounipolar (dorsal root ganglion sensory neurons).
  • CNS glia: astrocytes (blood-brain barrier), oligodendrocytes (CNS myelin), microglia (phagocytes), ependymal cells (CSF).
  • PNS glia: Schwann cells (PNS myelin, regeneration), satellite cells (support ganglion cell bodies).
  • Myelin: oligodendrocytes in the CNS, Schwann cells in the PNS. MS attacks CNS myelin; Guillain-Barré attacks PNS myelin.

Learning objectives

Syllabus week X: organization and cells of the nervous system. Guyton & Hall, chapter 46. By the end you can:

The neuron

The basic functional unit of the nervous system. It receives, integrates and transmits signals.

  • Dendrites and the cell body receive input through synapses: from a few hundred to as many as 200,000 synapses on one neuron (Guyton).
  • A single axon carries the output; it may branch many times and ends in synaptic terminals.
  • Most synapses pass the signal one way: from the axon of one neuron to the dendrites or cell body of the next.
  • The CNS contains about 80–100 billion neurons.

Types of neuron

By structure

TypeShapeWhere
MultipolarMany dendrites, one axonThe most common: motor neurons and interneurons
BipolarOne dendrite, one axonRetina, olfactory epithelium, vestibulocochlear pathways
PseudounipolarOne short process that splits into peripheral and central branchesSensory neurons of the dorsal root ganglia

By function

  • Sensory (afferent): carry information from receptors to the CNS.
  • Motor (efferent): carry commands from the CNS to effectors.
  • Interneurons: integrate and process within the CNS.

Neuroglia

Glia are the supporting cells of the nervous system: they support, protect and nourish nervous tissue.

CellWhereFunction
AstrocyteCNSStructural support; forms part of the blood-brain barrier (perivascular feet on capillaries); regulates ions and interstitial fluid; helps neuronal development; fills the space of dying neurons
OligodendrocyteCNSMyelinates CNS axons, so action potentials travel faster
MicrogliaCNSImmune defence and phagocytosis: move through the CNS engulfing infectious agents and debris
Ependymal cellCNSLines the ventricles and central canal; produces and circulates CSF
Schwann cellPNSMyelinates PNS axons; aids axon regeneration

Sensory, motor and integrative functions

From Guyton chapter 46, which fills in the deck's headings.

  • Sensory part: receptors (sight, hearing, touch and others) start most nervous activity.
  • Motor part: the effectors are skeletal muscle, smooth muscle of internal organs, and exocrine and endocrine glands.
  • Integration: more than 99% of sensory information is discarded as unimportant; the rest is channelled to the right motor region. Synapses decide where signals go and can be facilitated or inhibited. Memory is stored by facilitating synapses, mostly in the cerebral cortex.
LevelExamples of what it does
Spinal cordWalking movements, withdrawal reflexes, reflexes that stiffen the legs against gravity, local reflexes of vessels, gut and bladder
Lower brain (subcortical)Medulla and pons: arterial pressure and respiration; cerebellum and reticular substance: equilibrium; feeding reflexes
Higher brain (cortical)A vast memory store; makes the lower centres' functions precise; never works alone

In the clinic

Multiple sclerosis: loss of CNS myelin (oligodendrocytes). Guillain-Barré syndrome: loss of PNS myelin (Schwann cells), causing ascending weakness. Gliomas: tumours of glial cells, the commonest primary brain tumours.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • Ependymal cells make CSF; astrocytes form the blood-brain barrier.
  • The commonest neuron is multipolar.
  • Dorsal root ganglion neurons are pseudounipolar, not bipolar.
  • Glands are effectors too, not only muscles.

Key terms

CNS: brain and spinal cord · PNS: nerves and ganglia · Ganglion: cluster of neuron cell bodies outside the CNS · Afferent / efferent: toward / away from the CNS · Dendrite: receiving process · Axon: single output process · Synapse: junction between neurons · Interneuron: neuron within the CNS · Neuroglia: supporting cells · Myelin: insulating sheath speeding conduction · Blood-brain barrier: barrier between blood and brain tissue · CSF: cerebrospinal fluid · Effector: muscle or gland that responds · Glioma: tumour of glial cells.
