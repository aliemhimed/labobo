Muscle metabolism

Where a muscle's energy comes from and where it goes: ATP and its three back-up systems (phosphocreatine, glycogen-lactic acid, aerobic), how fast and how long each works, which sports rely on which, efficiency and heat, fatigue, oxygen debt, recovery of glycogen, and fuel use in long events. Written from Guyton & Hall chapters 6 and 85 (no deck for this week).

Labobo topic: Lec 15: Muscle Metabolism

In 60 seconds

  • Energy goes to the cross-bridge cycle, the Ca²⁺ pump (SR) and the Na⁺-K⁺ pump. More work splits more ATP (Fenn effect).
  • Stored ATP (about 4 mM) lasts only seconds; it is remade by three systems.
  • Phosphagen (ATP + phosphocreatine): fastest (4 mol ATP/min), lasts about 8–10 s: 100 m sprint, jumps, lifting.
  • Glycogen-lactic acid (anaerobic glycolysis): 2.5 mol/min, about 1.3–1.6 min more: 400 m, 100 m swim.
  • Aerobic (oxidation of carbohydrate, fat, protein): slowest (1 mol/min) but unlimited; over 95% of energy for sustained work: marathon.
  • Efficiency is under 25%; the rest is heat. Fatigue tracks glycogen depletion.
  • Oxygen debt about 11.5 L (alactacid 3.5 L + lactic acid 8 L); muscle glycogen needs about 2 days on a high-carbohydrate diet to recover.

Where the energy goes

  • Most of the energy drives the walk-along cross-bridge mechanism.
  • Smaller amounts pump Ca²⁺ back into the SR after contraction and pump Na⁺ and K⁺ to keep the membrane ready for action potentials.
  • Work W = L × D (load × distance). The more work, the more ATP is split (Fenn effect).

ATP and phosphocreatine

  • ATP = adenosine + three phosphates; each of the last two bonds stores about 7300 calories per mole. Splitting gives ADP, then AMP.
  • Muscle holds about 4 mM ATP: enough for only a few seconds of maximal contraction. ADP is rephosphorylated within a fraction of a second.
  • Phosphocreatine has an even higher-energy bond (about 10,300 cal/mol) and instantly hands its phosphate to ADP. Muscle has several times as much phosphocreatine as ATP.
Watch out

Guyton gives different figures in two chapters: ATP alone lasts "1–2 s" (ch. 6) or "about 3 s" (ch. 85); ATP + phosphocreatine last "5–8 s" (ch. 6) or "8–10 s" (ch. 85); phosphocreatine is "about 5×" or "2–4×" the ATP. Remember the order of magnitude: seconds for ATP, under about 10 s for the phosphagen system.

Fibre types match the systems (lecture 12): fast (type II) fibres are rich in glycolytic enzymes; slow (type I) fibres are rich in mitochondria and myoglobin.

Efficiency, heat and fatigue

  • Less than 25% of the energy in food becomes work; the rest becomes heat. About half is lost making ATP, and only 40–45% of ATP's energy becomes work.
  • Efficiency is highest at a moderate speed, about 30% of maximum. Very slow or isometric contraction does almost no external work (efficiency near zero); very fast contraction wastes energy in friction.
  • Fatigue rises almost in step with muscle glycogen depletion. Without blood flow (no O₂), fatigue is almost complete in 1–2 minutes. Lactic acid contributes to fatigue and the burning feeling.
  • Without ATP, cross-bridges cannot detach: rigor mortis.

Oxygen debt and recovery

  • The body stores about 2 L of O₂ (lung air, body fluids, haemoglobin, myoglobin), used up within about a minute of heavy exercise.
  • After exercise, extra O₂ is breathed in to rebuild the systems: the oxygen debt, about 11.5 L. The early alactacid part (about 3.5 L) restores phosphagen and stored O₂; the later lactic acid part (about 8 L) removes lactic acid.
  • Phosphocreatine and ATP are rebuilt using glycolytic energy; everything is rebuilt by aerobic metabolism. Lactic acid is partly oxidised and mostly turned back into glucose in the liver, then into glycogen.
  • Muscle glycogen takes days to recover: about 2 days on a high-carbohydrate diet; very little in 5 days on a high-fat, high-protein diet or no food.
In practice

Time to exhaustion at marathon pace depends on stored glycogen: about 240 min after a high-carbohydrate diet, 120 min after a mixed diet, 85 min after a high-fat diet. Athletes eat carbohydrate and avoid exhausting exercise in the 48 hours before an event.

Fuels in long exercise

  • Early in exercise muscle burns mainly carbohydrate; in events lasting more than 4–5 hours glycogen is almost used up.
  • The liver stores almost as much glycogen as muscle and releases it as glucose; glucose drinks can supply 30–40% of the energy in a marathon.
  • After the first 3–4 hours, fat supplies more than half of the energy; amino acids contribute much less.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • Phosphocreatine's bond holds more energy than ATP's.
  • Glycolysis is faster than oxidation but runs out sooner.
  • A 100 m sprint is almost all phosphagen; a 400 m race mainly glycogen-lactic.
  • Isometric contraction has near-zero efficiency because it does no external work.
  • Fat becomes the main fuel only after several hours.

Key terms

ATP: immediate energy source · Phosphocreatine: rapid phosphate donor · Phosphagen system: ATP + phosphocreatine · Glycolysis: anaerobic splitting of glucose to pyruvic acid · Lactic acid: product of glycolysis without O₂ · Aerobic system: oxidation in mitochondria · Fenn effect: more work, more ATP used · Efficiency: work out ÷ energy in · Fatigue: falling force with use · Oxygen debt: extra O₂ after exercise · Alactacid / lactic acid debt: early / late parts of the debt · Myoglobin: muscle O₂ store · Glycogen loading: high-carbohydrate diet before endurance events.
