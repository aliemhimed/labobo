Membrane potentials and action potentials

Why the inside of a cell is negative: ion gradients, equilibrium potentials and the Nernst equation, leak channels and the Na⁺-K⁺ pump. Then the action potential: threshold, the voltage-gated Na⁺ and K⁺ channels, refractory periods, saltatory conduction in myelinated fibres, and how calcium and local anaesthetics change excitability.

Labobo topic: Lec 9: Membrane & Action Potentials

In 60 seconds

  • Na⁺ is high outside (142 mEq/L) and low inside (10); K⁺ is high inside (140) and low outside (4).
  • Equilibrium potential: the voltage at which an ion has no net diffusion. Nernst: E = ±61 log(Cin/Cout). EK ≈ −94 mV, ENa ≈ +61 mV.
  • Resting potential: about −70 mV in neurons, −90 mV in large fibres and skeletal muscle, set mainly by K⁺ leak; the pump (3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in) adds about −4 mV.
  • Threshold about −55 mV: voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open (positive feedback) → depolarization and overshoot; they then inactivate, and slow K⁺ channels repolarize.
  • All-or-nothing; the absolute refractory period exists because inactivated Na⁺ channels cannot reopen.
  • Myelin + nodes of Ranvier = saltatory conduction: 5–50× faster, far less energy.
  • Low Ca²⁺ → hyperexcitable (tetany). Local anaesthetics block Na⁺ channels.

Ion gradients

  • Outside the cell: high Na⁺, Cl⁻ and Ca²⁺. Inside: high K⁺ and negatively charged proteins and phosphates that cannot leave.
  • The electrochemical gradient is the sum of the chemical (concentration) gradient and the electrical gradient; together they decide which way an ion diffuses.

Equilibrium potential and Nernst

The equilibrium potential of an ion is the membrane potential at which there is no net diffusion of that ion: the chemical force one way is exactly balanced by the electrical force the other way. It is also called the reversal potential.

Nernst equation (univalent ion, 37 °C): E (mV) = ±61 × log(Cin / Cout); the sign is negative for a positive ion. For a divalent ion such as Ca²⁺ the factor halves (the slide uses 61.5 and 30.75).

The driving force on an ion is Vm − Eion; when they are equal there is no net flow.

The resting membrane potential

  1. K⁺ leak: at rest the membrane is far more permeable to K⁺ (leak channels about 100× more permeable to K⁺ than Na⁺). K⁺ diffusion alone gives about −94 mV.
  2. Na⁺ leak: a small inward Na⁺ leak pulls the potential slightly positive: K⁺ and Na⁺ together give about −86 mV.
  3. Na⁺-K⁺ pump: electrogenic, 3 Na⁺ out for 2 K⁺ in, adding about −4 mV: net about −90 mV in large fibres.

Each ion contributes in proportion to its permeability (the Goldman equation), which is why K⁺ dominates. The slide's own weighted estimate also reaches −86 mV. Typical values: neurons −60 to −70 mV, skeletal muscle −85 to −95, smooth muscle −50 to −60, cardiac muscle −80 to −90, erythrocytes −8 to −12 mV. Only a tiny fraction of the ions inside need to move to create this potential.

Ion channels

ChannelControlled byExample
Ungated (leak)Always openK⁺ leak channels: many in nerve and muscle, so K⁺ conductance at rest is high
Voltage-gatedThe membrane potentialNa⁺ and K⁺ channels of the action potential; with the gate closed Na⁺ conductance is low
Ligand-gatedA chemical binding to a receptorAcetylcholine receptor at the neuromuscular junction
  • Voltage-gated Na⁺ channel: an activation gate (outside) opens at about −55 mV, raising Na⁺ permeability 500 to 5000-fold; an inactivation gate (inside) closes a few ten-thousandths of a second later and will not reopen until the membrane is back near rest.
  • Voltage-gated K⁺ channel: closed at rest, opens slowly, just as Na⁺ channels inactivate; closes again after repolarization.
  • Hodgkin and Huxley studied them with the voltage clamp on the squid giant axon. Tetrodotoxin blocks Na⁺ channels; tetraethylammonium blocks K⁺ channels.

The action potential

  1. Resting: polarized, about −70 mV.
  2. Threshold (about −55 mV): Na⁺ channels open; Na⁺ entry depolarizes further, opening more channels (positive feedback).
  3. Depolarization: the potential becomes less negative and, in large fibres, overshoots zero toward ENa.
  4. Repolarization: once the inside is positive, Na⁺ channels inactivate, Na⁺ conductance falls and K⁺ conductance rises: the potential returns toward rest.
  5. Undershoot: K⁺ channels close slowly, so the potential dips briefly below rest toward EK (about −90 mV), then K⁺ channels close.

At rest K⁺ conductance is 50–100× Na⁺ conductance. At the start of the action potential Na⁺ conductance rises up to 5000-fold; K⁺ conductance rises only about 30-fold, and later.

Watch out

Slide 10 defines repolarization as the potential "becoming more negative". More exactly, repolarization is the return toward the resting value; going below rest is hyperpolarization (the undershoot).

Threshold, propagation, refractory periods

  • A sudden rise of 15–30 mV is needed to reach threshold (from −70 to about −55 mV). Smaller stimuli cause only local (subthreshold) potentials.
  • All-or-nothing: once started, an action potential spreads over the whole membrane at full size, by local circuit currents, in all directions.
  • Absolute refractory period: no new action potential is possible because the Na⁺ channels are inactivated; in a large myelinated fibre it lasts about 1/2500 s, so the maximum rate is about 2500 impulses per second.
  • The Na⁺-K⁺ pump restores the gradients using ATP; a fibre can fire 100,000 to 50 million impulses before the gradients run down.

Myelin and saltatory conduction

  • Myelin (from Schwann cells in the PNS) gives high electrical resistance, reducing current leak; it cuts ion flow about 5000-fold.
  • Myelin is interrupted at the nodes of Ranvier (every 1–3 mm), where Na⁺ channels cluster.
  • The action potential jumps from node to node with little loss: saltatory conduction. This raises speed 5 to 50-fold and saves energy, because only the nodes depolarize.
  • Conduction velocity ranges from about 0.25 m/s (small unmyelinated fibres) to 100 m/s (large myelinated fibres).
In the clinic

In demyelinating disease the current leaks away between nodes, so conduction slows or fails: this is why loss of myelin causes weakness and numbness.

Calcium and local anaesthetics

  • Ca²⁺ is about 10,000 times more concentrated outside the cell. It binds the outside of the Na⁺ channel and makes it harder to open: high Ca²⁺ stabilises the membrane.
  • Low extracellular Ca²⁺ lets Na⁺ channels open with a smaller depolarization: nerves fire spontaneously and muscles go into tetany (dangerous if the breathing muscles are involved).
  • Voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels are "slow channels", important in cardiac and smooth muscle.
  • Local anaesthetics (procaine, tetracaine, lidocaine) make the Na⁺ channel activation gates harder to open, so impulses fail to pass the anaesthetised segment.
Watch out

Slide 13 says local anaesthetics "inhibit the Na⁺-K⁺ ATPase". That is wrong: they block voltage-gated Na⁺ channels (Guyton p. 77). Stopping the pump would only run the gradients down slowly over thousands of impulses.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • The resting potential is closest to EK because K⁺ is the most permeable ion at rest.
  • The pump contributes only a few mV directly; its main job is keeping the gradients.
  • Low calcium makes nerves more, not less, excitable.
  • The absolute refractory period is due to Na⁺ channel inactivation, not to K⁺ channels.
  • Local anaesthetics act on Na⁺ channels.

Key terms

Electrochemical gradient: chemical + electrical forces on an ion · Equilibrium (reversal) potential: no net flow of an ion · Nernst equation: calculates it · Goldman equation: potential from several ions · Electrogenic pump: moves net charge · Leak channel: always open · Voltage-gated channel: opened by voltage · Ligand-gated channel: opened by a chemical · Threshold: voltage that triggers an action potential · Overshoot: inside becomes positive · Inactivation gate: closes the Na⁺ channel · Refractory period: time when a new impulse cannot start · All-or-nothing: full size or none · Node of Ranvier: gap in myelin · Saltatory conduction: jumping from node to node · Tetany: sustained spasm from hyperexcitability.
