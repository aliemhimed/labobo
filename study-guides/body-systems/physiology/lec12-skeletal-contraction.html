Contraction of skeletal muscle

The sarcomere and its filaments; myosin, actin, troponin and tropomyosin; the cross-bridge cycle that slides the filaments; length-tension and load-velocity; isotonic and isometric contraction; fast and slow fibres; motor units, summation and tetanus; fatigue, levers, remodelling and rigor mortis.

Labobo topic: Lec 12: Contraction of Skeletal Muscle

In 60 seconds

  • The sarcomere (Z disc to Z disc) is the functional unit: thick myosin, thin actin, held by titin.
  • At rest tropomyosin covers actin's active sites. Ca²⁺ binds troponin C, moving tropomyosin aside.
  • Cross-bridge cycle: ATP cocks the myosin head → head binds actin → power stroke → new ATP detaches the head. The myosin head is the ATPase.
  • Force is greatest at sarcomere lengths of about 2.0–2.2 µm (optimal overlap); velocity falls as load rises.
  • Isotonic: muscle shortens and moves the load. Isometric: tension rises, no shortening.
  • Type I (slow, red, aerobic, fatigue-resistant: marathon) vs type II (fast, white, glycolytic, powerful, fatigable: sprint).
  • Force grows by recruiting more motor units (small first) and by higher frequency up to tetanus. Rigor mortis: no ATP, so cross-bridges cannot detach.

Structure of skeletal muscle

  • Muscle → fascicles (perimysium) → fibres (endomysium) → myofibrils → filaments. About 40% of the body is skeletal muscle. A fibre is 10–80 µm wide, usually runs the whole muscle length, and has one nerve ending near its middle.
  • Each myofibril has about 1500 myosin and 3000 actin filaments.
  • I band: actin only (light). A band: myosin plus overlapping actin (dark). Z disc: anchors actin. H zone and M line lie in the middle of the A band.
  • The sarcomere, between two Z discs, is the functional unit.
  • Titin, a huge springy protein (MW about 3 million), runs from the Z disc to the M line and holds actin and myosin in place.
  • T tubules are invaginations of the sarcolemma, flanked by the sarcoplasmic reticulum, the Ca²⁺ reservoir (lecture 11).

From impulse to cross-bridge cycle

  1. The action potential reaches the muscle and depolarizes it; it runs down the T tubules.
  2. The SR releases Ca²⁺ into the sarcoplasm.
  3. Ca²⁺ binds troponin C; tropomyosin moves and uncovers actin's active sites.
  4. The myosin head, already cocked by splitting ATP (ADP + Pi still bound), attaches to actin.
  5. Power stroke: the head tilts and pulls actin toward the centre of the sarcomere; ADP and Pi are released.
  6. A new ATP binds and the head detaches; splitting it re-cocks the head for the next cycle.
  7. The cycle repeats ("walk-along" theory) until Ca²⁺ is pumped back into the SR or the load is too great.

The filaments do not shorten: actin slides over myosin, the Z discs come closer, and the I band and H zone narrow while the A band stays the same. The more work a muscle does, the more ATP it splits (Fenn effect).

Watch out

Slide 8 says "actin binds to ATP and degrades it". It is the myosin head that binds and splits ATP (slide 5 itself gives the head its ATPase activity; Guyton p. 84). Actin only provides the binding sites.

Length, load and work

  • Length-tension: force depends on how many cross-bridges can reach actin. It is greatest at sarcomere lengths of about 2.0–2.2 µm (optimal resting length). Stretched further, overlap falls (zero force when actin and myosin no longer overlap); shortened below about 1.65 µm, the filaments collide and force falls.
  • In a whole muscle, passive tension rises with stretch because of the connective tissue; active tension is largest near the normal resting length.
  • Load-velocity: an unloaded muscle contracts fastest (about 0.1 s); velocity falls as the load rises and is zero when the load equals the maximum force.
  • Work W = L × D: load × distance moved against the load.

Motor units and summation

  • A motor unit is one motor neuron and all the fibres it supplies: as few as 2–3 in some laryngeal muscles, several hundred in the soleus, 80–100 on average.
  • Multiple fibre summation: more motor units are recruited. By the size principle, small units fire first and large units (up to 50× stronger) later; units fire asynchronously, so contraction is smooth.
  • Frequency summation: faster firing fuses twitches until they merge into tetanus, because Ca²⁺ stays high between impulses. Maximum tetanic force is about 3–4 kg/cm².
  • Treppe (staircase): after rest, the first contractions may be only half as strong as those 10–50 twitches later, as cytosolic Ca²⁺ builds up.
  • Muscle tone: a low rate of impulses from the spinal cord keeps resting muscles slightly tense.

Fatigue, levers and remodelling

  • Fatigue: prolonged strong contraction outruns the metabolic and contractile processes. It rises almost in direct proportion to glycogen depletion; transmission at the NMJ also falls slightly. With no blood flow, fatigue is almost complete in 1–2 minutes.
  • Levers: biceps can exert about 300 lb, but inserting about 2 inches from the elbow on a 14-inch lever, the hand lifts about 1/7 of that.
  • Hypertrophy: more actin and myosin per fibre (up to about 50% more) after 6–10 weeks of a few strong contractions a day. Atrophy uses the ATP-dependent ubiquitin-proteasome pathway. Stretch adds sarcomeres at the fibre ends; hyperplasia is rare.
  • Denervation: rapid atrophy; recovery is possible if the nerve regrows within months, but after 1–2 years fibres are replaced by fibrous and fatty tissue.

Rigor mortis and dystrophy

In the clinic

Rigor mortis: several hours after death all muscles go into contracture. Without ATP, myosin heads stay bound to actin, because ATP is needed to detach them. Rigor lasts until the proteins break down, about 15–25 hours later.

In the clinic

Muscular dystrophy: lack or mutation of dystrophin destabilises the membrane, lets in more Ca²⁺ and leads to protein breakdown and progressive weakness.

Watch out

Slide 15 says repeated signals "diminish ACh" as the cause of fatigue. Guyton gives the main cause as failure of contractile and metabolic processes (glycogen depletion); reduced NMJ transmission adds only a little.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • The myosin head, not actin, is the ATPase.
  • Ca²⁺ binds troponin C, not tropomyosin.
  • ATP is needed for detachment; no ATP means rigor.
  • Filaments do not shorten; the sarcomere does.
  • Small motor units are recruited first.

Key terms

Myofibril: chain of sarcomeres · Sarcomere: Z disc to Z disc · Titin: elastic scaffold protein · F-actin / G-actin: filament / single unit · Tropomyosin: covers actin sites · Troponin I, C, T: actin, calcium, tropomyosin binders · Cross-bridge: myosin arm and head · Power stroke: head tilt that pulls actin · Sliding filament: actin slides over myosin · Isotonic / isometric: shortening / no shortening · Motor unit: one neuron and its fibres · Size principle: small units first · Tetanus: fused contraction · Treppe: staircase effect · Myoglobin: O₂ store in red fibres · Rigor mortis: stiffness after death · Dystrophin: membrane-stabilising protein.
