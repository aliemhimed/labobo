Cardiac muscle

The three kinds of cardiac muscle; the latticework of fibres joined by intercalated discs into atrial and ventricular syncytia; the left ventricular twist; the ventricular action potential and its plateau, phase by phase; conduction velocity and the long refractory period; excitation-contraction coupling with calcium-induced calcium release; and relaxation. Written from Guyton & Hall chapter 9 (no deck for this week).

Labobo topic: Lec 14: Cardiac Muscle

In 60 seconds

  • Three types: atrial, ventricular and specialised excitatory and conductive fibres (weak contraction, automatic rhythm).
  • Striated like skeletal muscle, but cells are joined by intercalated discs with gap junctions: two functional syncytia, atrial and ventricular, linked only by the A-V bundle.
  • Ventricular action potential: about 105 mV (−85 to +20 mV) with a plateau of about 0.2 s, so contraction lasts up to 15× longer than in skeletal muscle.
  • The plateau comes from L-type (slow) Ca²⁺ channels plus a fivefold fall in K⁺ permeability.
  • Refractory period 0.25–0.30 s (ventricle), as long as the plateau, so the heart cannot be tetanized.
  • Ca²⁺ entering through L-type channels triggers ryanodine receptors to release more from the SR (calcium-induced calcium release); strength depends on extracellular Ca²⁺.
  • Relaxation: SERCA2 into the SR and the Na⁺-Ca²⁺ exchanger out of the cell.

The heart and its muscle

  • The heart is two pumps in series: the right heart to the lungs, the left heart to the body. In each, the atrium is a weak primer pump and the ventricle supplies the main force. The pericardium encloses it.
  • Three types of cardiac muscle: atrial and ventricular muscle contract like skeletal muscle but much longer; specialised excitatory and conductive fibres contract feebly but discharge rhythmically and conduct impulses.
  • Fibres form a latticework that divides and rejoins. They are striated, with actin and myosin sliding as in skeletal muscle.
  • Left ventricular twist: the outer (subepicardial) layer spirals leftward and the inner (subendocardial) layer rightward. In systole the apex turns clockwise and the base counterclockwise, wringing the ventricle; in diastole it untwists like a spring, helping rapid filling.

Intercalated discs and syncytia

  • Intercalated discs are the cell membranes between cardiac cells. There the membranes fuse into gap junctions, so ions and action potentials pass freely from cell to cell: excite one cell and the impulse spreads to all.
  • The heart has two syncytia: atrial (the walls of both atria) and ventricular (the walls of both ventricles).
  • They are separated by fibrous tissue round the A-V valves, which does not conduct; impulses cross only through the A-V bundle. This lets the atria contract shortly before the ventricles.

The ventricular action potential

It averages about 105 mV: from about −85 mV to about +20 mV. After the spike the membrane stays depolarized for about 0.2 s (the plateau), then repolarizes abruptly. Because of the plateau, ventricular contraction lasts up to 15 times longer than skeletal contraction.

Cause of the plateauHow
L-type (slow) Ca²⁺ channelsAlso called calcium-sodium channels; slower to open and stay open for several tenths of a second; Ca²⁺ and Na⁺ keep flowing in, and the Ca²⁺ also starts contraction
Fall in K⁺ permeabilityJust after the spike, K⁺ permeability drops about fivefold (not in skeletal muscle), so less K⁺ leaves to restore rest

Skeletal muscle uses almost only fast Na⁺ channels, open for a few thousandths of a second, so it has no plateau. When the slow channels close after 0.2–0.3 s, K⁺ permeability rises sharply and the membrane repolarizes.

Conduction and refractory periods

  • Conduction in atrial and ventricular muscle is about 0.3–0.5 m/s (about 1/250 of large nerve fibres, 1/10 of skeletal muscle); in Purkinje fibres up to 4 m/s.
  • Refractory period: the interval when a normal impulse cannot re-excite the muscle. Ventricle 0.25–0.30 s, about the length of the plateau; then a relative refractory period of about 0.05 s, when only a strong impulse can excite it (an early premature contraction). Atria about 0.15 s.
  • Because the refractory period lasts almost as long as contraction, twitches cannot fuse: the heart cannot be tetanized, and it can relax and fill between beats.

Excitation-contraction coupling

  1. The action potential spreads over the membrane and down the T tubules.
  2. L-type Ca²⁺ channels in the T tubules let extracellular Ca²⁺ in.
  3. This Ca²⁺ opens ryanodine receptor channels in the SR, releasing much more Ca²⁺ ("calcium sparks"): calcium-induced calcium release.
  4. Ca²⁺ binds troponin and the filaments slide, as in skeletal muscle.
  5. At the end of the plateau Ca²⁺ entry stops. SERCA2 pumps Ca²⁺ back into the SR, and the Na⁺-Ca²⁺ exchanger moves it out of the cell (the Na⁺ is then removed by the Na⁺-K⁺ pump). The muscle relaxes.
  • The cardiac SR is less developed and stores too little Ca²⁺ on its own, so the T-tubule Ca²⁺ is essential.
  • Cardiac T tubules are about 5 times wider (25 times the volume) than skeletal ones and hold mucopolysaccharides that bind Ca²⁺.
  • Strength therefore depends heavily on extracellular Ca²⁺: a heart in Ca²⁺-free solution soon stops; skeletal muscle hardly changes.
  • Contraction lasts about as long as the action potential: about 0.2 s in the atria and 0.3 s in the ventricles.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • The plateau is due to Ca²⁺ in and less K⁺ out, not to Na⁺.
  • Phase 0 is Na⁺; phase 2 is Ca²⁺; phase 3 is K⁺.
  • The atria and ventricles are separate syncytia; the fibrous skeleton does not conduct.
  • Heart muscle cannot be tetanized because its refractory period is long.
  • Low extracellular Ca²⁺ weakens the heart, but barely affects skeletal muscle force.

Key terms

Syncytium: cells acting as one · Intercalated disc: junction between cardiac cells · Gap junction: channel linking cytoplasm · A-V bundle: only electrical path from atria to ventricles · Purkinje fibres: fast conducting fibres · Plateau: prolonged depolarization · L-type Ca²⁺ channel: slow calcium-sodium channel · Refractory period: time when the muscle cannot be re-excited · Premature contraction: early beat in the relative refractory period · Calcium-induced calcium release: trigger Ca²⁺ opens RyR · SERCA2: cardiac SR Ca²⁺ pump · Na⁺-Ca²⁺ exchanger: removes Ca²⁺ from the cell · Ventricular twist: wringing motion of the left ventricle.
