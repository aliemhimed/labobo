Thoracic muscles and the diaphragm

Intrinsic muscles of the chest wall (the three intercostal layers, transversus thoracis, subcostals), the diaphragm with its attachments, openings and nerves, how breathing works by Boyle's law, and the extrinsic muscles: pectoralis major and minor, serratus anterior and subclavius.

Labobo topic: Lec 10: Thoracic Muscles & Diaphragm

In 60 seconds

  • Intrinsic muscles start and end on the thoracic cage and move it for breathing; extrinsic muscles move the pectoral girdle and arm.
  • External intercostals (11 pairs, "hands in pockets") elevate the ribs; internal and innermost intercostals depress them. The neurovascular bundle runs between internal and innermost.
  • Diaphragm: right crus L1–L3, left crus L1–L2; central tendon; right dome higher (liver).
  • Openings: IVC at T8, oesophagus at T10 (with vagal trunks), aorta at T12 (with azygos vein and thoracic duct).
  • Motor nerve: phrenic, C3–C5. Sensory: centre phrenic; periphery 6th–11th intercostal nerves.
  • Inspiration is active (diaphragm + external intercostals); quiet expiration is passive (elastic recoil).
  • Serratus anterior (long thoracic nerve) lets the arm rise above 90°.

Learning objectives

Syllabus week XII: thoracic muscles, their functional groups, the diaphragm. By the end you can:

  • sort the thoracic muscles into intrinsic and extrinsic groups;
  • give the attachments, fibre direction, action and nerve of each intrinsic muscle;
  • describe the diaphragm's attachments, openings and innervation;

Two groups of thoracic muscles

The muscles of the thoracic wall attach to the bony cage, keep the wall stable and take part in breathing.

Transversus thoracis and subcostals

  • Transversus thoracis: from the posterior surface of the lower sternum to the inner surface of costal cartilages 2–6; continuous below with transversus abdominis. Weakly depresses the ribs. Intercostal nerves T2–T6.
  • Subcostals: thin slips in the lower posterior wall, from the inner surface of a lower rib near its angle to the upper border of the 2nd or 3rd rib below; fibres parallel the innermost intercostals. Same action as the internal intercostals. Intercostal nerves.

The diaphragm

  • Crura (tendinous vertebral parts): right crus from L1–L3 and their discs, left crus from L1–L2. Some right crus fibres loop round the oesophageal opening as a physiological sphincter against gastric reflux.
  • Muscle fibres converge on the central tendon. There are two domes; the right is slightly higher because of the liver.
  • Peripheral attachments: lumbar vertebrae and arcuate ligaments, costal cartilages of the lower ribs, and the xiphoid process. The costal margin is incomplete behind, so the diaphragm anchors to the arch-shaped arcuate ligaments.
Watch out

Slide 13 says the costal part arises from the cartilages of ribs 7–10, attaching directly to ribs 11–12. Standard texts say the inner surfaces of the lower six costal cartilages (7–12). Both describe the same attachment to the lower ribs; if an option says "lower six ribs", it is correct.

Mnemonic

"I 8 10 Eggs AT 12": IVC at T8, oesophagus (10 Eggs) at T10, Aorta and Thoracic duct at T12. "C3, 4, 5 keeps the diaphragm alive".

  • Motor: the phrenic nerves (C3–C5), which pierce the diaphragm and supply it from its abdominal surface.
  • Sensory (pain, proprioception): central tendon by the phrenic nerves; peripheral muscle by the 6th–11th intercostal nerves.

How breathing works

Boyle's law: at constant temperature, the volume of a gas is inversely proportional to its pressure. The lungs stick to the chest wall through the pleural seal, so when the thorax enlarges the lungs enlarge and their pressure falls.

Inspiration (active)Quiet expiration (passive)
DiaphragmContracts and flattens: height of thorax ↑Relaxes to its dome: height ↓
External intercostalsContract, raising ribs and sternum: front-to-back depth ↑Relax; ribs and sternum fall
Lung pressureFalls below the outside air → air flows inElastic recoil raises it above the outside → air flows out

Forced expiration (exercise, or high airway resistance such as asthma) uses expiratory muscles: the abdominal muscles squeeze the abdomen and push the diaphragm up, and the internal intercostals pull the ribs down and in.

Pectoralis major is the most superficial muscle of the region; with pectoralis minor it forms the anterior wall of the axilla. Serratus anterior forms its medial wall.

In the clinic

Injury to the long thoracic nerve paralyses serratus anterior: the medial border of the scapula lifts off the ribs (winged scapula) and the arm cannot be raised above the horizontal.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • The vagal trunks pass with the oesophagus (T10), not with the aorta.
  • The thoracic duct and azygos vein pass with the aorta (T12).
  • The phrenic nerve is motor to the whole diaphragm; the periphery's sensation is intercostal.
  • Quiet expiration needs no muscle contraction.
  • Pectoralis major inserts in the intertubercular sulcus, not on the coracoid.

Key terms

Intrinsic thoracic muscles: both ends on the cage · Extrinsic thoracic muscles: anterior axioappendicular · Costal groove: groove on the lower inner rib · Interosseous / interchondral part: between bony ribs / costal cartilages · Crus: vertebral leg of the diaphragm · Central tendon: aponeurotic centre of the diaphragm · Arcuate ligaments: arches anchoring the diaphragm behind · Hiatus: opening · Phrenic nerve: C3–C5 · Boyle's law: pressure × volume constant · Elastic recoil: lungs spring back · Intertubercular sulcus: groove on the humerus · Coracoid process: hook of the scapula · Long thoracic nerve: nerve of serratus anterior · Winged scapula: sign of serratus anterior paralysis.
