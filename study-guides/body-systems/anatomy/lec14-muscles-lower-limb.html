Muscles of the pelvis and lower limb

The gluteal muscles and the Trendelenburg sign; the deep lateral rotators; the pelvic floor; the femoral triangle and the three thigh compartments with their nerves; the anterior, lateral and posterior compartments of the leg; and the dorsum and four plantar layers of the foot.

Labobo topic: Lec 14: Muscles of the Pelvis & Lower Limb

In 60 seconds

  • Gluteus maximus extends the hip (inferior gluteal nerve); medius, minimus and TFL abduct and medially rotate (superior gluteal nerve) and keep the pelvis level when walking.
  • Weak medius/minimus → Trendelenburg sign: standing on that leg, the pelvis drops on the opposite side.
  • Deep lateral rotators: piriformis (through the greater sciatic foramen), gemelli, obturator internus (through the lesser sciatic foramen), quadratus femoris.
  • Pelvic diaphragm = levator ani (puborectalis, pubococcygeus, iliococcygeus) + coccygeus. Puborectalis keeps the anorectal angle for continence.
  • Thigh: anterior = femoral nerve (quadriceps, sartorius); medial = obturator (adductors); posterior = sciatic (hamstrings).
  • Leg: anterior = deep fibular (dorsiflexors); lateral = superficial fibular (evertors); posterior = tibial (plantarflexors).
  • Sole: 10 intrinsic muscles in 4 layers, supplied by the medial and lateral plantar nerves.

Learning objectives

Syllabus weeks XVI–XVII: muscles of the lower limb; internal and external pelvic muscles; gluteal muscles; femoral triangle and muscles of the thigh; muscles of the leg and foot. By the end you can:

  • give the attachments, actions and nerves of the gluteal and deep hip muscles;
  • describe the muscles and functions of the pelvic floor;
  • describe the femoral triangle and the three compartments of the thigh;
  • describe the three compartments of the leg and the muscles of the foot;
  • predict the deficit from a lesion of each nerve of the lower limb.

Gluteal region: superficial muscles

Lower limb muscles form four groups: hip, thigh, leg, foot. The gluteal region lies behind the pelvis at the top of the femur; its muscles move the limb at the hip. The superficial group (three glutei + TFL) abducts and extends; the deep group laterally rotates.

In the clinic

Trendelenburg sign: the patient stands on one leg and the pelvis drops toward the unsupported (opposite) side. It means weakness of gluteus medius and minimus on the standing side, from a lesion of that side's superior gluteal nerve.

Small muscles under gluteus minimus. They laterally rotate the thigh and stabilise the hip by pulling the femoral head into the acetabulum. From superior to inferior:

Piriformis is the key landmark of the gluteal region; the sciatic nerve passes below it. Piriformis, obturator internus and the gemelli also abduct. Obturator internus with the two gemelli is sometimes called the triceps coxae.

Pelvic floor

The pelvic viscera (bladder, rectum, genital organs, end of the urethra) lie in the true pelvis. Its lateral walls are obturator internus and piriformis; piriformis also forms the posterior wall. The floor is the pelvic diaphragm: levator ani + coccygeus and their fascia.

Functions: supports the pelvic and abdominal viscera; resists rises in abdominal pressure (coughing, lifting); continence, acting as a sphincter on the rectum and urethra and relaxing for urination and defecation.

Iliopsoas and the femoral triangle

  • Psoas major (T12–L5 bodies, discs and transverse processes; anterior rami L1–L3) and iliacus (iliac fossa; femoral nerve) both insert on the lesser trochanter and flex the hip: together, iliopsoas.
  • The femoral triangle (no slide; standard anatomy): base the inguinal ligament, lateral border sartorius, medial border adductor longus (the slide confirms this one). Floor: iliopsoas and pectineus. Roof: fascia lata.
  • Contents, lateral to medial: femoral nerve, artery, vein, then the femoral canal with lymphatics (NAVeL). Its apex continues as the adductor canal.

Muscles of the foot

Extrinsic muscles come from the leg and invert, evert, plantarflex and dorsiflex. Intrinsic muscles lie in the foot and move individual toes and support the arches.

  • Dorsum: extensor digitorum brevis (calcaneus → long extensor tendons of toes II–IV) and extensor hallucis brevis (→ proximal phalanx of the great toe). Both help extend the toes; deep fibular nerve.
Sole layerMusclesNerve
1 (under the plantar fascia)Abductor hallucis; flexor digitorum brevis (flexes PIP of toes II–V); abductor digiti minimiMedial plantar for the first two; lateral plantar for ADM
2Quadratus plantae (helps FDL); 4 lumbricals (flex MTP, extend IP); FDL tendons pass hereLateral plantar; 1st lumbrical medial plantar

The medial plantar nerve supplies only four: abductor hallucis, flexor digitorum brevis, flexor hallucis brevis and the first lumbrical. Everything else in the sole is lateral plantar. In the foot the axis for abduction is the second toe.

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • In Trendelenburg sign the pelvis drops on the healthy (swing) side; the lesion is on the standing side.
  • Piriformis exits by the greater sciatic foramen; obturator internus by the lesser.
  • Rectus femoris is the only quadriceps that flexes the hip.
  • Biceps femoris short head is common fibular; everything else in the hamstrings is tibial.
  • Soleus does not cross the knee; gastrocnemius does.
  • Fibularis longus inserts on the medial side of the foot (medial cuneiform, MT I).

Key terms

Iliotibial tract: thickened fascia lata on the lateral thigh · Trendelenburg sign: pelvic drop from weak hip abductors · Greater / lesser sciatic foramen: exits from the pelvis · Triceps coxae: obturator internus + gemelli · Pelvic diaphragm: levator ani + coccygeus · Anorectal angle: bend kept by puborectalis · Iliopsoas: psoas major + iliacus · Femoral triangle: inguinal ligament, sartorius, adductor longus · Quadriceps femoris: rectus femoris + 3 vasti · Linea aspera: ridge on the back of the femur · Hamstrings: posterior thigh muscles · Calcaneal tendon: Achilles tendon · Inversion / eversion: sole inward / outward · Plantar nerves: medial and lateral, to the sole · Foot drop: loss of dorsiflexion.
