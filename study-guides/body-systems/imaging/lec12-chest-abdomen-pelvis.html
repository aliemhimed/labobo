Imaging the muscles of the chest, abdomen and pelvis

The chest wall muscles (pectorals, serratus anterior, the three intercostal layers) on CT and MRI; the diaphragm on X-ray, CT and ultrasound; the abdominal wall layers on CT, including an incisional hernia; the pelvic floor; subcutaneous emphysema; and when to choose MRI or ultrasound for muscle.

Labobo topic: Lec 12: Chest, Abdominal & Pelvic Muscles

In 60 seconds

  • Chest wall, from outside in: pectoralis major → pectoralis minor; laterally serratus anterior (ribs 1 to 8 or 9); between the ribs external, internal and innermost intercostals.
  • Pectoralis major has two heads, clavicular and sternocostal. Pectoralis minor pulls the scapula down and forward against the chest wall; with serratus anterior it protracts the scapula.
  • The diaphragm is the main muscle of inspiration: it contracts and flattens, pulling air in. It is seen on chest X-ray, CT and on ultrasound through the liver.
  • Abdominal wall on axial CT: rectus abdominis either side of the linea alba; laterally external oblique → internal oblique → transversus abdominis, outside to inside.
  • Pelvic floor: levator ani + coccygeus = pelvic diaphragm. Puborectalis slings the anorectal junction (angle about 80°).
  • Subcutaneous emphysema: gas in the soft tissues of the body wall or limbs, tracking along fascial planes; seen after a chest tube for pneumothorax.
  • MRI for detailed muscle, tendon and ligament injury; ultrasound for quick, dynamic, high-resolution tendon and muscle checks (Grainger & Allison).

Learning objectives

Syllabus week XIV: imaging the chest muscles; when to use MR imaging and when to use ultrasound; imaging of the muscles of the chest, abdomen and small pelvis. By the end you can:

  • name the chest wall muscles and find them on axial CT and MRI;
  • describe the diaphragm's action and the methods that show it;
  • identify the abdominal wall layers on CT and recognise an incisional hernia;
  • name the muscles of the pelvic floor;
  • define subcutaneous emphysema and say when MRI or ultrasound suits a muscle problem.

Chest wall muscles

The muscles of the anterior thorax move the arm and shoulder, help change thoracic volume in breathing and reinforce the thoracic wall. On the anterior chest the cephalic vein runs in the deltopectoral groove between deltoid and pectoralis major, and subclavius lies under the clavicle.

  • Intercostal muscles fill the spaces between the ribs in three layers: external, internal and innermost.
  • Serratus anterior arises from the superolateral surfaces of ribs 1 to 8 or 9 on the lateral thoracic wall.
  • On axial CT, read the wall from outside in: pectoralis major, then the intercostal layers between the ribs; serratus anterior wraps the lateral wall.

The pectoral muscles mostly act on the scapula. On axial MRI at the level of the shoulder you can trace pectoralis major (costosternal head) and pectoralis minor in front, serratus anterior laterally, and the back muscles (trapezius, rhomboid major, erector spinae) behind.

The diaphragm

A thin skeletal muscle at the base of the chest that separates the abdomen from the chest. It is the main muscle of inspiration.

  1. Inhalation: the diaphragm contracts and flattens, creating a vacuum effect that pulls air into the lungs.
  2. Exhalation: it relaxes and air is pushed out.
  • Chest X-ray (PA and lateral) shows the domes of the diaphragm as the lower borders of the lungs.
  • CT: the diaphragm is a thin band; on sagittal images it arches over the liver, and on axial images through the liver its anterior part is seen.
  • Ultrasound: the right hemidiaphragm is seen through the liver, which acts as an acoustic window.
In the clinic

Diaphragmatic hernia (Grainger & Allison p. 16): abdominal contents move into the chest through a defect. Bochdalek hernias are the commonest (70%), posterolateral and mostly left-sided; Morgagni hernias are anterior, at the right cardiophrenic angle. In eventration the diaphragm is thin but continuous, so it is not a hernia.

In the clinic

Traumatic rupture (p. 110) follows 0.8 to 5% of blunt thoraco-abdominal injuries, usually at the weak posterolateral part. About 70% are missed at first and the chest X-ray is normal in 25%. A left tear may show bowel or a coiled nasogastric tube in the left chest.

Abdominal wall

MusclePositionAction or note
Rectus abdominis (R)Top layer, front; two flat parallel muscles separated by the linea alba (the "six-pack")Flexes the spine, tenses the anterior wall, compresses the abdominal contents
External oblique (EO)Outermost lateral muscle, from the lower half of the ribs around and down to the pelvis; just under fat and skinCovers the sides of the abdomen
Internal oblique (IO)Under the external oblique on each sideMiddle lateral layer
Transversus abdominis (TA)Immediately inside the internal obliqueDeepest lateral layer

On axial CT the three lateral layers stack EO → IO → TA from outside in, and they end medially beside the rectus.

In the clinic

CT after laparoscopic surgery: a hernia at a prior hand-assist port site containing small bowel, with no obstruction or strangulation. Naming the wall layers shows exactly where the defect is.

Pelvic floor

  • Coccygeus (ischiococcygeus): a triangular sheet posterior to levator ani.
  • Coccygeus + levator ani = the pelvic diaphragm.
  • Levator ani parts on the slide: puborectalis, pubococcygeus, iliococcygeus. Openings: urethral hiatus, vaginal canal, rectal hiatus.
  • The puborectalis forms a sling round the anorectal junction, holding the anorectal angle at about 80°.

Subcutaneous emphysema

Gas in the subcutaneous tissues. The term is used for any soft-tissue emphysema of the body wall or limbs, because the gas often dissects into deeper soft tissue and muscle along fascial planes. On radiographs it shows as dark streaks of gas outlining muscle bundles.

In the clinic

Case: a 65-year-old woman with dyspnoea from a malignant pleural effusion. After drainage the chest X-ray showed a pneumothorax, so a chest tube was placed. CT on lung windows then showed extensive subcutaneous emphysema with the pneumothorax and the tube.

MRI or ultrasound?

The deck does not cover this syllabus line, so it comes from Grainger & Allison (p. 496).

MRIUltrasound
Best forLigaments, tendons, cartilage and muscle in detail, including deep structuresTendon, ligament and muscle injuries with high-resolution probes; quick and dynamic
Normal tendonNo signal (black) on routine sequencesHyperechoic parallel lines along its length
InjurySprain or tear raises water content → high T2 signal; fat suppression makes it stand outTendinitis: thicker, altered echogenicity. Tear: hypoechoic gap, often fluid in the sheath

Exam traps

Exam traps
  • The diaphragm flattens (descends) when it contracts; it rises when it relaxes.
  • The innermost intercostal is the deepest layer, not the internal.
  • Transversus abdominis is inside the internal oblique, the deepest lateral muscle.
  • Coccygeus lies behind levator ani, not in front of it.
  • Subcutaneous emphysema is gas, not fluid, in the soft tissues.
Watch out

Slide 11 writes "transverse abdominis". The standard name is transversus abdominis; both mean the same muscle.

Key terms

Deltopectoral groove: gap between deltoid and pectoralis major holding the cephalic vein · Intercostals: three muscle layers between the ribs · Serratus anterior: lateral chest muscle from ribs 1 to 8 or 9 · Protraction: moving the scapula forward · Diaphragm: main muscle of inspiration · Hemidiaphragm: one side of the diaphragm · Linea alba: midline band between the recti · Rectus abdominis: the "six-pack" · Transversus abdominis: deepest lateral abdominal muscle · Incisional (port-site) hernia: bowel through a surgical wall defect · Pelvic diaphragm: levator ani + coccygeus · Puborectalis: sling at the anorectal junction · Subcutaneous emphysema: gas in soft tissues · Pneumothorax: air in the pleural space · Eventration: thin but intact diaphragm · Hypoechoic: darker on ultrasound · Hyperechoic: brighter on ultrasound.
